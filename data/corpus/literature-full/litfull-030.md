# A comprehensive review of warm-mix asphalt mixtures: mix design, construction temperatures determination, performance and life-cycle assessment

Ning Liu, Liping Liu, Mingchen Li and Lijun Sun

The Key Laboratory of Road and Traffic Engineering, Ministry of Education, Tongji University, Shanghai, People’s Republic of China

## ABSTRACT

The paper provides a review of mix design, construction temperature determination, performance and life-cycle assessment (LCA) of WMA mixtures. The mix design of WMA mixtures using almost the same design protocols as the HMA mixtures. The techniques used for determining WMA mixtures’ construction temperatures include the analogy, phase angle, compaction energy index (CEI) and traffic densification index (TDI), and 4% air void methods. The use of Sasobit is advisable for the road prone to rutting. For the road prone to water damage, the use of foaming technologyis not suitable and Rediset is recommended. For the road prone to fatigue failure, the use of Water-based processes is not recommended, while Evotherm and Rediset are advisable. From an environmental point of view, waterbased processes are recommended for the production of WMA mixtures, followed by organic additives and chemical additives, whereas the use of water-bearing additives is not recommended.

## 1. Introduction

With the global economy prosperously developing, the total energy consumption and greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions have been increasing, which has aroused the common concern of all countries for energy shortage and environmental pollution. To cope with this global problem, most countries have signed the Kyoto Protocol and Paris Agreement, which put forward the requirements of energy saving and emission reduction for the development of each country (Caputo et al., 2020; Rogelj et al., 2016; Schleussner et al., 2016). The transportation industry has always been a major energy consumer and carbon emitter; in response to the call for energy saving and emission reduction, it is imperative to promote the low-carbon development of transportation infrastructure construction (Vaitkus et al., 2009; Wang et al., 2018b).

Asphalt pavement is widely used in highway construction for simple construction, convenient material extraction and good performance (Chang et al., 2020; Zapata & Gambatese, 2005). The asphalt mixtures used in asphalt pavement construction and maintenance can be categorised into hot-mix asphalt (HMA) mixtures, half-WMA mixtures, WMA mixtures and cold-mix asphalt (CMA) mixtures according to the production temperature (Olard, Le Noan, & Romier, 2007; Olard, Le Noan, Beduneau, & Romier, 2008; Vaitkus et al., 2009). The production temperature range of HMA mixtures is 150 ∼ 180°C; it needs to consume much fuel and emit a lot of harmful gases during the production process, which is not environment-friendly. The CMA mixtures are produced at an ambient temperature. It has the advantages of low carbon and energy saving, convenient construction. Still, the low bonding strength and flexibility make it often used for pavement repair and low-traffic pavement. Interestingly, the WMA mixtures have a production temperature between HMA mixtures and CMA mixtures and performance up to (or close to) HMA mixtures (EAPA, 2010). It has a wide variety of benefits, including lowering the fuel usage and production costs; reducing asphalt fumes and GHG emissions; improving the working environment; extending the paving season and compaction time; a longer hauling distances; shortening the time of opening to traffic; increasing the usage of RAP in hot recycled mixtures (Gandhi et al., 2009; Ingrassia et al., 2021; Olsen et al., 2021; Ziari et al., 2021). In addition, several aspects need to be noticed for using WMA technology, additional expenditure caused by asphalt plant modification and additives purchase; moisture-induced damages or bonding/coating issues caused by lower temperature and the residual moisture in foamed asphalt or incomplete dried aggregate; less resistant to rutting caused by lower oxidative aging (Gaudefroy, Olard, Le Quernec, & De La Roche, 2008; Gaudefroy, Olard, Beduneau, & De La Roche, 2009); few long-term performance data are available for reference (Kristjansdottr et al., 2007; Prakash & Suman, 2022; Wu et al., 2019).

WMA technology has become increasingly mature after more than 20 years of development. It can be categorised into three main categories: organic additives, chemical additives and foaming technology (Capitao et al., 2012; Diab, Sangiorgi, Ghabchi, Zaman, & Wahaballa, 2016). Various WMA products have been developed to produce WMA mixtures; the more representative ones are Aspha-min, Advera, Double Barrel Green, Sasobit, Evotherm, Rediset, etc. (Cheraghian et al., 2020). Compared with the HMA mixtures, the production temperature of WMA mixtures is lower by about $30 \sim 40^{\circ}C$ . According to the World Bank reports, a $1 0 ^ { \circ } \mathsf { C }$ reduction in the production temperature of one ton of asphalt mixtures saves 1L of fuel and avoids 1 kg of $\mathsf { C O } _ { 2 }$ emission (Jamshidi et al., 2013; Sukhija & Saboo, 2021). The WMA technology can achieve an overall energy saving range of 20 ∼ 35% and significantly reduce greenhouse gas emissions (Hettiarachchi et al., 2019; Pouranian & Shishehbor, 2019). The advantages of construction, environment and economy give natural advantages to WMA mixtures in some special engineering projects, such as the long tunnels, airport pavements, high-traffic city roads, urban roads in the densely inhabited district, construction in low-temperature seasons and cold areas, as well as thin and ultra-thin overlay in road maintenance.

So far, numerous investigations of WMA mixtures have been performed from laboratory to field. Most studies adopted the mix design protocols of HMA mixtures to design WMA mixtures, disregarding the effect of warm-mix characteristics in details, such as asphalt grade, aggregate’s moisture content and asphalt content, etc. and determined the construction temperature of WMA mixtures based on the manufacturer’s recommendation or engineering experience result from no unified effective determination method has been formed yet. In addition, for the selection of specific warm-mix products lacks clear references, existing review publications mainly discuss the performance of WMA mixtures in terms of three categories, namely organic additives, chemical additives and foaming technology and lack the comprehensive assessment reflecting the cradle to grave performance of WMA mixtures. While various WMA products of the same WMA technology possess different advantages and disadvantages, which affect the performance of WMA mixtures. Therefore, there is a need to review WMA mixtures’ mix design, determination of construction temperatures, performance of mixtures with representative WMA products in different WMA technologies and LCA of WMA mixtures.

This paper mainly integrates and analyses the literature related to the design, construction temperatures, performance and LCA of WMA mixtures. The resulting summary and outlook on the WMA technology are represented in the following five sections: (1) the development and application of WMA technology, (2) the WMA technology introduction, (3) the mix design of WMA mixtures and determination of their construction temperatures, (4) the WMA mixtures’ performance with representative WMA products in different WMA technologies and (5) the LCA of WMA mixtures. The purpose of this paper is to provide reference and guidance for the application of WMA technology.

## 2. Development and application of WMA technology

## 2.1. Europe

In response to the requirements of environmental policies, a joint venture between Shell and Kolo Veidekke companies developed the use of soft asphalt and emulsified asphalt to produce WMA as early as 1995 and used the WMA as one of the solutions to reduce carbon emissions in the construction process. Compared with HMA mixtures, the performance of WMA mixtures produced has not deteriorated, but the production cost increased by 20% (Hasan et al., 2017; Koenders et al., 2020). In 1997, the signing of Kyoto Protocol vigorously promoted the development of WMA technology (D’angelo et al., 2008; Huang & Qin, 2008; Liu, Wu, Chen, & Hu, 2009; Prowell & Hurley, 2011). This year, Sasobit developed by Sasol-Wax Corp. and Aspha-min developed by Eurovia Services Corp. started to be successfully applied in the pavement construction in many European countries. In 1998, Shell and Kolo Veidekke used foamed asphalt and soft asphalt to produce WMA, reducing costs without sacrificing performance. Harrison and Christodulaki first introduced the concept of WMA at the 1st Sydney international conference on asphalt pavements in 2000. In the same year, Koenders et al. thoroughly discussed WMA at the 2nd Euroasphalt & Eurobitume international conference held in Barcelona. They analysed the WAM-Foam technology application in pilot (test) roads in the UK, Netherlands and Norway. After the large-scale advertising and scientific substantiation, WMA has attracted widespread attention in Europe, Australia and South Africa (Dinis-Almeida et al., 2012; Hurley & Prowell, 2006a; Hurley & Prowell, 2006b; Robjent & Dosh, 2009).

After the WMA technology was successfully applied in Germany and Japan, it became widely used in Europe, especially in France. The consumption of WMA mixtures in France started from 8,000 tons in 2001, raising twice annually in 2002 and 2003 (15,000 and 30,000 tons, respectively) and then exponentially grew year by year (Gai & Dong, 2010; Harder et al., 2008; Li et al., 2010; Qin, Huang, Su, & Sun, 2009; Wang, 2007; Xu, Yan, Ji, & Gao, 2005; Yi, Gao, & Hu, 2009; Zhang, Yan, Zhou, & He, 2007). Figure 1 shows the WMA production evolution in Europe and the USA from 2013 to 2020 (EAPA, 2021, 2022).

## 2.2. The USA

In 2002, the National Asphalt Pavement Association (NAPA) inspected the WMA technology’s application in Europe (mainly Germany and Norway) and invited representatives of relevant companies to make a WMA technology introduction at the NAPA annual meeting held in San Diego, in January 2003. The latter event triggered large-scale WMA implementation via the USA government departments and industry. In 2004, the NAPA, the Federal Highway Administration (FHWA) and some manufacturers jointly established a fund for the National Asphalt Technology Center (NCAT) to study the feasibility of WMA technology application in the USA. This year, the WMA technology was demonstrated on-site at the asphalt world conference held in Nashville, Tennessee. Subsequently, Aspha-min WMA test roads were constructed in Ohio and Florida. In June 2005, NCAT submitted two evaluation reports on using Sasobit and Aspha-min to prepare WMA mixtures (Hurley & Prowell, 2005a, 2005b). By March 2006, the first WMA technical working group meeting was held in Maryland; the meeting evaluated WMA, further promoting WMA technology throughout the USA (Qian, 2006). In June of the same year, NCAT issued an evaluation report on Evotherm for preparing WMA mixtures (Hurley & Prowell, 2006a). By 2007, Double Barrel Green technology was first applied in Chattanooga, Tennessee. At the same time, technical regulation on WMA technology has been continuously established and improved. In 2007, NAPA conducted an in-depth study on the technical specifications of WMA technology and published ‘Warm-mix Asphalt Mixtures-Best Practices’ and ‘Warm-mix Asphalt Mixtures-Experiences of Highway Contractors’ (Liu, 2010; Ping, 2011; Zhu, Li, Wang, Zhou, & Wang, 2016). In 2008, the international technology scanning programme sponsored by the FHWA, the American Association of State Highway and Transportation Officials (AASHTO) and the National Cooperative Highway Research Programme (NCHRP) set up a scan team to evaluate innovative foreign technologies and practices in highway transportation systems. The scan team mainly visited France, the French Governmental agencies and contractors organised both workshops, visits on plants and WMA roadworks. After the scan was completed, team members developed comprehensive reports, in particular: ‘Warm-Mix Asphalt: European Practice’ (D’angelo et al., 2008). By March, NCHRP began researching WMA mixtures’ construction performance, gas emissions and performance to establish a WMA technology inspection system (Liu et al., 2009). In November 2008, the first international WMA conference was held in Nashville, Tennessee. Over 700 pavement professionals from around the world attended the conference. In that year alone, more than 36 states in the USA used WMA technology in new or overhauled pavement projects. By October 2011, the 2nd international WMA conference was held in St. Louis, Missouri, focusing on the global application of WMA technology and discussing its main problems and limitations (Song, He, Zhao, & Li, 2012).

According to NAPA survey data, the usage of WMA mixtures in the USA grew from 19.2 million tonnes in 2009 to 47.6 million tonnes in 2010, with an annual growth rate of 148%. In 2019, it reached 164 million tonnes, accounting for 38.9% of the asphalt mixtures’ consumption (Yao, 2016). ## 2.3. China

In May 2005, various WMA mixtures were jointly studied by the Research Institute of Highway Ministry of Transport (RIOH), the Beijing Municipal Road and Bridge Building Materials Group Co., Ltd. and the MeadWestvaco Co. The first WMA test road was successfully paved on the Beijing National Road 110 auxiliary line in September 2005 (Li et al., 2008). In 2006, the Ministry of Transport included the WMA technology research into the plan of the western transportation construction technology project, and the science and technology project ‘Study on the Application of WMA Technology’ declared by RIOH and other units jointly comprehensively evaluated the existing WMA technology and implemented test road research in many parts of the country. The Shanghai Municipal Corporation paved the second WMA test road in Shanghai. The world’s first modified SMA WMA test road was paved in Beijing (Wu, Shi, & Zhang, 2018). Since then, universities, research institutions and enterprises have invested in the research of WMA technology. In 2007, Jiangsu Transportation Research Institute first implemented smoke-free tunnel construction in the Taijin expressway Cangling tunnel. In August 2007, the Southeast University cooperated with Changzhou Traffic Engineering Co. to successfully conduct a trial paving of WMA mixtures on provincial road 340. By September 2007, the Henan Yiyang Expressway $\mathsf { C o } ,$ Ltd. used the Evotherm DAT to carry out pavement of middle and top layers in the test road in the Henan Zhubi expressway. While carrying out scientific research and engineering applications, relevant technical standards for WMA technology have been established and permanently refined. The Qinghai Province promulgated the ‘Technical specifications for construction of WMA mixtures’ based on its application experience and climatic conditions in 2009. The Beijing municipal government formulated and promulgated the ‘Technical guidelines for Beijing WMA pavement’ in January 2010. Subsequently, the engineering construction standard ‘Technical regulations for pavements using WMA’ was introduced in Shanghai in 2011. China’s national standard ‘WMA concrete’ was officially released and implemented in December 2014. In addition, the WMA products and technical standards of other provinces and cities in China are currently being implemented or finalised (Liang & Wu, 2008; Song et al., 2012; Yang & Tan, 2014; Zhang, Liu, Tang, & Tang, 2009).

## 3. WMA technology introduction

According to the difference in warm-mix mechanisms, the WMA technology is classified as foaming technology, organic additives and chemical additives.

## 3.1. Foaming technology

The foaming technology implies a small amount of water being added to the hot asphalt through specific technical means; the steam formed by evaporating water is trapped and wrapped in asphalt, inducing asphalt volume expansion to form a foamed asphalt. According to the difference in adding form of foaming water, the foaming technology divided into water-based processes and waterbearing additives. The water-based processes directly introduce a small amount of cold water into the hot bitumen through a water injection system to produce foamed asphalt; it has to be carried out in a foamed asphalt generator. When water-based processes produce foamed asphalt, selecting highgrade (penetration) or medium-grade asphalt is necessary, and the foaming water content should be appropriate. The asphalt foaming characteristics are commonly evaluated by expansion ratio and halflife; some new indices are proposed in recent studies, such as foam index, surface area index, rate of collapse of semistable foam and the bubble size distribution (Arega et al., 2014; Ozturk & Kutay, 2014). The main factors affecting asphalt foaming characteristics are foaming temperature, foaming water content, asphalt type, foaming pressure, additives and so on. Typical water injection systems include Accu-Shear (Stansteel), Aquablack (Maxam), AquaFoam, Double Barrel Green (Astec), etc. (Wang, Huang, & Qin, 2019) The foaming water content is 1–2% by mass of asphalt, which can expand the asphalt volume 5–10 times and reduce the asphalt mixture’s production temperature by $20{-}30^{\circ}C$ The water-based process is shown in Table 1 (Behnood, 2020; Kheradmand et al., 2014; Rubio et al., $\mathsf { 2 0 1 2 ; X i a o   e t   a l } , \mathsf { 2 0 1 0 ) }$ , and the indoor asphalt foaming equipment is described in Table 2 (Weng, 2016). Water-bearing additives, commonly known as zeolite, are minerals containing bound water of 20–30% mass, slowly releasing the bound water at temperatures of $8 0 \sim 1 8 5 ^ { \circ } C$ (Tang, Wang, Fu, Wu, & Wang, 2020). The water-bearing additives belong to the micro-foaming technology, mainly because the foam produced during the asphalt foaming process is composed of micro-bubbles, which can expand the volume of the asphalt by 1.67 times. The foaming process generally lasts 2–3 h, and the zeolite mineral eventually remains in the asphalt. The available water-bearing additives are synthetic zeolites, like Aspha-min and Advera. Natural zeolite, like clinoptilolite, is also used for foamed asphalt research (Sengoz et al., 2013; Sengoz & Gorkem, 2013; Topal et al., 2014; Valdes-Vidal et al., 2018; Woszuk & Franus, 2016). The usage of water-bearing additives commonly adopts a dry mixing process, and it can reduce the production temperature by $20 \sim 30^{\circ}C$ with $0.25 \sim 0.3\%$ by mass of mixtures. In addition, there are several other foaming technologies, including the following ones.

(1) Low-energy asphalt, a patented technology originated in France, adopts the following mixing sequence: In the first step, the preheated coarse aggregate and asphalt are mixed, where the coarse aggregate usually is heated to $1 5 0 ^ { \circ } \mathsf { C } ,$ and the asphalt is heated to normal mixing temperature, the unique formulations additives are added according to the need to regulate the expansion of the foam and to act as an antistripping agent. The wet fine aggregate with 3–4% moisture at ambient temperature is added and mixed in the second step. In this process, the wet fine aggregate loses water when contacting the hot mixtures, the water evaporates to make the asphalt foam and the foamed asphalt evenly coats the fine aggregate. The mixtures temperature equilibrium is reached under $1 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathsf { C }$ (Carter & Olard, 2012; Olard, Le Noan, Bonneau, Jourdan, & Cherfils, 2010; Romier et al., 2006).

## 3.2. Organic additives

Organic additives can be subdivided into three major categories: Fischer–Tropsch waxes, Montan waxes and fatty acid amides. The available organic additives include Sasobit, Asphaltan B, Licomont BS-100, Bitutech PER, Astech PER, SonneWarmix, Thiopave, LEADCAP, etc. (Wang et al., 2019) Using organic additives in asphalt mixtures can improve their workability and deformation resistance; they can be completely dissolved in asphalt to reduce its viscosity at higher temperatures, while at lower temperatures, they crystallise from the asphalt forming a net-like lattice structure, enhancing the asphalt stability (see Figure 3). Therefore, users can select the organic additives with a suitable melting point (Ji, Xu, & Luo, 2010; Li, Wang, & Huang, 2011; $\mathsf { W e i } _ { r }$ 2008). The common dosage of organic additives is $2   \sim   4 \%$ by mass of the mixtures, and the construction temperature reduction of the asphalt mixtures is $20 \sim 30^{\circ}C$ (Rubio et al., 2012). Table 5 lists common organic additives (Al-Ansary et al., 2010; Behnood, 2020; Capitao et al., 2012; Kheradmand et al., 2014; Rubio et al., 2012).

Using chemical additives to produce WMA mixtures can reduce the high-temperature viscosity of asphalt and improve the adhesion of asphalt and aggregates (Tang et al., 2020). The surfactant molecules, whose one end is an oleophilic group and the other is a hydrophilic group, in chemical additives; the oleophilic group connects with the asphalt; the hydrophilic group combines with the water molecules. The surfactant molecules adsorb the water molecules to move with the asphalt, and the water molecules can reduce the asphalt viscosity during the mixing process. ## 4. WMA mixtures design and construction temperatures determination

## 4.1. Mix design method

Due to no particular requirements in the mix design of WMA mixtures, almost all WMA mix design protocols are similar to those of conventional HMA mixtures (Wang et al., 2018a). The widely implemented mix design methods include Marshall, Superpave, high-modulus asphalt mixtures, Hubbard-field, Hveem and so on (Asi, 2007; Roberts et al., 2002). The Marshall method, with some regional differences, is widely used in Europe and Asia, while the Superpave method and high-modulus asphalt mixtures are mainly used in the USA and France, respectively (Corte, 2001; Zaumains, 2010; Zeiada et al., 2022). A review by Kumar and Suresha (2019) stated that the critical aspects of the mix design of WMA mixtures are similar to those of HMA mixtures, including materials selection, aggregate gradation, asphalt content, additives dosage, compaction method, test environment requirements, etc.

## 4.1.1. Materials’ selection

As for the asphalt selection, WMA mixtures usually use the same grade as HMA mixtures, but the technical requirement must meet the project site’s climate and traffic conditions. Some studies suggest that the asphalt should select harder asphalt for certain types of WMA technology because low production temperature reduces the asphalt aging and good workability decreases the air voids; the above two aspects increase the risk of rutting damage on WMA pavements compared with the HMA pavements (Hurley & Prowell, 2005a, 2005b; Kim et al., 2011). It should be noted that this effect can also be offset by using recycled asphalt or crumb rubber-modified asphalt (Akisetty, Xiao, Gandhi, & Amirkhanian, 2011; Arega et al., 2011; Rodríguez-Alloza et al., 2014). According to the USA code for mix design of WMA mixtures, the production temperature of WMA mixtures is $5 6 ^ { \circ } C$ or lower than that of HMA mixtures; its asphalt’s high-temperature grade needs to increase by one grade to meet the anti-rutting performance required by the specification (TRB, 2014).

The WMA mixtures are similar to the HMA mixtures for the aggregate selection. Considering the reduction in production temperature of WMA mixtures compared to the HMA mixtures, there is a problem of the aggregate incomplete drying for the preparation of WMA mixtures (Kim et al., 2012; Yee & Hamzah, 2019), and some studies suggest that attention should be paid to the aggregate’s water content (D’angelo et al., 2008; Sukhija & Saboo, 2021).

In addition, WMA technology is commonly used to improve RAP content in hot recycled mixtures due to high production temperature will cause secondary aging of RAP, and too high RAP content will reduce the workability of mixtures (N. S. Guo et al., 2014, M. Guo et al., 2020; Mallick et al., 2008; Nejad et al., 2014; Tao & Mallick, 2009; Vidal et al., 2013; Zhao et al., 2013). There is a concern for the usage of RAP that the particles of RAP milled from asphalt pavements are of various sizes; the milled RAP needs to be crushed and screened before utilisation; this helps mix designers control the properties of asphalt mixtures by varying the RAP content and virgin aggregate and binder contents. Generally, a typical asphalt plant separates RAP into two or three sizes according to the stockpile size (Copeland, 2011; Hettiarachchi et al., 2019).

## 4.1.2. .Aggregate gradation

For the aggregate gradation of WMA mixtures, Koenders et al. (2020) and Romier et al. (2006) noted that WMA technology applies not only to dense-graded mixtures, but also to other gradations, including SMA, open-graded, stone-filled, coarse base mixtures. So far, WMA mixtures have been used in all types of aggregate gradation, including dense-graded, stone mastic, porous and mastic asphalt (Zaumains, 2010), which adopt the same gradation as HMA mixtures in field and laboratory tests (Copeland et al., 2010; Prowell et al., 2007; Wasiuddin et al., 2007; Xiao et al., 2009; Zelelew et al., 2013). These researches show no need to modify or adjust the aggregate gradation to better adapt to WMA mixtures (Chowdhury & Button, 2008).

## 4.1.3. Asphalt content

For the optimum asphalt content of WMA mixtures, Zaumains (2010) suggested that the following two points had to be considered. Lower mixing temperature leads to less asphalt absorption occurring in aggregates; Better compaction possibility results in fewer air voids; this indicates a necessity to reduce the asphalt content in WMA mixtures. Experiments of Hurley and Prowell (2006b) proved that the optimum asphalt content of WMA mixtures could be reduced by about 0.5% compared to an equivalent HMA. However, until now, almost all studies recommend that WMA mixtures adopt the same asphalt content as traditional HMA mixtures. One of the primary reasons is the concern that the reduced asphalt content may lead to problems in durability, permeability and water damage of the resulting paving mixtures.

After conducting a lot of research, NCAT specifies that the optimum asphalt content of WMA mixtures should be determined following the HMA standard method without considering the addition of WMA additives and then be used in WMA mixtures (Button, Estakhri, & Wimsatt, 2007; Chowdhury & Button, 2008). In addition, Wang (2013) proposed a volume parameter calculation method for determining the asphalt content of WMA mixtures; the design air voids were assumed to be 4%, and the void in mineral aggregate was taken as the minimum value required. The relevant calculation parameters of asphalt and mineral materials were brought into the volume parameter calculation formula for a back-calculation to obtain the asphalt content.

## 4.1.4. Particularities for water-based processes

Currently, the design of foamed asphalt mixtures adopted water-based processes prevalently uses the standard mix design protocols to determine the optimum asphalt content and then estimate the foaming water content based on the foaming equipment manufacturer’s recommendation, engineering judgment, or previous experience. However, numerous experiments indicated that certain asphalts had little foaming ability and minor changes in the foaming water content could result in a considerable difference in foamed mixtures’ workability and coatability. This practice ignored the validation of asphalt foaming ability and failed to account for the effect of foaming on asphalt and mixture properties, therefore, could not ensure the optimal foamed asphalt mixtures’ performance. NCHRP developed a foamed asphalt mix design method that involved validating asphalt foaming ability and involved the evaluation of workability and coatability for optimising foaming water content (Newcomb et al., 2015). In the study of Yin et al. (2016), additions to the standard mix design procedure were proposed to accommodate foamed asphalt mixtures, including workability evaluation for optimising foaming water content and coatability evaluation of mixture performance.

The summary of the mix design of WMA mixtures is represented in Table 7. At present, the mix design method of WMA mixtures is the same as that of HMA mixtures on the whole, only for the warm-mix characteristics in some specific design aspects of the adjustment to make the performance of WMA mixtures meet the relevant technical requirements. This way is easy for professional technicians to master and use, effectively addressing engineering applications’ needs. However, there has not formed a scientific and standard method for selecting asphalt grade and asphalt content, and the impact of those on the mixtures’ performance has a different understanding, though some researchers have tried to study the influence of asphalt grade and content, as well as aggregate moisture, on final mixtures’ performance (Olard, Beduneau, Bonneau, Dupriet, & Seignez, 2009; Olard, Beduneau, Seignez, Dupriet, & Bonneau 2009). In future research, the influence of warm-mix characteristics on the asphalt grade and content, as well as incompletely dry aggregate and asphalt content should be investigated to establish a set of practical, accurate and effective mix design methods for WMA mixtures.

## 4.2. Construction temperatures determination method

The standard method, popularly known as the equi-viscous method, for determining construction temperatures is developed for base asphalt based on viscosity measurements conducted at a single shear rate value (ASTM, 2016). The equi-viscous method application on modified asphalt often yields unrealistically high temperatures without incorporating the effect of shear rate because the viscosity of modified asphalt, unlike the base asphalt, has a dependency on shear rate even at higher temperatures $( > 1 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathsf { C } )$ (Yetkin et al., 2000). To eliminate the limitations of the equi-viscous method, its refinements have been proposed by many authors, including the zero shear viscosity method, simplified zero shear viscosity method, high shear rate method, high shear rate method evolution, steady shear flow and flow behaviour method (Almusawi et al., 2021; Saboo & Kumar, 2015; Sukhija et al., 2021; Watson, West, Turner, & Casola, 2010; Yetkin et al., 2000; Yildirim, Solaimanian, & Kennedy, 2006). For WMA, viscosity-based methods may be applicable for the WMA technologies that change the asphalt’s viscosity. Still, it is unsuitable for WMA technology with a weak influence on asphalt viscosity, such as foaming technology and chemical additives (Caputo et al., 2020; Silva, Oliveira, Ferreira, & Pereira, 2010). At the same time, several studies have confirmed that viscosity-based methods are insensitive to WMA (Bennert et al., 2010; Gudimettla, Allen Cooley, & Ray Brown, 2003; Mo et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2013). Hence, various researchers have done numerous studies to specify a more practical approach to determining construction temperatures for WMA. The available method for determining the construction temperatures of WMA can be divided into the analogy method, phase angle method, CEI & TDI method and 4% air voids method.

## 4.2.1. Analogy method

According to the analogy method, the compacting temperature of WMA mixtures is determined by the compacting temperature of HMA mixtures, at which the analogy index value of WMA mixtures is equal to that of HMA mixtures at the corresponding compacting temperature. As for the selection of analogy indices, different analogy indices have been proposed in different studies. The bulk density was proposed by the German Asphalt Paving Association (Rubio et al., 2012); the asphalt film thickness was recommended by Prowell et al. (Hurley & Prowell, 2005b; Jamshidi et al., 2013); air voids were selected by Silva, Oliveira et al. (Oliveira et al., 2012, 2013; Silva, Oliveira, Peralta, & Zoorob,2010); the mixing torque was put forward by Wang et al. (2013); the mixing current was used by Yang (2012). To reduce the workload, Ma, He, Zhao, and Huang (2015) used the analogy method with viscosity index to determine the mixing and compaction temperature of WMA mixtures in terms of the asphalt mortar. In this method, the base asphalt mortar and WMA mortar were prepared with a filler-asphalt ratio of 1.

## 4.2.2. Phase angle method

In this method, the phase-angle master curve is established from the frequency sweep test data using a reference temperature of $8 0 ^ { \circ } \mathsf { C }$ through the ‘Time-Temperature Superposition’ procedure; the frequency corresponding to the phase angle of $8 6 ^ { \circ }$ is substituted into Equations (1) and (2) to calculate the mixing and compacting temperature (Bennert et al., 2010; West, 2008).

$$
T _ { m } ( ^ { \circ } \mathsf { F } ) = 3 2 5 \omega ^ { - 0 . 0 1 3 }\tag{1}
$$

$$
T _ { c } ( ^ { \circ } \mathsf { F } ) = 3 0 0 \omega ^ { - 0 . 0 1 2 }\tag{2}
$$

where $T _ { m }$ is the mixing temperature, °F; $T _ { c }$ is the compacting temperature, $^ { \circ } \mathbb { F } ;$ ω is the frequency, rad/s.

## 4.2.3. CEI & TDI method

In this method, the compaction energy index (CEI) and the traffic densification index (TDI) are derived from the gyratory compaction curves to determine WMA mixtures’ construction temperatures. The temperature with the lowest CEI value is preferred as the construction temperatures. If there are two or more equal CEI values, the temperature corresponding to the maximum TDI value is the construction temperature (Sanchez-Alonso et al., 2011).

## 4.2.4. 4% air voids’ method

A study by Qin (2009) proposed to use air voids of 4% as the target value to determine the construction temperatures of WMA mixtures. Several studies subsequently adopted this method, which showed that it is the most reliable and feasible method, although it somewhat increased the workload (Sun, Wang, & Meng, 2010; Wang et al., 2022; Wang & Huang, 2010).

In future research, a comparative study of the various methods used to determine the construction temperatures of WMA mixtures needs to be conducted to establish a standard method in terms of practicality, reliability and economy.

## 5.2. Organic additives

Currently, the most representative product of organic additives is Sasobit, which, German Sasol Wax Corp.’s product, was initially used as an anti-rutting additive and then adopted as a WMA additive due to its ability to reduce the viscosity of asphalt. Sasobit is a mixture of narrowly distributed synthetic saturated hydrocarbons produced in the coal gasification process using the Fischer–Tropsch process, so it is also called Fischer–Tropsch paraffin. It is usually in granular form and can be directly added to asphalt or mixtures. The dosage of Sasobit is generally within the range of $0 . 8   \sim   4 \%$ by the mass of asphalt.

Table 12 shows the summary of Sasobit effects on the performance of WMA mixtures. The integrated statistics on Sasobit effects on the performance of WMA mixtures are shown in Figure 7. It can be seen from Table 12 and Figure 7 that the research on the performance of Sasobit WMA mixtures mainly focused on the high-temperature performance, low-temperature performance and water stability, while only a few studies were devoted to their fatigue performance. Different studies have consistently shown that adding Sasobit to mixtures could improve the high-temperature rutting resistance. Sasobit is a fine crystalline and long-chain aliphatic polymethylene hydrocarbon with a melting temperature of about $1 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathsf { C } .$ It can crystallise from the asphalt to form a net-like lattice structure to enhance the high-temperature stability of the asphalt binder when the temperature is lower than its melting point (Chen et al., 2021). In contrast, existing studies have not yet reached a consensus on the effect of Sasobit on the low-temperature performance, water stability and fatigue performance of the asphalt mixtures, which mainly results from the difference in the test materials, test conditions and evaluation indicators used in different studies.

## (1) Evotherm

The Evotherm series of WMA additives is a chemically active agent package developed by Mead Westvaco Corp. So far, three generations of Evotherm ET, Evotherm DAT and Evotherm 3G have been launched. Evotherm ET is an asphalt emulsion prepared by surfactants, with a solid content of 70%. It can be directly mixed with hot aggregates to produce WMA mixtures, with a temperature drop above $5 5 ^ { \circ } C ,$ Evotherm DAT is a specific concentration aqueous solution prepared with surfactants, using the same chemical ingredients as Evotherm ET but releasing less water to the asphalt mixtures than Evotherm ET. It can be directly added to asphalt or mixing drums during the production of asphalt mixtures. As recommended by the manufacturer, the preferred dosage of Evotherm DAT is 5% by mass of asphalt binders, which can reduce the construction temperature by about $4 5 \sim 5 5 ^ { \circ } C$ . Evotherm 3G is an anhydrous mixture composed of surfactants, emulsifiers and anti-stripping agents. It is used in the same way as Evotherm DAT. In contrast, Evotherm 3G has a more powerful effect on reducing the construction temperature; only 0.5% by the asphalt mass can reduce the construction temperatures by $3 5 \sim 4 5 ^ { \circ } C$ (Arega et al., 2011). Considering the convenience, Evotherm ET has been replaced by Evotherm DAT and Evotherm 3G.

Since the Evotherm series of WMA additives launch, the application research on Evotherm WMA mixtures has been ongoing. Table 13 is the summary of Evotherm effects on the performance of WMA mixtures. The integrated statistics of Evotherm effects on the performance of WMA mixtures are shown in Figure 8. It can be seen from Table 13 and Figure 8 that adding Evothrem is adverse to the high-temperature rutting resistance of the asphalt mixtures but contributes to the fatigue resistance. Evotherm in the asphalt mixtures can reduce the construction temperatures, weakening the asphalt aging in the mixing and compaction process. In addition, the lipophilic group of surfactants molecule in Evotherm additives is connected with the asphalt, and its hydrophilic group is connected with the aggregate, enhancing the adhesion strength between the asphalt and the aggregate (Capitao et al., 2012); this is beneficial to the improvement of fatigue resistance. However, the existing research on the low-temperature performance and water stability of WMA mixtures adding Evotherm additives has not yet reached a unified conclusion due to the difference in test materials, test condition, test method and evaluation standards used in different studies.

## 5.4. Summary

Regarding the single WMA product in different WMA technologies on the performance of asphalt mixtures, the relatively consistent conclusions proposed in different studies mainly include: -1 The water-bearing additives, such as Aspha-min and Advera, are adverse to the water stability of the asphalt mixtures. -2 Water-based processes harm the low-temperature performance and water stability of the asphalt mixtures. -3 Sasobit can improve the high-temperature anti-rutting performance of the asphalt mixtures. -4 Evotherm added to asphalt mixtures can improve the anti-fatigue performance but harms the high-temperature anti-rutting performance. -5 Adding Rediset can improve the anti-water damage and anti-fatigue performance of asphalt mixtures. Based on the above findings, for the selection and use of the above WMA products, we give the following recommendations: -1 Sasobit is recommended for highways in hot areas, heavy traffic sections of highways and grade I highways, long slope sections of mountainous highways and where rutting is expected to occur. -2 For roads and highways in humid and rainy areas, heavy traffic sections of highways and where water damage is expected to occur, the use of foaming technology (Aspha-min, Advera and Water-based processes) is not suitable and Rediset is recommended. -3 For road sections in cold region with large temperature differences, large traffic flow and where fatigue damage is expected to occur, the use of Water-based processes is not suitable and Evotherm and Rediset are recommended.

In future research, the different WMA products on the performance of asphalt mixtures should be studied to clarify the positive and negative effects of different WMA products. On the one hand, optimisation measures of performance should be put forward for different WMA products. On the other hand, guidance should be established for selecting and using different WMA products.

Apart from construction temperatures, the associated additional factors such as warm-mix additives, royalties and plant modifications also need to be considered in the entire life cycle of WMA pavement (Blankendaal et al., 2014; Hassan, 2010). In this regard, a complete life cycle assessment (LCA) is required to measure the economic, social and environmental impacts of WMA pavement during the service life. As a basic mature concept and analysis method, LCA has been accepted and applied in the road industry (Yu & Lu, 2012). The life-cycle of asphalt pavement is usually divided into five main stages: asphalt mixture production, construction, use, maintenance/rehabilitation and end-of-life (Sollazzo et al., 2020). To ascertain the comprehensive impact of WMA with different WMA technology, the present section compiles the information published on the LCA of WMA in terms of foaming technology, organic additives and chemical additives, including life-cycle cost analysis (LCCA), life-cycle environmental assessment (LCEA) and life-cycle social assessment (LCSA).

## 6.1. Foaming technology

## 6.1.1. Water-bearing additives

Research on the LCA of WMA used water-bearing additives, Vidal et al. (2013) evaluated the environmental impacts of different road paving with HMA and with WMA-added zeolite during their entire life cycle. It was found that the impact of WMA is almost equal to that of HMA, in which the reduction in the impact of WMA due to lowering the manufacturing temperature is offset by the greater impact of the zeolite used. A similar finding was observed by Hasan et al. (2020) that the air emissions and energy consumption of WMA are only slightly reduced compared to control HMA, the observed pattern also be attributed to the material production and processing of zeolite weaking the effect of energy saving and emission reduction provided by the lower heating.

## 6.1.2. Water-based processes

Water-based processes are heavily applied in asphalt industry, but research on its LCA is scared. Mazumder et al. (2016) quantified the environmental burdens of the HMA and the WMA with waterbased processes. Compared with HMA, WMA achieves a reduction of 26% on the global warming impact, a reduction of 29% on acidification and a reduction of 25% on both fossil fuel consumption and photo oxidant formation in the life-cycle period excluding the end-of-life phase. For the environmental impact during asphalt mixture production, it was found by Mohd Hasan and You (2015) that the WMA using water-based processes reduced the energy consumption and total GHG emissions about 1.96% and 5.65% compared with HMA, respectively.

With respect to the low-energy asphalt, Ventura et al. (2009) conducted a LCEA both half-WMA mixtures with low-energy asphalt process and HMA mixtures in the stages of asphalt mixture production and construction. The classical environmental indicators of Global Warming Potential (GWP), Acidification Potential (AP), Photochemical Ozone Creation Potential (POCP) and Eutrophication Index (EI) were calculated. It can be seen from Figure 10 that all computed indicators are lower for half-WMA mixtures than for HMA mixtures, hence expressing the better environmental performance achieved for the half-WMA mixtures with low-energy asphalt process.

## 6.2. Organic additives

According to the LCA of WMA with organic additives, Zheng et al. (2019) conducted a life-cycle sustainability assessment case, including HMA mixtures, WMA mixtures with Sasobit (WMAS) and HMA mixtures with RAP (HMAR), using a comprehensive pavement life-cycle sustainability assessment methodology that integrated LCCA, LCEA and LCSA. The case mainly focused on the maintenance strategies, only consisted of two phases of pavement life-cycle: asphalt mixture production and construction. As shown in Table 15, HMAR contributed the best economic performance, while WMAS achieved the best environmental and social performance. To evaluate the Sasobit dosage on environmental impact of WMA mixtures, Rodríguez-Alloza et al. (2015) quantified the embodied energy requirement and GHG emissions via a comprehensive hybrid input-output-based LCA. It was found that the mixtures with 2% and 4% Sasobit could achieve from 19% to 16% reduction on the total energy requirement. The higher Sasobit dosage, the lower energy saving, as a result of the energy to produce the wax offset the energy saving brought by lower construction temperatures. As for the GHG emissions savings, the mixtures could achieve a 16% to 18% reduction (for 4% and 2% Sasobit, respectively) of the total $\mathsf { C O } _ { 2 }$ per tonne of mixtures manufactured compared to HMA mixtures.

## 6.3. Chemical additives

At present, the relevant studies on the LCA of WMA mixtures with chemical additives are available, involving different chemical additives such as Evotherm, Rediset. For the Evotherm, Ma et al. (2019) conducted a LCEA for WMA with Evotherm and HMA pavements. Assuming comparable long-term performances with that of HMA pavement, the environmental impacts of WMA pavement related to greenhouse gases and PM 2.5 emissions were significantly less than that of HMA pavement, while the resource consumptions of both WMA and HMA pavements in entire life-cycle were almost at the same level due to the energy consumption of additive production offset the energy saving by lowing construction temperatures. According to Cheng, Chen, Yan, and Zheng’s (2010) study, when life cycle of pavement only considering the two stages: asphalt mixture production and construction, using WMA to replace HMA can achieve a reduction of 22.4% on energy consumption, more than 70% of photochemical ozone formation potentials saving, and above 55% reduction for three impact categories of global warming 100 years, acidification and human toxicity via air, respectively. With respect to the Rediset, Butt and Birgisson (2015) evaluated the environmental impact of WMA pavement using aggregates from two different sources with or without Rediset. For the skärlunda aggregate, adding Rediset reduces the $\mathsf { C O } _ { 2 - \mathsf { e q } }$ emissions and total expended energy were 38% and 34%, while reduction of the $\mathsf { C O } _ { 2 - \mathsf { e q } }$ emissions and total expended energy were 12% and 2% for Arlanda aggregate.

In addition, there are some studies without telling the additive’s name. Leng and Al-Qadi (2011) evaluated and compared the life-cycle environmental and economic performances of two mixtures: a warm SMA with a chemical additive and a hot SMA. With mixing temperature decreases from $1 6 3   ^ { \circ } \mathsf { C }$ to 138 °C, 6.8%, 6.5%, 7.5% and 4.2% reductions were achieved for global warming, fossil fuel depletion, criteria air pollutant and smog in the stages of asphalt mixture production and construction. The initial construction cost of the warm SMA was slightly higher than that of the hot SMA caused for using additive, and the user cost of the warm SMA was 25.4% less than that of the control SMA for earlier traffic opening, but the total economic cost of the warm SMA was lower than that of the hot SMA in the whole life cycle. Besides, Vega-Araujo et al. (2019, 2020) also undertaken LCA of WMA and HMA to evaluate the potential environmental impact associated with the use of chemical additives for the production of WMA. The results of 10 impact categories, including ozone depletion, global warming, photochemical smog formation, acidification, eutrophication, human health cancer, human health noncancer, human health particulate or respiratory effects, ecotoxicity and fossil fuel depletion, indicated the use of chemical additives for WMA leads to a positive effect of the environmental profile with respect to the HMA mixtures.

## 6.4. Comparison of different WMA technology

The manufacture of WMA mixtures using different WMA technologies adopts different additives or production processes. Thus, the comprehensive impact of WMA pavements using different WMA technologies is also different in their entire life-cycle period. According to Weng’s (2016) research, not all WMA technologies had energy saving and emission reduction benefits compared with HMA. WMA using water-based processes had the most significant energy saving and emission reduction benefits, while the WMA technology using warm-mix additives also required energy consumption due to the production of additives, resulting in a weakened energy saving and emission reduction benefits in the asphalt mixtures production phase, especially WMA with the zeolite, whose energy consumption was even slightly higher than that of the HMA. Belc et al. (2021) conducted a comparative environmental assessment of certain WMA mixtures prepared with organic additives, chemical additives and synthetic zeolite and the corresponding HMA mixtures. Compared with HMA mixtures, the WMA mixtures with organic and chemical additives achieved a smaller environmental impact, while the WMA with synthetic zeolite resulted in a bigger environmental impact. This conclusion had also been verified in Anthonissen et al.’s (2015) study, it was found that the environmental impact of WMA with synthetic zeolite was higher than that of HMA, while the WMA with organic Fischer–Tropsch wax leads to a lower environmental impact than the HMA.

With respect to the comparison of specific WMA productions in different WMA technology, Tatari et al. (2012) evaluated the environmental benefits of different WMA pavements. The rank of resource consumption in different WMA pavements is WMA with Aspha-min (WMAA) > WMA with Sasobit (WMAS) > WMA with Evotherm (WMAE) > HMA, and that of total emissions is WMAA > WMAE > HMA > WMAS. Besides, Martinez-Soto et al. (2023) calculated and compared the environmental benefits of the HMA mixtures and WMA mixtures with natural zeolite or Evotherm. Results showed that a mean reduction of 5% for WMA was observed in all environmental impact indicators (global warming, terrestrial acidification, freshwater eutrophication, marine ecotoxicity, human toxicity, agricultural land occupation and ozone depletion) and the greatest difference was produced in the human toxicity where the difference was up to 14%. The results of the fossil resource scarcity indicated that there were no significant differences between the WMA mixtures with zeolite or Evotherm and the HMA mixtures.

## 6.5. Summary

It can be seen from the foregoing review that the studies on the LCA of WMA are scanty, a more systematic and complete comparative study between different WMA technologies is further recommended. Based on the above findings, the relatively consistent conclusions, in terms of foaming technology, organic additives and chemical additives, proposed in different studies mainly include: From the perspective of the whole life-cycle, -1 the environmental benefits of WMA mixtures prepared with water-bearing additives are poor because the production and processing of additives pull-down the effect of energy saving and emission reduction produced by lowing manufacture temperature; -2 the environmental benefits of WMA mixtures prepared by water-based processes are best as a result of only water is injected during manufacture process; -3 the environmental benefits of WMA mixtures prepared with organic additives or chemical additives are general due to a certain amount of energy consumption and emissions generated by the additives’ production, which have some impacts on the energy saving and emission reduction effect brought by lower temperature; -4 the rank of environmental benefits of different WMA technologies is water-based processes > organic additives and chemical additives > water-bearing additives. Based on the above conclusions, assuming the long-term performance of WMA mixtures is equal to that of HMA mixtures, it is preferable to choose water-based processes for WMA preparation from the point of view of whole life-cycle environmental benefits, followed by organic additives and chemical additives, and should not water-bearing additives.

## 7. Conclusions

Compared with conventional HMA mixtures, WMA mixtures provide significant environmental, technical and economic benefits. In line with the concept of developing a circular economy and building low-carbon transportation, WMA technology has been a research hotspot in pavement industry. This paper mainly composes, analyses and summarises the researches on WMA mixtures’ design, construction temperatures, performance and LCA. Some conclusions are obtained as follow.

(1) The mix design method of WMA mixtures is the same as that of HMA mixtures on the whole, only for the warm-mix characteristics in some specific design aspects of the adjustment to make the performance of WMA mixtures meet the relevant technical requirements or equal to HMA mixtures’ performance, such as, harder asphalt should be selected, paying attention to the aggregate’s moisture content, and WMA mixtures using water-based processes need to validate the foaming ability of asphalt and evaluate the workability and coatability of mixtures.

(2) Mixing and compacting temperatures are key control indicators for construction of asphalt mixtures. The method of determining the mixing and compaction temperatures of WMA mixtures mainly includes the analogy method, phase angle method, CEI & TDI method and 4% air voids method, with no unified standard method being established yet.

(3) Various WMA products have different effects on the performance of asphalt mixtures, and special attention should be paid to their application scenarios. In particular, Aspha-min and Advera are not recommended for road sections prone to water damage. Water-based processes are not suitable for road sections prone to low-temperature cracking and water damage. Sasobit is recommended for road sections prone to rutting. Evotherm is suitable for road sections prone to fatigue cracking but not for road sections prone to rutting. Rediset is suitable for road sections prone to fatigue cracking and water damage.

(4) From the perspective of the whole life-cycle, the environmental benefits of WMA mixtures with water-based processes in WMA technologies are the best, followed by organic additives and chemical additives, and the worst is water-bearing additives. Water-based processes are recommended for the production of WMA mixtures from the perspective of environmental protection, followed by organic additives and chemical additives, and should not water-bearing additives.
