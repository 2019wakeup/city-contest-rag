## A B S T R A C T

The use of asphalt mixtures containing previously paved asphalt mixture from milled pavements, so-called reclaimed asphalt pavements or RAP, has been around for more than half a century. Historically, relatively low RAP contents were used, around 15% of the total mix. Recently, the use of high RAP contents has increased and now it is common to design mixtures with 30% or higher RAP contents, especially in the US. At these higher recycled contents, the characteristics of the recycled material begin to significantly affect the behaviors of the asphalt mixtures and alter the pavement performance. The use of recycled materials in asphalt mixtures at these contents may result in diminishing returns in terms of prospective performance at or beyond a certain incorporation dosage level. To overcome this barrier, recycling agents (RA) have been proposed to extend the breakeven point and/or provide savings, both in terms of cost-effectiveness as well as environmental impacts. This paper is an examination of the use of RAs in recycled asphalt mixtures. There have been many reviews regarding RAs in the recent past, notably the review compiled as part of the United States National Cooperative Highway Research Program (NCHRP) Project 09–58. However, this field is evolving very quickly and there have been notable advances that require a renewed examination of the state of the art with respect to RA usage. In these intervening years, some specific advances have been made to propose novel methods for understanding the chemical nature of RAs, how RAP interacts with the virgin binder and RA, and how acceptable performance of recycled asphalt binder blends and mixtures can be achieved through dosage selection methods. However, the scientific knowledge on the selection and use of RAs in asphalt pavements is limited, and there is a need to develop robust methodologies that establish threshold criteria and performance metrics to facilitate their use on a regular basis. While there do exist some general trends with respect to RA effectiveness, the specific benefits and limitations found in individual studies vary greatly. Thus, given the current state of knowledge, RAs should be evaluated on a material-by-material basis using asphalt mixtures designed and delivered using local practices.

## 1. Introduction

The use of asphalt mixtures containing previously paved asphalt mixture from milled pavements, so-called reclaimed asphalt pavements or RAP, has been around for more than half a century (Al-Qadi et al., 2007). For many years, the typical content levels were low enough that the asphalt mixtures containing recycled asphalt materials could be reliably designed through the methods used to design mixtures that did not contain recycled materials without any substantial modifications. In the early 2000s, the National Cooperative Highway Research Program (NCHRP) Project 09–12 recommended using the same virgin asphalt binder grade for the mixtures containing up to 20% RAP content depending on the RAP binder stiffness (McDaniel et al., 2000). However, in recent years the recycled asphalt material contents have increased and now it is common to design mixtures with 30% or higher RAP (McDaniel et al., 2000). At these higher recycled contents, the effect of the recycled material begins to affect the behaviors of these asphalt mixtures and alter the pavement performance, which may result in diminishing returns at or beyond a certain incorporation dosage level if proper measures are not taken (Tarsi et al., 2020; Zaumanis et al., 2013).

To overcome this barrier, recycling agents (RAs) are used to extend the break-even point and/or provide savings, both in terms of performance, cost-effectiveness as well as environmental impacts. The primary purpose of using RAs is to restore the engineering properties of recycled materials so that these can be reused in pavement construction without sacrificing the short- or long-term performance. Although the use of recycled asphalt material is very beneficial in terms of reduction in production costs, conserving natural resources, reducing landfill space requirements, and above all, environment benefits of sustainability, it is essential to produce these mixes with promising performance to ensure pavement longevity, low maintenance costs, and use of higher RAP contents.

The scientific knowledge on the selection and use of RAs in asphalt pavements is limited, and there is a need to develop robust methodologies that establish threshold criteria and performance metrics to facilitate their use on a regular basis. In 2014, NCHRP Project 09–58 was commissioned to study the effects of RAs on asphalt mixtures containing high contents of recycled asphalt materials. It eventually recommended a draft AASHTO standard practice to characterize asphalt mixtures with high recycled materials contents incorporating RAs using recycled binder blend and asphalt mixture testing (Epps-Martin et al., 2020). The information presented in this study were collected through searching common databases such as TRIS (Transportation Research Information Services), Scopus, Google Scholar, ProQuest, Web of Science, etc. This literature review summarizes and synthesizes the various laboratory and field studies employing RAP and RAs to derive tangible lessons on their selection and use in recycling asphalt pavements. The performance concerns associated with the use of these products and shortcomings of the current specifications are highlighted, and potential means of improving binder characterization to enable evaluation of recycling agents, and dosage selection procedures are discussed. Finally, this review identifies the knowledge gaps that currently exist.

## 2. Fundamentals of recycling and recycling agents

## 2.1. Overview

Asphalt binder is a complex material whose properties are affected by its chemical and rheological make-up. Asphalt binder can be viewed as a colloid medium consisting of highly polar and friable asphaltenes dispersed in a fluid-like soluble maltene phase. The polar asphaltene phase is insoluble in n-heptane while the maltene phase is soluble. The maltene phase can be further divided in sub-phases based on the solubility of the maltenes in solvents of varying strength. The least polar and most easily dissolved phase are the saturates, the next highest phase in terms of polarity and dissolution potential are the aromatics, and finally the resins are the most polar and least solvent of the sub-phases contained within the maltenes. The resins are polar and peptize the asphaltenes in the aromatics and saturates. The balance of the saturates, aromatics, resins, and asphaltenes (i.e., SARA) components affects the rheological properties of asphalt binders. The rheological behavior of asphalt binders can be altered by the relative proportioning of these four phases (Aftgelt and Harle, 1975; Mousavi et al., 2016; Salehfard et al., 2021) and also by the dispersive properties of the maltenes (Aftgelt and Harle, 1975; Petersen, 2009; Yu et al., 2014). A higher proportion of maltene phase with strong dispersive power is related to higher ductility and more flowable asphalt binders. On the other hand, a higher proportion of asphaltenes and/or a maltene phase with lower dispersive power is related to lower ductility and less flowable binder.

Another important phenomenon affecting asphalt binder behaviors is oxidative aging. Oxidative aging is a process that begins to occur from the time that an asphalt mixture is being produced. It occurs as oxygen reacts with the polar components of asphalt and is purported to convert polar aromatics and resins to asphaltenes (Petersen, 2009). The loss of molecules in the maltene phase also causes the asphaltenes to agglomerate, as the maltene phase is no longer providing adequate dispersion.

Given this behavior, one approach to quantifying oxidation is to measure changes to the SARA fractions. While changes in the relative proportions of SARA fractions afford one way to understand and track oxidation, the simple mass proportion changes measured do not fully capture the chemical reactions taking place (Epps-Martin et al., 2020). More accurate and representative assessment of oxidative aging can be quantified by the amount of oxidation products, notably sulfoxides and carbonyls. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) can be used to track the formation of these functional groups, which occur at wavelengths of $1 7 0 0 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ and $1 0 3 0 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ , respectively. In addition to the sites for oxidation, additional elements such vanadium can also aid in aging. However, it should be noted that while some vanadium-containing molecules can catalyze oxidation, others do not (Petersen, 2009). The molecular size distribution within asphalt binders as measured by Gel Permeation Chromatography (GPC) and other techniques also evolves with aging. As an asphalt binder ages, a greater number of large molecules are produced and thus measures that quantify the proportion of large size molecules (LSM) as a proportion have been used to quantify aging (Li et al., 2020).

Oxidative aging can also be tracked by examining the rheological changes in asphalt over time. Generally speaking, oxidative aging causes increases in the viscosity, stiffness, and brittleness of asphalt. In the case of performance-graded binders, these changes affect both the high- and low-temperature performance grades (PGs). Binder grade data from NCHRP Project 09–58 is summarized in Table 1 and shows a comparison between reclaimed material binder grades (from Recycled Asphalt Pavement (RAP) and Recycled Asphalt Shingles (RAS)) and virgin binder grades used in various states (Epps-Martin et al., 2020). It can be seen that the high- and low-temperature grades of the reclaimed materials exceed that of the virgin, presumably due to oxidative aging. Hence, using these recycled (aged) binders in asphalt mixtures imparts stiffness and (though not directly evaluated in the PG system) brittleness, making them less workable, difficult to compact, and thus, prone to raveling, cracking, and other durability-related pavement distresses (Tran et al., 2012). In order to enable use of more recycled asphalt materials without compromising the pavement performance, many strategies have been employed such as using a softer PG grade virgin binder, warm-mix additives, RAs, or a combination thereof (Epps-Martin et al., 2020; Williams, 2017).

This review exclusively focuses on the use of RAs. Fundamentally, these agents act to restore the rheological properties of recycled and therefore, aged binders by either: a) improving asphaltene to maltene ratio that improves the rheology of the blends, b) reducing the size of asphaltene clusters, c) enhancing the dispersive power of maltene phase, and/or d) amplifying the molecular mobility. All four of these mechanisms will result in a reduction in viscosity, modulus and stiffness, and brittleness of the asphalt binder but vary in terms of their effectiveness, compatibility with asphalt binders of different origins, and long-term efficacy (Al-Saffar et al., 2021; Hassanpour-Kasanagh et al., 2020; Kaseer et al., 2019b; Mazzoni et al., 2018; Mohammadafzali et al., 2015; Shen et al., 2007a, 2007b; Tarsi et al., 2020; Xu et al., 2020; Zaumanis et al., 2014b; Zhou et al., 2020; Ziari et al., 2019a). The specific reported benefits of using a recycling agent include.

a. Improving the workability by reducing stiffness of recycled asphalt mixtures (Im et al., 2014; Kaseer et al., 2017; Munoz et al., 2015; Tran et al., 2012),

b. Improving cracking performance by reducing the embrittlement of recycled asphalt mixtures (A. Chen et al., 2021; Kaseer et al., 2018; W. S. Mogawer et al., 2013; Yin et al., 2017), and

c. Reducing production costs, emissions, and landfill space requirements (Haghshenas et al., 2019; Robinette and Epps, 2010; Zaumanis et al., 2016).

Despite the widespread understanding of the need and mechanisms of RAs, there is still a very limited understanding about the effectiveness of RAs in the above three categories, both in the short- and long-term, and their overall cost-to-benefit ratio. The research gaps concerning the use of RAs, which have limited their use by the departments of transportation (DOTs), can be broadly divided into six categories as shown in Fig. 1 (Habbouche et al., 2022; Hussein et al., 2020; Kaseer et al., 2019b; Tarsi et al., 2020; Zaumanis et al., 2014a). The lack of expertise and comprehensive studies in these six categories need to be addressed before using RAs on a regular basis.

## 2.2. Rejuvenation mechanism

It must be first understood that RAs do not restore the chemistry of an aged asphalt binder, i.e., these do not reverse the oxidation process. Rather their goal is to reverse the impacts of oxidation on rheology and performance characteristics of the binder. Rejuvenation mechanisms operate through three processes: dispersion, diffusion, and compatibility. The main points about each process are summarized in Table 2.

The dispersion of RA into the virgin binder and recycled asphalt should be uniform and homogeneous to ensure that all the RAP available binder is rejuvenated and useful in the recycled asphalt mixture. The dispersion can be affected by many factors including mixing time (Kaseer et al., 2019b), mixing temperature (Kaseer et al., 2019b; Romera et al., 2006; Zaumanis et al., 2013; Zhou et al., 2012), where and how the RA is added to the mixture (Behnood, 2019; Kaseer et al., 2019b; Tarsi et al., 2020; West and Copeland, 2015), RA dosage (Ar´ambula-Mercado et al., 2018; Im et al., 2014; Little et al., 1981; Mohammadafzali et al., 2017; Oliveira et al., 2013; Rodrí- guez-Fernandez ´ et al., 2019; Yan et al., 2014; Yin et al., 2014, 2017), recycled material content (E. Arambula-Mercado ´ et al., 2018; Karki and Zhou, 2016; Kaseer et al., 2019b; KASEER et al., 2020a; W. S. Mogawer et al., 2013; Roja et al., 2020; Yousefi et al., 2021), the inherent viscosity of the virgin binder and recycling agent, and others (Edith Arambula-Mercado ´ et al., 2018; Garcia Cucalon et al., 2019; Kaseer et al., 2017; KASEER et al., 2020a; Ma et al., 2010; McDaniel and Anderson, 2001). Another factor, which is indirectly affected by some of these factors, but is distinctly important is the tendency of the RAP materials to form clusters (Oliver and H., 2001). Clusters are agglomerations of the adhered RAP materials that do not break apart during the mixing process. When a substantial amount of these clusters exist, a large portion of the RAP binder may be inaccessible to the RA which affects the overall effectiveness of the RA to ensure good performance of the mixture (Bressi et al., 2015a, 2015b, 2016; Yang et al., 2021).

Bressi et al. (2015a) studied RAP clusters by preparing mix with fine RAP (less than 4 mm particles or less than 8 mm depending on mix) and coarse virgin aggregates (4–22 mm or 8–16 mm for other mix). The authors conducted sieve analysis on the mixed sample, extracted and recovered binder from the fine portion, and compared to design assuming complete blending. They confirmed clusters could exist using tracer-based microscopy. The authors proposed that clusters could prevent uniform distribution of the virgin binder, increasing the heterogeneity of the mixture. It could also reduce the surface area to be covered by virgin binder, thereby, reducing the required binder for adequate coating. This phenomenon was further evaluated and confirmed by Bressi et al. (2016, 2015b) in their subsequent studies. Xu et al. (2019) also postulated clustering to be an inhibitor of blending by comparing pre- and post-extraction RAP gradation and Cantabro-crushing test. Zhu et al. (2020) also arrived at the same conclusion by evaluating agglomerations in RAP using the LA abrasion test. Some recent studies focusing on evaluating the recycled binder availability have listed the agglomerations of the recycled material as the primary inhibitor of availability of recycled binder in asphalt mixtures containing RAP and/or RAS (Pape et al., 2022; Pape and Castorena, 2022). Fig. 2 shows a schematic of clustering phenomenon.

With respect to the rejuvenation mechanism, the understanding of the blending diffusion process is very limited. The literature indicates that this process can occur completely, partially, or not at all, as shown in Fig. 3, depending on a variety of the mixing and design considerations such as mixing time and temperature, binder layer thickness, viscosity and thermal stability of component materials, RA type and dosage, and mix volumetrics (Cong et al., 2016a; Ding et al., 2021; Hussein et al., 2020; Karlsson and Isacsson, 2003; Kriz et al., 2014; Ma et al., 2015; Oliver, 1974; Ravichandran and Castorena, 2022; Wang et al., 2017; Wu et al., 2020; Xu et al., 2018). However, as discussed above, clustering or agglomeration phenomena may occur and lead to no blending at all for some inaccessible RAP leading to failure susceptible zones in the mix (Bressi et al., 2015a, 2015b, 2016; Xu et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2021; Zhu et al., 2020). Molecular dynamics simulations indicate increase in translational mobility of all the SARA fractions due to the addition of RAs, possibly improving the recycling efficiency, and improved interfacial diffusion (Ding et al., 2016b; Ren et al., 2023; G. Xu et al., 2018; Xu and Wang, 2018; M. Xu et al., 2017).

Despite the lack of a consistent understanding of diffusion, it is still believed to be highly related to the ultimate performance of an asphalt mixture containing RA. Rutting is considered to be a critical distress in the case that there is incomplete diffusion of the recycling agent into the aged asphalt binder primarily during the early stages of when the road is opened for traffic (Carpenter and Wolosick, 1980; Tran et al., 2012; Zaumanis and Mallick, 2013). The incomplete diffusion of RA into the binder is believed to result in a higher concentration of the RA in the outer layers of the binder film, which behaves as a soft film causing enhanced movement of aggregates under the traffic movement, thereby leading to permanent deformation. Zaumanis et al. (2015) reported that this incomplete diffusion of the recycling agent could cause both fatigue and rutting distresses as the former can be caused by incomplete diffusion of RA into the RAP causing lower effective binder contents (Al-Qadi et al., 2007; Huang et al., 2005) while the later can be caused by the formation of a softer outer layer as discussed above. In a similar study by Huang et al. (2005), a staged extraction and recovery process showed that while only a small portion of RAP binder participated in the remixing process, the mixture tests indicated improved performance.

Compatibility of the asphalt binder blends is very important because creating a combined asphalt binder system with only a target rheological index in mind may not ensure that the properties of the system are equivalent to that of an unaged system of the same grade. Several researchers have postulated that restoring the chemical compatibility of the binder is important to ensure adequate long-term performance when using reclaimed asphalt materials (Daly et al., 2019; Dave et al., 2019; Elkashef et al., 2019; Garcia Cucalon et al., 2017, 2019; Tabatabaee and Kurth, 2017; Tran et al., 2012). In this literature review, the term ‘chemical compatibility’ refers to interaction between the various solvency phases and other microstructural features of the RA and asphalt binder. Some microstructural features that can arise and may be important include the formation of asphaltene clusters and/or the crystallization of waxes within the Saturates phase of the binder (Daly et al., 2019; Huang et al., 2014; Luo et al., 2021; Prosperi and Bocci, 2021; Sreeram et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2021). One example of how ‘chemical compatibility’ informs the RA discussion includes the observation that binders with poor compatibility have been shown to be prone to oxidative age hardening (Petersen, 2009). Thus, if a binder softens but does not restore compatibility, it may be prone to oxidation induced hardening.

One means of examining chemical compatibility is by using colloidal instability, measured by the Gaestel index (Kleiziene et al., 2019). The Gaestel index (I<sub>c</sub>) can be calculated by using the concentrations of saturates $( S _ { a } ) _ { ; }$ , aromatics $( A _ { r } ) ,$ resins $( R _ { e } ) _ { ; }$ , and asphaltenes $( A _ { s } )$ in Equation (1). As $I _ { c }$ increases, the literature suggests that binder compatibility increases (Cavalli et al., 2019; Kleiziene et al., 2019; Li et al., 2009; Mansourkhaki et al., 2020; Oyekunle, 2007; Siddiqui and Ali, 1999a). The premise for this inference is that having a high amount of resins and asphaltenes relative to asphaltenes and saturates helps prevents the formation of asphaltene clusters and crystallized wax formation (Kleiziene et al., 2019).

I_{c}=\frac{\left(A_{r}+R_{e}\right)}{\left(S_{a}+A_{s}\right)}\times100\%\tag{1}
$$

In this test, the aggregates are simulated using a marble slab consisting of 10 mm diameter indentations where in the blended binder droplets are placed. If the width of the ring is greater than 1.5 mm, the binder blend is considered incompatible. In another study, an exudation droplet test was used to test the compatibility of binder blends with an RA and a petroleum based softening agent at dosage rates from 1 to 20% and it was found that all blends were compatible (Glaser and Porot, 2014).

The automated flocculation titrimeter (AFT) is another means to evaluate binder compatibility (Chandio et al., 2015; Holleran et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2017). A higher $P _ { a }$ indicates the asphaltenes are more peptized and a higher $P _ { 0 }$ indicates higher solvation by the maltenes. Typically, P values fall between 2.5 and 5 (Chandio et al., 2015).

$$
P   =   \frac { P _ { o } } { 1 - P _ { a } }\tag{2}
$$

## 2.3. Recycling agents

## 2.3.1. Classification of recycling agents

There have many attempts to classify RA over the years as their usage has increased. These classification systems have been based on different criteria such as RA source, the RA physical properties, the RA rheological properties, and others. With a greater number of RAs on the market, it is becoming a challenge to define the appropriate selection, differentiation, and classification criteria unless the supplier offers the same information or the relevant testing and analysis is done on the RA (Kaseer et al., 2019b). Currently, four classification systems for RAs have been proposed based on different criteria as shown in Fig. 5.

ASTM D4552 was published in 1999 and is one of the first classification system for petroleum-based RAs (ASTM D4552, 1999). In 2018, an ASTM task force was formed to revise the classification system to include the non-petroleum-based recycling agents. It is important to note that this classification is only used to assess the suitability of RAs for use in hot mix asphalt and does not consider any performance impacts of RAs. Further, this classification system is independent of asphalt or aggregate in question. The revised version ASTM D4552-20 was published in July 2020 with updated thresholds and revised test methods to include all RAs irrespective of their sources (ASTM D4552-20, 2020). It classifies RAs based on their physical property and mainly uses viscosity at $6 0   ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C }$ to classify them. Other relevant properties include saturates content, flash point, specific gravity, and weight change as per ASTM D2872 (ASTM D2872, 2022), as shown in Table 3. This classification system can be used to screen the RAs for safety, handling, and durability purposes to meet the basic requirement to be used in an asphalt plant (Puchalski and Tabatabaee, 2021). In general, it is reported that lesser dosage of organic-based RAs is required to achieve the same effect on viscosity and/or high temperature PG of the recycled binder blend as compared to petroleum-based RAs (Ali and Mohammadafzali, 2015; Kaseer et al., 2020a; Osmari et al., 2017a; Zaumanis et al., 2014c). However, contrary observations have also been reported, although rare (Nabizadeh et al., 2017).

The second most of these systems is the so-called National Center of Asphalt Technology (NCAT) classification system, which differentiates RAs based on their chemical origins (NCAT, 2014). The NCAT classification is shown in Table 4 (NCAT, 2014), where it is seen that the system divides all RAs into three categories including petroleum-based, organic or non-petroleum-based, and emulsion-based, with sub-categories under each type. It is important to note that each main category has very different RAs from different sources even though the chemical nature appears to be similar as shown under the constituents heading in Table 4.

There are some drawbacks to a purely chemical based specification system. For example, it does not directly address or elucidate the mechanism of rejuvenation in each category. Despite these limitations, researchers have used the NCAT classification system as a starting point to develop additional classification systems (ASTM D4552-20; ASTM D4552; Bajaj et al., 2020; Haghshenas et al., 2019, 2018). For example, subsequent research has found out that organic-based oils are more effective than petroleum-based oils (Ali and Mohammadafzali, 2015; Kaseer et al., 2020a; Osmari et al., 2017a; Zaumanis et al., 2014c) and thus, recent NCAT efforts have focused on classifying bio-based RAs more specifically (Puchalski, 2021). In doing $\mathbf { s o } ,$ a fatty acids index has been proposed for classifying RAs as shown in Equation (5). The stability of fatty acids in the RA is related to the degree on unsaturation, however, the limits for the type of fatty acids as well as the degree of unsaturation are yet to be defined (Puchalski, 2021).

$$
\Delta_{fluctuation} = \left[ Standard(\%) + Monosaturated(\%)\right] - Polysaturated(\%)\tag{5}
$$

Nebraska’s classification system for RAs is based on the raw material sources. It defines five classes of RAs as shown in Table 5. This classification is inspired by NCAT’s classification system, but adds an additional layer of information on the effectiveness of RAs based on changes in low-temperature and high-temperature performance grades and cracking resistance (Haghshenas, 2021). It also includes typical dosage levels in each class of RA and provides advisories and cautions when using each RA class. This classification is still in its draft stage and the final version has yet to be published, although several scientific articles and reports have already been produced (Haghshenas et al., 2016, 2018, 2019, 2020, 2021a; Nabizadeh et al., 2017; Nsengiyumva et al., 2020). The authors of this system claim there are four distinct benefits of this system (and its associated guidance).

• the ability to classify based on the RAs impact on both high and low temperature PG,

• the ability to provide typical dosage levels,

• the ability to track improvements in cracking resistance for different aging and temperature conditions, and

• the existence of advisory and cautionary descriptions for each RA.

In this system, paraffinic oil, which lacks polar components, is a softener because even though it has a low $C A _ { g }$ it has poor compatibility and high aging sensitivity. Aromatic extracts, which have polar components that replace some of the maltenes, act as replenishers at higher doses for most binder blend combinations. Finally, vegetable oils, bio-oils, and tall oils are emulsifiers that increase the chemical compatibility by aiding the dispersion of asphaltene agglomerations and result in more effective binder blends. Even though these oils are sensitive to aging, the rheological impact of such aging is minimal (Bajaj et al., 2020; Epps Martin, 2021; Kaseer et al., 2019b). ## 2.3.2. Chemistry of RAs

Most of the commercially available RAs are proprietary, thereby making it difficult to offer a detailed chemical description of their composition. However, the asphaltene/maltene ratio is reported to affect the effectiveness of a RA as discussed in the above sections. Haghshenas et al. (2020) conducted a chemical analysis of the RAs based on the NCAT classification to better understand the underlying mechanisms. They found out that significant chemical differences exist between petroleum-based and bio-based recycling agents. Notably, the FTIR spectra of petroleum-based RAs such as aromatic extracts, naphthenic, and paraffinic oils are very similar to that of unmodified and unaged asphalt binders. Specifically, the researchers found that fractions of the RAs could be separated into Saturate, Resin, and Aromatic components based on their dissolution in different solvents. Also, the oxygen content of these RAs lies within a typical range as that of an unmodified virgin binder. The paraffinic and naphthenic oils had a high proportion of saturates, which may cause a compatibility concern for the corresponding binder blends and mixtures. However, these RAs do not have a high oxygen content or the presence of chemical bonds which would otherwise increase the aging and moisture sensitivity of these binder blends or mixtures. In the case of bio-based oils, hydrogen bonds were detected in tall oils and triglycerides/fatty acids, and the authors argue that these may increase the moisture sensitivity of the corresponding asphalt binder blends and mixtures. Further, these RAs had a high oxygen content indicating long-term performance concerns for the corresponding binder blends and mixtures. The authors also propose using the region around the peak of $1 7 4 0   \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ from the FTIR spectra to detect the presence of triglyceride/fatty acid RAs. ## 2.3.3. Rheology and aging of RAs

The effectiveness of RAs has been reported to decrease as the recycled blended binders and asphalt mixtures oxidize. This effect is speculated to be due to the chemical changes in the RAs themselves, which may reduce the dispersive power of the maltene phase. A recent study reported that bio-based oils show substantial changes after laboratory aging while petroleum-based oils showed no or little change with aging (Xu et al., 2020). The changes that occur in the RA itself due to aging may or may not affects its rejuvenation characteristics.

Xu et al. (2020) evaluated the rheological characteristics of RAs and their aging resistance as part of asphalt binders using dynamic shear rheometer (DSR) and rotational viscosity (RV) measurements. Some RAs such as bio-based oils showed tremendous rheological and chemical changes upon 20- and 40-hr PAV aging, other RAs (paraffinic oils) showed little to no change upon aging. This effect was carried into the recycled binder blends where those blends with different RAs aged in a way that corresponded to the RA aging. The researchers also concluded that the aging resistance of recycled binder blends not only depends on the RA type but also on the dosage level. This finding is still preliminary and current research regularly acknowledges that more studies are needed before a thorough understanding of RA oxidation alone is understood (Abdelaziz et al., 2020; Epps-Martin et al., 2020).

NCHRP Project 09–58 researchers studied the aging characteristics of the RAs alone using DSR measurement of complex viscosity at $1 5 \; ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C }$ and 10 rad/s using 50 mm parallel plate geometry at three aging conditions such as (i) unaged, (ii) RTFO and 20-hr PAV, and (iii) RTFO and 40-hr PAV. An aging index using the ratio of complex viscosity at an aged to the unaged condition was used to evaluate the aging sensitivity. It was found that petroleum-based RAs have a lower aging index, attributed to their chemically stable nature while the bio-based RAs had a higher aging index, attributed to the presence of reactive double bonds in them. FTIR analysis also indicated the higher oxidation for bio-based RAs and the results confirmed the observations from DSR testing. However, the corresponding binder blends did not show similar observations. It was concluded that the presence of natural antioxidants in asphalt binders, such as phenols, might be causing a different behavior of the recycled binder blend than the RAs alone. It should be noted that these antioxidants do not suppress any oxidation mechanisms in the asphalt binder itself, but these may help to protect the reactive double bonds present in bio-based RAs. It was concluded that in order to understand the role of RAs in the recycled binder blends, these should be studied as part of the binder blends rather than alone to capture the actual phenomenon occurring in the recycled binder blends.

## 2.4. Aspects of mixing

## 2.4.1. RA dosage selection

In addition to having adequate metrics to evaluate the performance of binders, a corresponding dosage selection procedure for RAs is essential. Fundamentally, the objective of dosage selection is to identify a level that improves the rejuvenated asphalt mixture’s cracking resistance without compromising its rutting resistance. Currently, the typical practice for this selection is to use the manufacturer’s recommendations (West et al., 2009). This approach is questionable in cases where a single product is combined with different materials (e.g., virgin binder, RAP, RAS) of drastically different properties and concentrations. The compatibility of these products can be an issue as well. Therefore, there is a need for a method to select and/or verify additive dosages that can be used by agencies and contractors based upon the target virgin binder properties and can vary as a function of the type of recycled material included within the mixture (i.e., RAP and/or RAS) and their proportion within the asphalt mixture $\mathbf { i } . \mathbf { e } . ,$ Recycled Binder Ratios (RBR%) (Epps-Martin et al., 2020).

Substantial effort has been dedicated to the development of a robust procedure for RA dosage selection in NCHRP Project 09–58 (Epps-Martin et al., 2020). Researchers considered three means of dosage selection in that project using PG grades and $\varDelta T _ { c }$ (difference of two critical low temperatures).

● Method 1 - restore low-temperature $\mathbf { P G } ,$ verify high-temperature PG,

● Method 2 - achieve $\varDelta T _ { c } = - 5 . 0$ after 20 h of PAV aging, and

● Method 3 - restore high-temperature grade.

Method 1 was found to yield the lowest RA dosages while Method 2 resulted in the highest recycling agent dosages. Method 2 also produced dosages that resulted in high-temperature grades that were too low and could result in rutting issues. Method 3 produced the intermediate dosage and resulted in the highest possible amount of RA without compromising rutting resistance (insomuch as the PG grading criteria are related to rutting resistance and as the RAP materials properties are not changing during production). Though it was not a specific objective of Method 3, it was also found to over restore the low-temperature PG at least to that of the target binder. As a result of these two aspects, the NCHRP Project 09–58 research team has proposed the use of Method 3 in practice.

## 2.4.2. Virgin and recycled materials

Recycled mixtures, by their nature, contain both recycled components (aggregate and binder) and virgin components (aggregate and binder). All four of these components have important roles to play with respect to the blending and long-term performance of mixes with RAs. Of highest importance in the current research is the virgin and recycled binder. Virgin binders are used to rejuvenate the RAP along with the RAs. Up to an RBR of 0.5, virgin binder is the largest component in a recycled binder blend and its properties affect the overall performance of the recycled binder blend as well as the effectiveness of the RAs, both in the short- and long-term. The RAP mixtures, owing to their high degree of oxidation, are stiff and brittle. As such, the use of softer virgin binders is often recommended to reduce the stiffness and improve the workability of the recycled asphalt mixtures (Edith Arambula-Mercado ´ et al., 2018; McDaniel et al., 2000). Thus, virgin binders with lower viscosity are effective at increasing the workability of the recycled asphalt mixtures (Noferini et al., 2017; O’Rear et al., 2020). Similarly, virgin binders with lower high temperature and/or low temperature PG grades have proven useful in reducing the stiffness of the recycled asphalt (Epps-Martin et al., 2020).

Another important aspect of virgin binders is whether they are modified or unmodified. Most studies identified in the literature and cited above, use unmodified virgin binders, and very few have evaluated the effect of binder modification (polymeric, chemical, or otherwise) on the effectiveness of RAs. For instance, Kaseer et al. (2020) found that the impact of polymer modification of virgin binders on the RA dosage selection and its long-term effectiveness was minimal. A similar result was reported by Tran et al. (2017) who studied both the laboratory and field sections of three asphalt mixtures: two with an styrene-butadiene-styrene (SBS)-modified virgin binder and one with an unmodified virgin binder. The three mixes yielded similar intermediate and low temperature cracking resistance without negatively affecting the rutting and moisture damage resistance. The two mixes with SBS-modified virgin binder, one with RA and the other without, showed similar stiffness, which was used to conclude that the effect of RA on stiffness was not statistically different from the third mix which has unmodified virgin binder and RA.

Secondly, the production or mixing temperature is important to ensure diffusion of RA into the recycled materials. A slightly higher production temperature accelerates the rate of diffusion, thereby improving the effectiveness of the RA in rejuvenating the mix. It is recommended to increase the temperature by at least 22 ◦C as compared to the virgin mix to improve the diffusion mechanics of RAs (Zaumanis et al., 2013). However, it should be noted that higher mixing temperatures and longer mixing times can potentially increase aging. Finally, the third important aspect of mixing is the method of incorporation of RAs into the virgin binder and/or the recycled materials, which affects their rejuvenation effectiveness. There are two main methods employed as shown in Fig. 10.

In Method 1, the RA is first mixed with the recycled materials and then virgin binder and aggregates are added, whereas in Method 2, the RA is first added to the virgin binder, and the ‘RA + virgin binder’ blend is added to the recycled materials. Method 1 is considered advantageous over Method 2 because it is believed to allow better diffusion (Kaseer et al., 2019b). Better diffusion is purportedly associated with improved properties and thus better performance of the recycled mix, while incomplete diffusion results in durability issues and worse mixture properties causing poor performance in the long-term. Method 1 is currently followed in Japan while Method 2 is currently followed in US. One of the primary reasons for Method 2 being used in US is the design and production line of the asphalt plants in the US, which restrict RAs to be added to the virgin binders first followed by the addition of the blend to the recycled materials rather than otherwise (West and Copeland, 2015). Ma et al. (2020) studied the impact of these two different blending methods and found out that the later method resulted in lesser aging effect on the binder blends after short term aging, however, the long-term aging effects appeared to be similar for both the blends.

NCHRP Project 09–58 proposed a specific laboratory blending protocol for preparation of recycled binder blends for testing and evaluation using Method 2 (Epps-Martin et al., 2020). The method starts by extracting and recovering the RAP/RAS binders from the parent recycled mixture. Then, both the base binder and recycled binders are pre-heated for 15 min. The RAP binder or RAs may be heated at elevated temperatures from $1 6 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ to $2 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C }$ while the base binder is heated to ensure sufficient fluidity. Next, the RA is added to the base binder and hand mixed using a spatula for 30 s. The blend is put back into oven for 1 min and any RAS binder (if used) is added. If RAS binder is not used, then RAP binder is added at this stage. If RAS binder is used, then the blend is returned to the oven for 1 min before the RAP binder is added. Whenever a recycled material is added to the blend, it is mixed by hand for 30 s using a spatula. Once all components have been added, the blend is heated in the oven for another 1 min and hand stirred with a spatula for 30 s. This final step may be repeated two times to ensure homogeneity in the blend before pouring original samples and RTFO bottles immediately after to avoid additional re-heating. Usually, the prepared binder blends are used for further testing and analysis immediately. However, it is often a practice in the state agency (e.g., DOTs) or research laboratories to store the binders for future binder or mixture testing and analysis, or there may be a lag between sample preparation and testing. In such a case, it is important that the binder blends remain stable over the storage duration. Based on the authors’ review of literature, this important aspect of mixing, that is, the storage of blends containing RAs, has not been researched in the peer reviewed literature at the time of writing this review. The shelf life, storage time, and phase separation of the recycled blends has not been studied yet and it is important that it is considered in the planning stage of a project to ensure the longevity of the binder blends and their validity for use in recycled asphalt pavement projects.

## 3. Performance testing and evaluation

## 3.1. Recycled asphalt binder blends

## 3.1.1. Rutting

Since RAs are added to restore the binder rheology and improve the cracking resistance of the recycled binder blends, there is a threat to the rutting resistance of the resultant mixtures. This concern is considered and should be taken into account through the dosage selection of RAs and the blending protocols therein. Previous research indicates that the rutting potential of recycled binder blends usually decreases with the addition of RAs. This decrease is typically quantified in terms of dynamic shear complex modulus |G\*| (a decrease) and phase angle δ (an increase) (Ali et al., 2016; Karki and Zhou, 2016; Oliveira et al., 2013; Osmari et al., 2017a). In the NCHRP Project 09–58 research, the binder parameter and tests used to assess the rutting resistance were high temperature PG grade after short-term aging as per AASHTO T 315 and AASHTO M 320. In that project, as long as the target high temperature PG grade was achieved according to the above AASHTO specs, the rejuvenated binder blend was assumed to have acceptable rutting resistance at the binder level (Epps-Martin et al., 2020). Several test methods and corresponding parameters have been used to assess the rutting resistance of recycled binder blends as summarized in Table 9.

Ali et al. (2016) found that the high temperature PG grade of recycled binder blends was less than that of the control binder while Zhou et al. (2019) found that the high temp PG grade may be similar between the target and recycled binder blends. It is important to select an appropriate dosage of RA to pass the rutting requirement (Guduru et al., 2021; Zaumanis et al., 2014b). Zahoor et al. (2021) reviewed studies using waste cooking oil as a RA and found out that waste cooking oil may drastically reduce rutting resistance unless polymer modification is done to the recycled binder blends to counter the effect. Mohammadafzali et al. (2015) also used PG grading tests to evaluate the effectiveness of RAs after long term aging and determined the high temperature PG grades of recycled binder blends (two base binders and five recycling agents) after PAV aging of 20 h, 40 h, and 60 h. They found out that the high temperature PG grade for recycled binder blends containing a water-base emulsion RA and an aromatic extract RA were lower than that of original binder while it was higher for petroleum-based RA and bio-oil based RA across all aging levels and base binders in general.

## 3.1.2. Fatigue cracking

In general, the primary testing used to study the fatigue performance of recycled asphalt binder blends is the AASHTO T 315 procedure on PAV aged binder at intermediate temperature using DSR (AASHTO, 2020). These tests determine the fundamental material properties under oscillatory shear such as the norm of the complex shear modulus $( | G ^ { \ast } | )$ and phase angle (δ). The |G\*| quantifies the magnitude of the material’s stress response to strain (or vice versa) and δ quantifies the delay occurring between the stress and strain, i.e., the elastic or viscous nature of the material. A higher $| G ^ { \star } |$ means the response (stress or strain) to an imposed input (strain or stress) is stiffer while a higher δ means the behavior is more viscous (or less elastic). The addition of RAP is often shown to increase |G\*| and decrease δ (Abdelaziz et al., 2021; Ansari et al., 2021; Arabzadeh et al., 2021; Grilli et al., 2017; Yang et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2021) while the addition of RAs is found to work in the opposite direction, i.e., RAs tend to decrease the stiffness and increase the viscous behavior of RAP binder blends (Grilli et al., 2017; Karki and Zhou, 2016; Kaseer et al., 2020b; Oliveira et al., 2013; Osmari et al., 2017a; Yin et al., 2017; Yu et al., 2014).

Ferreira et al. (2021) studied the aging characteristics and the heterogeneities involved therein with RAP binder and found that stiffness of the binder was significantly higher and varied across the RAP layers around the aggregates, indicating heterogeneity within the RAP itself. Ansari et al. (2021) evaluated the performance of recycled asphalt binder blends using master curves and found that the stiffness increases while the phase angle decreases with increasing aging levels. Zhu et al. (2021) studied the recycled binder blend properties after short aging, and found that the high temperature PG grade increased with increasing RAP content from 20% to 40%. Further, this grade was higher after 600 min of RTFO aging than 300 min of RTFO aging across all blends. A similar trend was observed in the |G\*| maser curves constructed using Christensen–Anderson–Marasteanu Model (CAM) at a reference temperature of $2 5 \; ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C } .$ Daryaee et al. (2020) used 30% and 50% RAP binder in conjunction with an RA and waste polymer, and observed that $| G ^ { \dot { \kappa } } |$ sinδ increased from 3,530 kPa to $^ { 5 , 7 7 0 }$ kPa as RAP content increased from 30% to 50% in a waste polymer modified binder blend. However, upon the addition of an RA, |G\*|sinδ decreased to 3,450 kPa, indicating that the RA was effective in restoring the binder blend rheology.

However, in the authors’ experience, the effect of RAs on stiffness and phase angle is not proportionate and appears to be more effective in decreasing the modulus of the binder blends than increasing the phase angle. A similar observation has been made by Singhvi et al. (2021) who noted that the required dosage of RA to meet PG grade appears to be dependent on the modulus change needed and not as equally dependent on the phase angles. The studies that primarily study the effect of rejuvenation on recycled binder blends using modulus and phase angle, use a lumped parameter, such as the Superpave fatigue parameter, which may not be able to capture the impacts of RAs on stiffness and time dependency separately.

A similar approach to studying these properties together is the use of black space diagrams and several indices that use a combination of the parameters. The most widely discussed index being the Glover-Rowe (G-R) parameter, $| G ^ { \star } | \cos ^ { 2 } \delta / \sin \delta ,$ which operates in the black space. The G-R parameter has two thresholds, 180 kPa and 600 kPa, to indicate the onset and significant occurrence of block cracking, respectively. The effect of aging is also captured in the black space diagram as it leads to a higher modulus and a lower phase angle (Glover et al., 2005; Rowe, 2011). The G-R parameter has been extensively used to assess the effectiveness of RAs in improving the non-load related cracking resistance of recycled binder blends along with tracking the rheological changes with aging (Ansari et al., 2021; Edith Arambula-Mercado ´ et al., 2018; Garcia Cucalon et al., 2017; Haghshenas et al., 2021a; Karki and Zhou, 2016; Kaseer et al., 2021; Kaseer et al., 2020a; Yin et al., 2017; R. Zhang et al., 2021; Zhu et al., 2021). NCHRP Project 09–58 extensively used G-R parameter to evaluate the effectiveness of recycled binder blends. Fig. 11 shows a schematic of how, in black space, the G-R parameter is used to capture the effects of recycling agents at improving improve the cracking resistance of recycled binder blends.

Haghshenas et al. (2021b) studied the cracking and moisture damage characteristics of engineering recycled binder blends (five different Ras) under different aging levels. They used the G-R parameter to compute the aging time to induce the onset of cracking and found that binders containing paraffinic and naphthenic oil Ras do not satisfy the requirements as their lines interest the cracking onset lines before 40 h of PAV aging, specifically at 38 h and 39 h of aging, respectively. However, the other three binders with aromatic extracts, triglycerides/fatty acids, and tall oil Ras pass the 40 h criterion with aging times to onset of cracking at 55 $\mathbf { h } ,   4 3   \mathbf { h } ,$ and 49 h, respectively. Of all the Ras, aromatic extracts were found to be most beneficial and effective.

Kaseer et al. (2021) used these indices along with other binder and mixture indices to evaluate the relationship between binder and mixture cracking behaviors. A similar study was done by Zhang et al. (2021). Both found that these indices provide useful insights about the binder cracking performance and relate to the mixture cracking performance.

In addition to the above indices, Singhvi et al. (2021) added more crossover indices such as crossover shear modulus $( | G ^ { \star } | _ { c } )$ and crossover reduced frequency (ω<sub>c</sub>) at 10 rad/s in their assessment of recycled binder blend cracking. Another parameter used was ‘Black Angle’, which is arguably an indicator of predominant mechanical response of asphalt binders at intermediate temperature (Singhvi et al., 2021). This indicator is inspired by a good correlation of phase angle with cracking (Pauli et al., 2018) as well as from the NCHRP Project 09–59, which found an inverse proportionality between the fatigue exponent and phase angle at 10 rad/s (Christensen and Tran, 2021). The angle is measured at equal stiffness condition corresponding to the $| G ^ { \star } |$ of a typical binder passing Superpave intermediate temperature criteria (Kriz et al., 2019). Singhvi et al. (2021) also referred to the phase angle at the $| G ^ { \star } |$ of 8,967 kPa as the black angle and showed that it had a strong correlation with VETT (Singhvi et al., 2021). They found out that all these parameters yielded good correlations and were able to assess the recycled binder blend cracking performance. However, there were some inconsistencies in ranking which were attributed to the threshold values obtained from the literature and the stiffness dependence of some parameters. More importantly, all parameters exhibited a clear trend with increasing age level.

Zhang et al. (2021) defined two more transition temperatures based on test results obtained using 4 mm parallel plate DSR testing, including the glass transition temperature $( T _ { g } ) _ { 1 }$ , which is identified as the temperature in the glassy transition region at which the loss modulus starts to drop and intermediate region temperature range (ΔT<sub>IR</sub>) difference between $T _ { \delta = 4 5 ^ { \circ } }$ ◦ and $T _ { g }$ indicating the total span of transition region. They found a good correlation between the binder parameters but all the binder parameters in general correlated well with each other in the context of various aging conditions, which they concluded highlights their possible use in assessing cracking performance of asphalt binder blends. Finally, the Linear Amplitude Sweep (LAS) test, which has been an important approach for the characterization of fatigue resistance at intermediate temperatures of PAV aged binders, has only recently been used for evaluating recycled binder blends. The NCHRP Project 09–58 researchers recognized the need for improved characterization of intermediate temperature cracking resistance, especially in case of polymer-modified asphalt binders. Several new indices have been proposed based on the LAS test results to evaluate the fatigue (and healing) performance of the binders and consequently evaluate the effectiveness of recycled binder blends based with respect to cracking resistance. Table 11 gives a summary of the LAS test-based indices. Asadi et al. (2021b) proposed a cohesive healing indicator based on LAS test and used it to compare the healing potential of unaged, aged, rejuvenated, and reaged-after-rejuvenation binders. They generated healing master curves as a function of reduced rest period and compared across the four binders. They found the following order for their healing potential: unaged > rejuvenated > reaged-after-rejuvenation > aged. This result indicates that the RA not only restores the healing potential of the aged binder, but also reduces its sensitivity to further aging. In another study by Mainieri et al. (2021), a more direct measure of cracking has been proposed as an alternate interpretation of LAS test results. The proposed parameter, $\Delta | \mathbf { G } ^ { \& } | _ { \mathrm { p e a k } }$ τ quantifies the reduction in complex shear modulus at the peak shear stress and is used to gauge the performance of different recycled binder blends based on their fatigue and damage performance characteristics (Singhvi et al., 2021).

## 3.1.3. Thermal cracking

Thermal cracking potential has been most typically evaluated using the Bending Beam Rheometer (BBR); however, other tests have also been found from the literature. Two parameters from BBR test are used to characterize the modulus and relaxation characteristics of the binder blends, creep stiffness at 60 s, S(60), and log-log slope of relaxation at 60 s, m(60). This test method is used to assess the low temperature PG grade as per AASHTO T 313 and M 320. When recycled materials are added to asphalt binder S(60) and m(60) increase. With respect to RAs, it is generally reported that the addition of RAs to recycled binder blends decreases S and increases m-value (Epps-Martin et al., 2020). Further, another parameter obtained from BBR test is the difference in critical temperature between S(60) and m(60), $\Delta T _ { c ^ { \prime } }$ . The $\varDelta T _ { c } ,$ has been given considerable attention in recent years for capturing the effects of embrittlement on pavement cracking potential. The parameter was first introduced by Anderson et al. (2011) who observed a strong correlation between $\varDelta T _ { c }$ and pavement block cracking; correspondingly, the authors postulated that $\varDelta T _ { c }$ is a good indicator of asphalt binder ductility at low temperature. They found that the value of $\varDelta T _ { c }$ decreases with oxidative aging and thus, trends with aging level may be helpful to identifying binders susceptible to oxidative embrittlement. Subsequently, researchers have found that the parameter can be used to identify problems associated with REOB, with values falling below $- 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C }$ indicating concern (Asphalt Institute, 2016). The parameter could be implemented into practice relatively easily because it can be calculated using the results of standard BBR testing used for PG determination.

A survey distributed to a task force of 14 members from different state agencies was used to gain perspective on agencies’ opinions and use of (ΔT<sub>c</sub>). The majority agreed it is an indicator of age-related cracking. However, the task force was more split on its use as an effective specification parameter. Two of the 14 respondents did not find enough data to support using $\varDelta T _ { c }$ as a binder parameter while the majority conditionally supported it due to the fact that it is needed to evaluate the quality of asphalt binders (Asphalt Institute, 2019). Of the 12 members that support using $\varDelta T _ { \infty }$ ten members have a current specification, indicated in Table 12 (Asphalt Institute, 2019).

They also recommended using BBR test and $\Delta T _ { c }$ as a parameter to evaluate the low temperature performance of recycled binder blends after short- and long-term aging. In this case, the $\Delta T _ { c }$ threshold of $\geq - 5 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for recycled binder blends. They concluded that adequate performance of recycled binder blends can be controlled by the thresholds of ΔT<sub>c</sub> in addition to high temperature PG grade and G-R parameter. These thresholds have been proposed as part of the draft AASHTO standard practice for the characterization of recycled asphalt mixtures in Appendix I of NCHRP 09–58 final report (Epps-Martin et al., 2020).

In the authors’ opinion, the true effectiveness of RAs can be known by looking at the material properties directly instead of surrogates that combine multiple parameters. This can be a useful and potential area of further research in recycled binder testing in assessing the effectiveness of RAs. In a study by Preciado et al. (2023a), a suite of recycle binder blends were analyzed using multivariate statistical analysis to assess the similarity of the RA-modified systems with respect to the rheological properties of Virginia’s and North Carolina’s virgin binders. The study indicated that the effectiveness of an RA, defined therein as the rate of change of a rheological parameter with respect to RA dosage, is not universal and depends on the rheological property examined. The multivariate analysis suggested that RAs can yield combination of properties in a recycled system outside the typical pattern of rheological properties for a binder system independently of the RA dosage.

Preciado et al. (2023b) also proposed a framework to evaluate RAs based on low-temperature rheological performance. In their work, the authors proposed a black space-based parameter, $S R _ { D } ,$ that provides information about the creep stiffness-relaxation balance that can be achieved upon rejuvenation of a recycled system. It was found that the $S R _ { D }$ parameter is similar for blends produced with the same RA (excluding paraffinic oils), suggesting that the $S R _ { D }$ can be potentially used a product-dependent parameter to inform the RA’s acceptance process.

## 3.1.4. Long-term aging and changes in chemical composition

Current Superpave PG specifications (AASHTO M 320 and AASHTO M 332) simulate short- and long-term aging of asphalt binder using the RTFO and PAV, respectively. Recent studies show that the current 20-hr PAV procedure provides insufficient oxidation to mimic the effects of prolonged field aging (Glover et al., 2005; Wright et al., 2011). Standard PAV aging has also been found insufficient to capture the negative effects of additives. Recent research suggests that binder test results from 40-hrs of PAV conditioning is a better means of gauging field performance after five or more years in service and also better captures modified binder embrittlement than standard 20-h PAV aging (Asphalt Institute, 2016; Bennert et al., 2016; Reinke et al., 2017). Concerns have been raised regarding the additional time burden of the extended PAV aging procedure. However, a reduction in the binder film thickness within the PAV and/or increasing the aging temperature can be used to alleviate this concern, which was the focus of NCHRP Project 09–61. NCHRP Project 09–61 refined short- and long-term laboratory aging procedures for asphalt binders. There the researchers are proposing no changes to the RTFO, but they are proposing several changes to the PAV. The refined long-term aging procedure will consist of a PAV procedure, retaining the standard air pressure of 2.1 MPa and 20 h duration but decreasing the binder film thickness by using 12.5 g per pan rather than 50 g per pan and using elevated temperature compared to the current procedure. The aging temperatures are currently being calibrated as a function of climatic conditions to reflect 12 years of field aging using 26 binders. The revised procedure is anticipated to induce more aging compared to the current PAV procedure, which was developed to reflect five to seven years of in-service aging (Bonaquist et al., 2021). However, there are some practical issues with the NCHRP Project 09–61 aging procedure such the creating a uniform film thickness, the fact that the procedure produces less total aged binder per run of the PAV, and has additional equipment requirements such as a nitrogen oven, etc. On the other hand, PAV 40 h aging procedure produces statistically similar results are NCHRP Project 09–61 procedure and is more efficient in terms of time and material aged.

In terms of long term aging of recycled binder blends, substantial attention has been given to binders that contain re-refined engine oil bottoms (REOB) because they contribute to the premature cracking of pavements as a result of embrittlement (Asphalt Institute, 2016). Furthermore, it has been cautioned that the aging susceptibility of bio-materials has not been well-studied (Daly, 2017) and preliminary evaluation indicates potential concerns. Kaseer et al. (2019b) reported that numerous studies have reported a loss of recycling agent effectiveness with long-term aging, the extent of which depends upon the type and dose of the recycling agent. NCHRP Project 09–58 researchers studied the long-term aging of recycled binder blends by examining the different chemical peaks caused by Sulfoxide and Carbonyl using Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscope (FTIR) for PAV 20-hr and PAV 40-hr aging conditions. The Carbonyl Area (CA) was calculated as the area under the FTIR spectrum between 1650 $\mathbf { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ to $1 8 2 0 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ and its growth rate $( C A _ { g } )$ was tracked as aging level increased. DSR testing to get the master curves and its related parameters as discussed in Section 3.1.2 were used to evaluate the effectiveness of recycling agents initially and with aging. Two asphalt binders, three recycled materials, and two RAs were taken for this study and the RA dosage was selected to restore the low temperature PG grade of the target binder. All the binder blends containing RAs showed improved performance after short-term (RTFO) aging based on the G-R parameter at $1 5   ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C }$ and 0.005 rad/s. However, all the blends exceeded the cracking onset threshold after 20-hr PAV aging and exhibited similar performance as that of a worst-performing base asphalt binder. ## 3.2. Recycled asphalt mixtures

## 3.2.1. Stiffness

The stiffness of asphalt mixtures is an important property used for the mechanistic-empirical design of asphalt pavement structures. Historically, stiffness characterization of Hot Mix Asphalt (HMA) was defined by the use of the resilient modulus, $\mathrm { , } M _ { R }$ (ASTM D7369-20, 2020), and more recently the dynamic modulus, |E\*| (AASHTP T 342, 2019; Loulizi et al., 2006). When RAP is included in asphalt mixture, the conventional wisdom is that the stiffness of HMA will increase and lead to higher cracking potential (Baek et al., 2012). Therefore, several research studies have been conducted on the use of RA to mitigate this effect. The general trend from these studies is that RAs promote softening of recycled asphalt mixtures and reduce the mixture stiffness that occurs because the RAP is incorporated (Bonicelli et al., 2017; W. S. Mogawer et al., 2013; Munoz et al., 2015; Pradhan and Sahoo, 2021; Rodrí- guez-Fernandez ´ et al., 2019; Tran et al., 2012; Vackova et al., 2022; Wrobel ´ et al., 2021; Yan et al., 2021; J. Zhang et al., 2020). However, a balance must be made because excessive softening of recycled HMA can lead to pavement rutting (Espinoza-Luque et al., 2018). ## 3.2.2. Rutting

In the context of high RBR mixtures and RAs, a substantial amount of attention has been given to how cracking resistance of the mixtures is affected. However, rutting behaviors are equally important for ensuring long-term performance of asphalt pavements. In NCHRP Project 09–58, rutting was evaluated using the Hamburg Wheel Track Test (HWTT) and the Asphalt Pavement Analyzer (APA). For these experiments, an aromatic extract, reacted bio-based oils, a paraffinic oil, tall oils and modified vegetable oils were evaluated. Also, for the experiments, RA doses were selected to restore the continuous high temperature PG grade. Results showed that the bio-oil- and tall oil-based RAs exhibited superior rutting performance, followed by vegetable oil- and aromaticbased RAs, which had a similar performance, and finally by the paraffinic oil-based RA. In general, findings from HWTT and APA test results of the NCHRP Project 09–58 indicate that the approach of selecting RA doses to restore continuous climatic high temperature grade does not excessively increase the rutting potential of the asphalt mixtures $\mathrm { ( E p p - }$ s-Martin et al., 2020; Fried et al., 2022).

Recent studies by Zhang et al. (J. Zhang et al., 2021) using the Wheel Track Test (WTT) showed that recycled mixtures with an aromatic oil, a plasticizer, an anti-stripping agent, and a soft binder can achieve higher rutting resistance compared to asphalt mixtures without RAP. Similar findings are reported by Moniri et al. (2021) and Zhang and Bahia (2021) using oil-based recycling agents. However, opposite results are found by Wrobel ´ et al. (2021) using organic rejuvenators at a dose of 2% by weight of asphalt binder. Aguirre et al. (2017) conducted a study to evaluate the effect of four commercial RAs on the rutting performance of HMA containing recycled asphalt shingles. As found by Wrobel ´ et al.

(2021), a detrimental effect in permanent deformation resistance was observed for all mixtures containing RAs. Additionally, many other tests and indices are used by researchers to evaluate the rutting performance of recycled asphalt mixtures, summarized in Table 16.

Meroni et al. (2021) studied the performance characteristics of four asphalt mixes, two of which had 45% RAP material by weight, with one of them having a bio-based RA. For rutting evaluation, the High Temperature Indirect Tensile (HT-IDT) strength test, APA, flow number test, and Stress Sweep Rutting (SSR) test were used. They found out that the mix with rejuvenator performed better than the mix with no rejuvenator for HT-IDT test. Interestingly in terms of the rut depth from APA test, the recycled asphalt mix with rejuvenator had a smaller rut depth as compared to the one without a rejuvenator. It must be noted that the former has a slightly lower optimum asphalt binder (by 0.4–0.5%) content than the latter mix. In terms of flow number, none of tested mixes reached the flow point under the testing conditions; however, the mix without a rejuvenator continued to be the worst performer. Finally, in terms of Rutting Strain Index (RSI) from the SSR test, the mix with rejuvenator had a lower RSI (better performing) than the mixture without rejuvenator.

On the other hand, Zhou et al. (2021) evaluated 23 asphalt mixes for rutting and cracking performance. Two of these mixes contained RA at different dosages and 25% RAP by weight of total mix. They used Rutting Tolerance Index $( R T _ { i n d e x } )$ from IDEAL Rutting Test (IDEAL-RT) and a new Rutting Resistance Index (RRI) from HWTT to evaluate the rutting potential of each mixture. In contrast to the findings of Meroni et al. (2021), they found out that two RA mixes have a low RRI (7.4 and 7.3), which means they perform worse, as well as a low $R T _ { i n d e x }$ (53.4 and 43.1). These values failed to satisfy the minimum acceptance RRI criterion (8) and the minimum $\mathrm { R T _ { i n d e x } }$ criterion (60) for PG64-XX mixes. A similar observation was made by Zhang et al. (2021) using new measures from HWTT.

As mentioned before, the effect of RAs depends on a multiple factors such as the amount of RA incorporated, the effectiveness of the method to determine optimal RA content, and the inherent characteristics of the parent mixture that they are being used in (Kaseer et al., 2019b). Another important factor that can affect the mixture performance characteristics is if the asphalt content remains the same, increases, or decreases with the addition of an RA. Though not explicitly evaluated in the literature, the substantial variation in conclusions across researchers may also suggest that laboratory handling (e.g., specimen preparation) and testing procedures have a role to play as well.

## 3.2.3. Fatigue cracking

The fatigue cracking of recycled asphalt mixtures has been reported to improve upon the addition of RAs (Espinoza-Luque et al., 2018; Im et al., 2014; W. Mogawer et al., 2013; Mogawer et al., 2016, 2015; Yan et al., 2014; Yin et al., 2017). Based on the studies identified, the factors affecting the intermediate cracking performance of recycled asphalt mixtures include RA type, but more importantly the dosage level plays a vital role. Further, the effectiveness of the RA in improving the fatigue resistance is a function of aging level (Epps-Martin et al., 2020). Several studies have reported that the effectiveness of RAs decreases with long-term aging (Edith Arambula-Mercado ´ et al., 2018; Yin et al., 2017). NCHRP Project 09–58 researchers showed that the long-term effectiveness of RAs can be ensured by the dosage selection method, which in their case was chosen to match the continuous high temperature PG of the binder blends based on climate and traffic requirements (Epps-Martin et al., 2020). Table 17 summarizes the metrics from a variety of tests that have been used to evaluate the fatigue performance of recycled asphalt mixtures.

In order to track the effect of recycling agents on cracking performance and how it changes with aging, NCHRP Project 09–58 researchers used a mixture black space and a corresponding mixture G-R parameter $( G { \cdot } R _ { m } ) _ { : }$ along with other metrics to track the magnitude and direction of change in mixture rheological properties with aging.

Further, this improvement in fatigue life was more pronounced at 500με than 700με. A similar observation was made by the authors in another study employing both the warm mix additives and recycling agent in recycled asphalt mixtures (Mirhosseini et al., 2020).

## 3.2.4. Thermal cracking

The addition of RAP increases the modulus and brittleness of asphalt mixtures and subsequently increases the susceptibility of the mixture to low temperature cracking. The addition of RAs is reported to lower the low temperature stiffness characteristics and improve the relaxation properties of recycled asphalt mixtures (H. Chen et al., 2021; Shen et al.,

2007b; Song et al., 2021; Tran et al., 2012; WIELINSKI et al., 2017; Wu et al., 2021; Yan et al., 2014; Zaumanis et al., 2013), thus there is likely a positive effect from RA addition on thermal cracking potential. Several tests and metrics have been used to assess the low temperature cracking of recycled asphalt mixtures as summarized in Table 18. The test temperature for the metrics reported in Table 18 are not included to allow the readers select the temperature that best suits their local practices and research purposes based on the respective requirements and guidelines. Traditionally, the Thermal Stress Restrained Specimen Tensile Strength Test (TSRST) has been used to assess the low temperature cracking of recycled asphalt mixtures. Several studies have found that the addition of RAs improves low temperature properties but the extent depends on the RA type (Hajj et al., 2013; W. S. Mogawer et al., 2013; Shen et al., 2004). However, Cooper et al. (2015) found that RAs may worsen the low temperature performance if recycled asphalt shingles are used.

NCHRP Project 09–58 researchers used the Uniaxial Thermal Stress and Strain test (UTSST) to evaluate the thermal cracking resistance of recycled asphalt mixtures. They found that increased aging or the addition of RAP to an asphalt mixture shortened the viscous softening plateau and shifted the crack initiation and fracture temperatures to warmer values. The addition of RAs resulted in the opposite trend, but the recycled asphalt mixtures with high RBR (>0.3) did not meet the long-term aging thresholds and showed inadequate low temperature cracking. A higher dosage of RA ensured sufficient low temperature cracking resistance after long term aging. The NCHRP Project 09–58 researchers also built upon the work of Romero (2016) and used BBR test for assessing recycled asphalt mixtures. They found out that an adequate dosage of RAs was able to produce recycled asphalt mixtures that had similar or lower creep stiffness and higher or similar relaxation as compared to the control mixtures (Epps-Martin et al., 2020).

Boz and Solaimanian (2018) used an impact resonance test and its measures to assess the low temperature cracking of recycled asphalt mixtures and concluded that addition of RAs lowers the low temperature stiffness while improving the relaxation properties, which agreed with the findings of NCHRP Project 09–58 (Epps-Martin et al., 2020). Jahangiri et al. (2019) used Disk-shaped Compact Tension test (DC(T)) to study the low temperature performance characteristics of recycled asphalt mixtures and found that a grade bumping to one or two lower grades in addition to recycling agents or other additives might be useful to ensure acceptable thermal performance. Tran et al. (2012) used an Indirect Tension test (IDT) at low-temperatures to determine critical failure temperature for the tested mixtures and found that addition of recycling agent resulted in a lower critical failure temperature than those without a recycling agent. ## 3.2.5. Moisture damage

It has been shown that the molecular composition of the asphaltbinder as well as the aggregate chemistry are key factors in the loss of adhesive bonding of the asphalt-binder and aggregate interface (Cala and Caro, 2021; Caro et al., 2008; Kim et al., 2008). Therefore, the growing use of RAs in asphalt mixtures presents an opportunity to improve moisture damage resistance by incorporating adequate components that enhance the interfacial bonding (typically found in anti-stripping agents) within the formulations of RA (E. H. Fini et al., 2020). NCHRP Project 09–58 researchers admitted the difficulty of separating rutting resistance from moisture susceptibility in the HWTT and noted that some recycled asphalt mixtures failed the HWTT criterion; however, upon additional dry HWTT testing, the same mixtures showed adequate rutting resistance. They pointed out that the addition of RAs may ensure adequate rutting resistance, but moisture susceptibility may still be an issue and further research was needed (Epps-Martin et al., 2020). A recent study by Zhang et al. (2021) indicated that rejuvenated blends had a higher stripping potential than virgin blends while the reverse was true for rutting potential. The authors used HWTT test data and prosed four different indices to assess the rutting and stripping damage mechanisms.

Moisture damage of asphalt mixtures (with and without RAs) has been studied in several different ways as summarized in Table 19. Tran et al. (2017) evaluated the moisture susceptibility of two recycled asphalt mixtures containing the RA Hydrogreen: 1) 40% RAP and SBS-modified PG 70-22 with 0.75% of RA and 2) 25% RAP,5% RAS, and PG 64-22 with 1.33% of RA, using HWTT and TSR. HWTT results indicated that both mixtures containing RA passed the Texas Department of Transportation’s (TxDOT) rut criteria and met the minimum TSR criteria of 80%. Others have reached similar conclusions with the HWTT using a variety of RAs, dosage selection procedures, and raw materials (Aguirre et al., 2017; Cooper et al., 2017; Im et al., 2014; Sabhafer and Hossain, 2017).

Cao et al. (2021) also observed that RAs improve the moisture resistance of recycled asphalt mixtures using surface free energy method and water boiling test. As with the other performance measures described above; however, these findings are not universal. Zhang and Bahia (2021) used a modified HWTT and concluded that the addition of RAs may significantly reduce the moisture damage resistance and the relative effect is a function of the type and composition of the RA. ## 3.2.6. Activity, availability, and blending studies

As discussed in Section 2.4.1, the degree of blending between the recycled binder and virgin binder affects the overall performance of the recycled asphalt mixtures, the extent of which depends on several factors such material properties of RAP, virgin binder, aggregates, mixing parameters such time, temperature, etc. The use of RAs typically should enhance the degree of blending and allow more binder contribution from RAP in the recycled asphalt mixtures. Thus, two quantitative measures have been proposed to quantify the amount of binder available from RAP and the positive impacts of RA on this availability. This behavior has been termed by Lo Presti et al. (2020) as Degree of binder Activity (DoA) and Degree of binder Availability (DoAv) as shown in Equations (13) and (14) respectively.

Theoretically, the addition of RA will not affect the amount of active binder from RAP; however, it will affect the available binder from RAP and the extent of available binder will affect the performance properties of recycled binder blends as well as recycled asphalt mixtures. So, the Degree of Blending (DoB) has been defined as an indicator of the extent to which the aged RAP binder contributes to the final properties of recycled binder blend comprising of virgin binder, RAP binder and recycling agent (Lo Presti et al., 2020). NCHRP Project 09–58 researchers proposed a framework to calculate the recycled binder availability using a so-called size-exclusion method. The framework consists of preparing two loose mixtures, with and without RAP. The virgin mixture is prepared with virgin binder and virgin aggregates of only three sizes, coarse, intermediate, and fine, while the RAP mixture is prepared with virgin binder (adjusted to match the same total binder content) and virgin aggregates for coarse and fine size, and RAP material for intermediate size. Both mixtures are short-term aged and sieved again to fraction out the three sizes. The fractions for intermediate size from both the virgin and RAP mixes are used to calculate the binder content using the ignition oven. NCHRP Project 09–58 researchers assumed the RAP binder availability for the virgin mix as zero percent and a 100 percent for the RAP mix, and using the interpolation between the two extremes calculated a Binder Availability Factor (BAF) (Kaseer et al., 2019a). In order to further the use of this method for practical purposes, they proposed a set of equations based on high temperature PG grade of extracted and recovered RAP binder as well as the mixing temperature as given in Equation (18).

$$
RAPBAF = m \times PGH_{RAP} + b\tag{19}
$$

where;

m = the slope (equals − 0.014 and − 0.010 for $1 4 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ and $1 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C } ,$ respectively) and

$$
\begin{aligned} &\mathsf{b} = \mathsf{the intercept} (\mathsf{equals} 1.898 \mathsf{and} 1.771 \mathsf{for} 140 \mathsf{^{\circ}C} \mathsf{and} 150 \mathsf{^{\circ}C},\\ &\mathsf{respectively}) (\mathsf{Epps-Marrin et al., 2020}).\\ \end{aligned}
$$

Many other methods have been used to estimate the blending parameters (DoA, DoAv, and DoB), but there is not yet a standardized test yet. In a recent review of literature on blending between RAP binder, virgin binder, and RAs by Orešković et al. (2020), these methods were classified under four categories with several tests and methods under each category as summarized in Table 20. It is also noted that as of this writing, there is a forthcoming National Cooperative Highway Research Program effort (NCHRP Project 09–68) on this issue. In a recent study by Alvis et al. (2023), the role of asphalt mixture constituents on the recycled binder contribution was investigated using Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy tracer based microscopy analysis. It was found that type and source of virgin binder and recycled materials greatly affect the proportion of recycled binder that blends with virgin binder. More work is needed to understand the dynamics of recycled binder within the asphalt mixture matrix and how much of it can be considered as part of virgin binder during the mix design process.

Pape and Castorena (2021) recently proposed a more robust sample preparation and fabrication procedure to evaluate the binder blending using energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). Pape and Castorena (2022) have also developed what they believe to be a more practical and easily implementable test method and analysis procedure. The underlying assumption used in the study is that RAP binder that is unavailable for blending is held in the agglomerations of adhered RAP particles in recycled asphalt mixtures. Their proposed method involves combining a sieving analysis with an ignition oven test to estimate binder availability and uses an experimentally validated microstructural investigations of mastic film thickness in asphalt mixtures by Underwood and Kim (2013). The method involves the gradation of RAP, RAP aggregates, RAP aggregate specific gravity, and RAP binder content. This eliminates any need for binder testing of RAP binder as required by NCHRP Project 09–58 test procedure (Epps-Martin et al., 2020), thus eliminating need for any extraction and recovery. The results from this study align well with the tracer-based study microscopic investigations (Pape and Castorena, 2021). The proportion of recycled binder that blends with virgin binder was found to be dependent on the virgin binder, type of recycled material, and source, while age level and content of recycled material and additives did not have any significant impacts on recycled binder availability (Alvis et al., 2023). Some studies recommend studying binder availability using ignition oven method especially for in-place hot in-place mix recycling (Ma et al., 2021, 2022a, 2022b).

## 4. Knowledge gaps and Scope for future work

There have been many efforts to study the positive reuse of asphalt mixture. Relatively recently, the use of RAs has become a widely available tool for engineers to further increase the use of recycled materials and still ensure good performing pavements. NCHRP Project 09–58 served as an experimentally rigorous effort to advance and consolidate the existing knowledge on the use of RAs. This review has focused on summarizing not only the major outcomes of NCHRP Project 09–58 but also the progress that has been made since that project ended. In these intervening years, the number of papers published has increased significantly; however, the proportion of new knowledge to further our understanding of underlying mechanisms has not improved significantly. Very recently some advances have been made to propose novel methods for understanding the chemical nature of RAs, how RAP interacts with the virgin binder and recycling agent, and how acceptable performance of recycled asphalt binder blends and mixtures can be achieved through dosage selection. However, many questions still need to be answered.

• First and foremost, a robust classification system that is blind to the origin or source of RAs does not exist. The closest method to this goal that currently exists is the Texas A&M University’s classification system; however, it is purely based on rejuvenation mechanism (three in this case), which is an oversimplified version of complex interaction of virgin binder, RAP binder, and RA. A chemical-, rheological-, and microstructural classification system that focuses on dissecting the mechanisms at play would allow better selection and screening of RAs.

• The dosage selection methods focus on one property at a time while ensuring acceptable performance at other temperatures or conditions. A unified dosage selection method that ensures optimal performance across all conditions such as low-, intermediate-, and hightemperature conditions as well as chemical compatibility does not exist yet.

• There have been huge efforts to study the impact of RAs on performance properties of recycling asphalt binder blends and mixtures through systemic studies, change of multiple variables, mixing or testing conditions, use of additional additives, etc. Although cross-verification and repeatability of studies is essential and a part of scientific process, most studies have not added to the collective knowledge since NCHRP Project 09–58. There is a need to perform a meta-analysis of these studies and identify the critical aspects on the use of RAs. Some possible factors that can lead to the variation or difference in conclusions across various studies is how RAs are considered in selecting the binder content in the mixture, that is, is the RA considered binder in the mix design or is the mix design redone to optimize binder content with RA, etc.

• Very little modeling efforts have been made to capture the effect of RAs on rheology and performance of both recycled binder blends and mixtures. Also, studies focusing on the blend of RAP and RAs only or virgin binder and RAs only to understand the underlying mechanisms at work and the impact of RAs on different performance indicators can be helpful in this regard. These studies should include tests in addition to the traditional testing such as FTIR, GPC, MFT, etc. More work is needed in this direction.

• The cost of using RAs will depend on the type and amount of RA that is being used. Therefore, cost consideration in the selection and screening of RAs can be of prime importance. However, very few studies have proposed any methodology to consider the cost of RAs in their selection or evaluation for use in recycled pavements.

• Since most of the RAs are chemically active, it is essential to study the environmental impacts of recycling agents both in the short-term and long-term. Given the lack of good long-term performance data, chemical proprietorship of RAs, difference in dosage selection methods, etc., the assessment of long-term impacts in terms of life cycle assessment (LCA) and life cycle cost assessment (LCCA) of recycling agents is a challenging task. However, it is useful to start with the existing literature on recycling agents while making plausible assumptions to kick-start LCA/LCCA of these systems. Further, these RAs are or will be used during production, mixing, and compaction on a large scale, so the nature of emissions out of recycled asphalt mixtures employing RAs needs to be explored. Some RAs may contain volatile organic compounds (VOCs), which can contribute to air pollution and have adverse health effects. Therefore, it is essential to select RAs that have low VOC content and meet the required environmental standards. Another concern is the potential for RAP to contain contaminants such as heavy metals or asbestos, which could pose health and safety risks to workers and the public, and it may exacerbate with the addition of RAs. Thus, given the complexity and complicatedness of the phenomenon, more research is warranted to study the environmental impacts of RAs, both in the short-term and long-term.

• With increasing use of RAs in the recycling asphalt pavements, the recyclability of the recycled asphalt materials containing RAs for future use, needs to be studied and accounted for in life-cycle assessment (LCA) and life-cycle cost analysis (LCCA). In other words, how sustainable and recyclable is the sustainable use of RAs in recycling asphalt pavements.

In addition to these knowledge gaps, the review of the literature has found that there are no clear, definitive, and universally accepted metrics to evaluate the effectiveness of RAs other than those obtained from dosage selection methods. In practice, the contractors propose the RA dosage, and the DOT needs to evaluate if a particular dosage will ensure long term performance. So, methods, metrics, and frameworks that allow screening and selection of RAs based on their effectiveness in recycled asphalt binder blends and mixtures are needed. Also, the literature review has found that while there do exist some general trends with respect to RA effectiveness, the specific benefits and limitations found in individual studies vary greatly. Thus, given the current state of knowledge, RAs should be evaluated on a material-by-material basis using asphalt mixtures designed and delivered using local practices. One such way would be to evaluate the effectiveness of the RAs for the RAP contents used locally and try to come up with an approved product list or benchmarking indices that allow the local use of RAs ensuring satisfactory performance, both in the short- and long-term (Fried et al., 2022; Habbouche et al., 2022; Preciado et al., 2023a, 2023b).
