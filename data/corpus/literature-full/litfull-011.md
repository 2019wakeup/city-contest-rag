# Ultraviolet ageing of bituminous materials: A comprehensive literature review from 2011 to 2022

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Asphalt pavements Bituminous materials Photo-oxidative ageing Ultraviolet radiation

## A B S T R A C T

The transport infrastructure is crucial to maintaining the interconnection of urban, rural, and commercial centres, thus allowing the socioeconomic growth of populations and communities. Nevertheless, due to the ageing phenomenon, the useful life of asphalt pavements is significantly reduced. Therefore, a greater consumption of financial resources is necessary to carry out maintenance and rehabilitation activities that guarantee the functionality of the pavement structures. Accordingly, it is essential to understand the complexity of the ageing of bituminous materials to deal with this problem. Unfortunately, although there is extensive reference literature on thermal ageing, relatively few research efforts have been carried out to understand ultraviolet (UV) ageing. The preceding is of particular concern because UV ageing is one of the predominant ageing processes during the inservice time of these materials. In this way, the present investigation intends to perform an exhaustive review of state of the art to establish the level of scientific development, the knowledge gaps and the aspects that are still necessary to explore more in-depth regarding the UV ageing of bituminous materials. Consequently, throughout this manuscript, the UV ageing mechanisms of the bitumen and bituminous mixtures are explained, as the laboratory protocols used to simulate, evaluate, and mitigate it.

## 1. Introduction

Around the world, more than 80 % of the highways were constructed utilizing asphalt pavements, and even in some countries such as Canada and People’s Republic of China (PR China), this proportion exceeds 90 % [1–3]. This type of paving alternative uses bitumen as the binder material to produce bituminous mixtures, which are the material that composes the wearing courses in the structural package of flexible pavements [4–7]. Nonetheless, the increasing global demand to enhance the sustainability features of the paving industry has led to examining the eco-efficiency of asphalt pavements because the bituminous materials undergo accelerated deterioration caused by environmental factors [8–10]. The rapid deterioration of asphalt pavements due to weathering is comprehended as the ageing phenomenon [11–14]. The combination of heat, moisture, oxygen and ultraviolet (UV) radiation generates this ageing process that can be simplified as the physical hardening of bitumen [8,15–17]. The physic-chemical changes in the bitumen cause several effects on the bituminous mixtures, such as an increase in the rigidity and viscosity, which can be translated into a high bearing capacity and an elevated rutting resistance [18,19]. However, the loss of adhesion between aggregates and bitumen also occurs, generating brittle mixtures with low fatigue resistance [18,19]. Therefore, it is considered that ageing is one of the main factors that reduce the performance and service life of bituminous materials [17,20].

Because the ageing phenomenon is one of the most critical aspects affecting the service life of bituminous mixture, this topic has been intensely studied in the road infrastructure industry [12,21]. In fact, the hardening effect caused by the ageing process on the bitumen was recognized in the 20th century and has been broadly studied during the last seven decades [22,23]. In this way, researchers worldwide have been capable of assessing the role of diverse facets in the ageing phenomenon, such as environmental factors, preventive techniques, and reconstructive activities of asphalt pavements [8,17]. However, although these crucial research efforts, there is currently no consensus regarding the ageing of bituminous materials; nevertheless, state of the art recognizes UV radiation as the main factor governing this process [6,15,24].

Even though UV radiation is one of the primary producers of bituminous materials ageing, few research efforts have concentrated on UV ageing since most investigations focus on thermal ageing [25,26]. On the other hand, because global warming and climate change cause UV radiation to become more potent on the earth’s surface, UV ageing on asphalt pavements has become more severe over the years [27]. These two reasons have led several researchers to pay attention (especially in recent years) to the study of UV ageing on bituminous materials, intending to develop novel materials with high resistance to UV ageing and improved long-term performance [28,29]. Thus, achieving to optimize the consumption of resources in the transport infrastructure sector. These same reasons are those that justify the development of this article. This manuscript presents a comprehensive literature review of UV ageing phenomena on bituminous materials intending to provide state of the art with a critical examination of scientific and investigative advances on this topic. Furthermore, the document highlights key aspects, such as industry trends, research gaps, and challenges to overcome in future research.

The structure of the paper is explained subsequently. In **Section 2**, a bibliometric analysis regarding the last decade is presented. **Section 3** clarifies essential concepts such as electromagnetic spectra, UV radiation, and direct normal irradiance. Next, **Sections 4-7** disclose the UV ageing phenomena based on crucial sub-topics. **Section 4** establishes the UV ageing mechanism in bitumen and its repercussion on other bituminous materials. In **Section 5**, the protocols to simulate the UV ageing on bitumen and bituminous mixtures are discussed. Likewise, **Section 6** address the laboratory tests mainly employed to assess the UV ageing on bitumen and bituminous mixtures. Succeeding, in **Section 7**, the additives usually utilized in the industry to mitigate the UV ageing on bituminous materials are examined. Finally, **Section 8** summarises the main conclusions of this investigation; also, some recommendations for future research lines are listed.

Such is the damage that UV radiation has on organic compounds, that it generates mutations in the deoxyribonucleic acid of living creatures [204,211], causes skin cancer in humans [212,213], is capable of disinfecting water for drinks and food products [214–216], and accelerates the degradation of some plastics [217,218]. The alterations generated by the UV radiation on the bitumen are called the UV ageing phenomena [124,179]. Notably, due to the astronomical and geographical factors, the regions with the highest UV intensity are those areas of high latitude, high altitude, and plateaus, whilst the lowest UV intensity develops near the poles [50,64,68,220]. ## 4. UV ageing phenomena on bituminous materials

## 4.1. Bitumen chemistry

Bitumen is one of the products obtained from refining petroleum (also called crude oil) and, due to its viscoelastic properties, is widely employed in the road infrastructure industry as a binder material to the construction of asphalt pavements [48,143,146,219,229–231]. Due to its hydrophobicity and high adhesion to other materials, bitumen is also used to manufacture roofing membranes and carry out waterproofing activities [5,12,30,94,179,232].

Each bitumen has a distinct chemical composition due to the differences in the origins of the base crude oils and, thus, different engineering properties and denoted performance [133,219]. Although differences exist in the chemistry of bitumens, these materials can be understood as a complex combination of more than one thousand types of hydrocarbons (mainly aliphatic and aromatic hydrocarbons) and oxygen compounds with a small quantity of nitrogen and sulphur compounds [49,120,155,233]. Further, there are also traces of metals in bitumen [8,139,229,234]. A particular mixture of hydrocarbon molecules confers a unique temperature-dependent viscoelastic behavior to the bitumen [232,235]. According to the polarity of these compounds, they can be classified into four groups (so-called SARA fractions) from lower to higher polarity, namely Saturates, Aromatics, Resins and Asphaltenes [49,154,236,237]. Table 2 and Fig. 7 presents the main characteristics and the typical molecular formula of the SARA fractions, respectively. The SARA fractions are usually considered a colloidal system in which micelles of asphaltenes (solid particles with high polarity and molecular weight) remain in maltenes (oily environment with lower polarity and molecular weight composed of saturates, aromatics and resins) [12,120]. Fig. 8 shows the schematic representation of this model.

Due to the asphaltenes guaranteeing the adhesion with aggregates and the maltenes providing fluidity and workability, the proportion of SARA fractions plays a fundamental role in the performance of bituminous materials and their engineering properties [12,65,238]. In turn, some researchers [8,12,236,239,240] have discovered the influence of the balances of the SARA fractions as described below: (i) more saturates and aromatics confer the bitumen a more liquid-like state; (ii) more resins and asphaltenes provoke the bitumen into a more solid-like state and rigidity; (iii) more aromatic and asphaltene develops hightemperature properties, whilst a low asphaltene content improves the temperature sensitivity behavior; (iv) the viscosity increases proportionally to the resins content (including polar aromatics); and (v) saturates, aromatics and asphaltenes facilitate the flow of bitumen. From the previous, it is clear that the engineering performance of bitumen is governed by a delicate chemical equilibrium in the SARA fractions. Precisely, this is a problem that causes the UV ageing phenomena: a chain effect that provides a progressive chemical restructuring in the bitumen that ends with a decrease in its useful life [13,20,205]. Such is the effect of UV ageing on bitumen that it causes the transition from a “sol-like” to a “gel-like” state, i.e., induces a decrease in the colloidal stability index (Ic) [46,146,241]; this evolution is presented in Fig. 9. ## 4.2. Ageing phenomena

Due to its viscoelastic properties, bitumen governs flexible pavement performance and serves as a waterproofing barrier to keep the aggregates jointly within the bituminous mixtures [15,48,65]. Nevertheless, during the service life of asphalt pavements, the bitumen is continuously exposed to weathering, which implies heat, rain, UV radiation, wind, and other environmental factors [11,30,48,122,245]. These environmental conditions accelerate the normal deterioration process that pavement structures suffer due to traffic loads [13,19,20]. This complex process synthesizes the ageing phenomenon [30]. Besides, it is valid to clarify that the ageing rate and molecular interaction depend on the bitumen’s kind and chemical composition, i.e., molecular size, composition, and polarity [13,155].

The ageing of bitumen is remarkably complex, in which more than fifteen different chemical mechanisms are involved [13,246]. Nevertheless, three ageing mechanisms are recognized as principles, namely volatilization, oxidation, and steric hardening [11,13,20,246,247]. These mechanisms are explained below:

• Volatilization refers to an irreversible physical phenomenon involving the evaporation of the light components of the bitumen [13,20]. Therefore, changes in the composition and proportion of the SARA fractions are generated [11,12,120]. The loss of volatile molecules (principally saturates and aromatics) reduces the adhesion forces and workability whilst increasing the bitumen’s viscosity [13,138]. Likewise, volatilization causes the bitumen increases its softening point and reduce its penetration [106,155]. This mechanism occurs at high temperatures, mainly during the production of the bituminous mixture [13,120]. Notably, when the bitumen is heated to more than 150 ◦C, augmenting the temperature by 10–12 ◦C induces the duplication of the volatilization rate [155,239].

• Oxidation refers to an irreversible chemical reaction between the bitumen and the atmospheric oxygen [10]. During oxidation, the carbon–carbon chains of the bitumen are broken and combined with oxygen, which alters and reorganizes the functional groups and the SARA fractions [11,118]. Basically, part of the aromatics is converted into resins and then into asphaltenes, which reduces the proportion of resins and aromatics while increasing asphaltene fractions [11,20]. This mechanism is explained in greater depth in the following subsections of this article.

• Steric hardening refers to a reversible structural reorganization/ rearrangement of the bitumen molecules generated by temperature exposure for prolonged periods (even close to room temperature), heat or mechanical work [20,155,248,249]. The effect of steric hardening in the bitumen is time-dependent and can be understood as the progressive increment of viscosity and brittleness, together with the loss of healing properties [249,250]. Chemically, this process involves crystallizing specific bitumen molecules (mainly asphaltenes), while physically, it is evidenced by slightly volume contractions and the appearance of cracks on the top of the bituminous mixture [11,20,155,249,251].

• Some of the other minor ageing mechanisms are aromatization, exudation, polymerization, separation, syneresis, thixotropy, among others [13,139,246].

The above ageing mechanisms appear in two major stages: shortterm and long-term ageing [12,135,138,155]. The short-term ageing comprises the bituminous mixture production and the activities of pavement construction, i.e., storage, mixing, transport and laying down [15,131,138]. Meanwhile, long-term ageing occurs throughout the entire service life of the asphalt pavement [26,113,123]. Although the ageing mechanisms ensue in both stages, it is recognized that volatilization dominates in short-term ageing, while oxidation and steric hardening prevail in long-term ageing [11,13,157,247,252]. Furthermore, these ageing mechanisms are activated by two ageing processes, namely thermal ageing and UV ageing [92,113,134,144,151]. In this way, the oxidation caused by temperature is called thermo-oxidative ageing; meanwhile, the oxidation caused by UV radiation is called UV ageing, photo-oxidative ageing or light-oxidative ageing [54,156,168,170]. Most thermal ageing happens during short-term ageing, and otherwise, UV ageing is taken during the long-term due to environmental conditions [142,179]. Accordingly, UV ageing does not initiate volatilization or steric hardening, but it could accelerate these mechanisms [13,17,48,120,135,151]; in order to explore this topic in more depth, these investigations can be consulted [22,62,249,253,254]. Besides, moisture or humidity can catalyze these physical–chemical changes significantly, so much so that some researchers classify moisture damage as a type of ageing [11,138,177,184]. The water damage is also denominated as water ageing [73,106]. This effect is because water provides a source of hydrogen atoms that can increase the oxidation reactions and may disrupt the colloidal structure of the bitumen [8,73]. Physically, the water ageing on bitumen manifests as a loss of adhesion between the bitumen and aggregate interface, the loss of cohesion in the bituminous mixture, and then the stripping [71,106,247].

Fig. 10 shows the concurrence of the types of ageing mentioned above. This graph illustrates that during the short-term ageing stage, volatilization is the primary ageing mechanism [13,120,155]; meanwhile, oxidation and steric hardening predominantly occur during the long-term ageing stage [249,254,255]. Similarly, thermal ageing is mainly involved during the short-term stage, whilst the other ageing processes (i.e., UV and water ageing) are practically exclusive to the long-term stage [12,25,73].

The ageing phenomenon causes an increase in the viscosity of the bitumen and, consequently, the increase of the bituminous material’s stiffness [8,38,155,247]. Because of this, the accumulation of permanent deformations due to traffic loads is decreased [18,29,113]. In this way, the rutting resistance of the bitumen is improved [25,66,256]. Likewise, the fatigue resistance of the bitumen would be more elevated after the ageing occurs due to the creation of a cross-linked network structure in the bitumen matrix, which require more energy to break [82,123,135,239]. Nevertheless, some authors consider that the ageing phenomenon augments brittleness and reduces the fatigue life of bituminous materials [11,18,59,248]. Two factors occasion the previous discrepancy: (i) the decrease in cohesive and adhesions forces due to ageing mechanisms [13,138,257]; and (ii) the type of test performed to measure the resistance to fatigue failure, i.e., stress-controlled conditions or strain-controlled conditions [31,258,259]. These factors are explained below.

Fatigue cracking is one of the principal failure mechanisms in bituminous materials [260–262]. The fatigue failure is governed by two properties, namely cohesive bond energy (within bitumen) and adhesive bond energy (between bitumen and aggregates) [263–265]. In this way, cohesion is the intermolecular force that holds the bitumen molecules [263,266–268]. Meanwhile, adhesion is the quantity of molecular force demanded to maintain the union of bitumen and aggregates [256,263,269,270]. There are different theories and methods to understand and measure these forces; however, the concept of surface free energy (SFE) is gaining notoriety in the state of the art to explain these properties [271–273]. The SFE is defined as the amount of external work needed on a material to create a new interface per unit area in a vacuum [272,274–277]. Accordingly, the SFE is an inherent characteristic of all materials associated with chemical reactions along their surface [276,277]. Experimentally, the SFE indirectly estimates the contact angles between two surfaces (usually by implementing probe/testing liquids) [276,278]. As shown in **Equation (3)**, the total SFE is calculated as the sum of two components: (i) the nonpolar component, also known as the Lifshitz-van der Waals or dispersive component; and (ii) the polar component, also known as the Lewis or base and acid component [274,275,277]. Generally, the cohesion fracture energy correlates positively with the nonpolar component, whilst the adhesion fracture energy keeps this behaviour with the polar component [273,278,279]. On the other hand, the combined impact of oxidation and other ageing mechanisms increases the asphaltenes fraction in bitumen, resulting in chemical alterations that reduce (progressively with exposure time) the total SFE [275,280]. Thus, the reduction in SFE caused by the ageing of the bitumen can induce fatigue cracks due to adhesion or cohesion failures [281–284]. Likewise, as SFE decreases, the resistance to moisture damage is also diminished in bituminous materials [76,276,277,285]. Consequently, due to these alterations in the SFE, the fatigue life of bituminous materials can be reduced after the ageing phenomena, despite the presence of new cross-linked network structures in the chemical bitumen structure.

The other factor that can alter the results of the fatigue resistance evaluation on bituminous materials is the type of test performed [258,286]. Typically, fatigue tests are based on applying load repetitions until the material develops a failure condition, i.e., cracking [262,287]. Mainly, there are two types of tests to assess fatigue in the laboratory: stress-controlled tests and strain-controlled tests [288,289]. In the first, the stress remains constant throughout the load repetitions, but the strain (i.e., deformation originated due to stress) progressively increases [290,291]. In the second, the strain stays constant, but the stress progressively increases while conducting load repetitions [292,293]. On the other hand, with the increase in load repetition number, bituminous materials lose stiffness [260,261]. Therefore, they require less load and stress to produce the same strain [294–297]. Consequently, straincontrolled tests treat materials with higher rigidity under a more severe load level [31,298]. Precisely, the aged bituminous materials exhibit greater stiffness than their non-aged control sample [11,13,47]. Hence, a strain-controlled test could provide the false outcome that ageing phenomena reduce the fatigue life of the materials evaluated [31,299]. Thus, stress-controlled tests are a more suitable approximation to measure fatigue performance [31,300]. Although it is preferable to implement stress-controlled tests over strain-controlled tests to estimate the effect of ageing, this preference may not be appropriate if other mechanical regards are considered. Notably, [297,301–303] can be consulted to address this topic more in-depth. The preceding has motivated the development of fatigue tests under an intermediate load condition, which oscillates between controlled stress and controlled strain states [289,292,304,305].

## 4.3. Oxidation of bituminous materials

The primary ageing mechanism that induces the hardening of bitumen is oxidation [130,246]. The previous presents the importance of comprehending this mechanism. Nonetheless, there is no consensus on the oxidation of bituminous materials in the literature [8]; this is because oxidation is a complex phenomenon that depends on many variables. When asphalt pavements are constructed, the surface of the bituminous mixture is exposed to the atmosphere, leading to oxygen diffusion within the bitumen matrix [12,138,257]. The main variables that govern oxygen diffusion are the air-void distribution in the mixture (i.e., oxygen penetration capacity), the thickness of bitumen around aggregates, environmental temperature, atmospheric pressure, and humidity [138,155,306]. Within these variables, the one that can be handled from the approach of design methodologies is the air-void distribution. In fact, due to the void interconnection, the greater the air voids, the more quickly and deeply the oxygen molecules will be able to penetrate the bituminous mixture [13,307]. Clearly, oxygen diffusion occurs progressively from the surface to the bottom, and thereupon the bitumen oxidation is considerably more elevated on the surface than in deeper layers [13,166]. Although there is no unanimity, it is considered that the depth of oxidation on bituminous material is limited to approximately 1000 μm [257]. On the other hand, some investigations have stated that the aggregates’ characteristics (i.e., source, chemical composition, gradation, filler content, among others) can influence the oxidation mechanisms [84,138].

When oxygen interacts with the organic molecules of the bitumen, a chain effect is generated that involves molecular dispersion, the production of new polar molecules, and the formation of new functional groups [8,308]. Fig. 11 shows the functional groups assembled during the oxidative ageing of bitumen. In turn, oxidative ageing can be described by three different chemical changes, namely fragmentation, oxygen addition, and condensation [138,309]. Fig. 12 explains this transition. Below, each of these alterations chemicals is explained in detail.

Fragmentation is the most effortless oxidation effect to comprehend. Basically, fragmentation consists of the large molecules breaking, generating smaller ones, thus forming molecules of water, carbon dioxide, acetic acid and methane as by-products [69,138]. In this way, the proportion of large molecules in the bitumen progressively decreases, whilst the proportion of small molecules increases [61,133].

During the O2 addition, the free oxygen in the atmosphere reacts with the bitumen molecules generating new functional groups, specifically the carbonyl (C––O) and sulfoxide (S––O) group formation [11,138,235,237]. It is valid to clarify that these are not the only groups that typically originate; others of lesser importance are also formed, such as the hydroxyl, acid and ester groups [239,311]. These recently created oxygen-containing molecules allow an increment in the oxygen content of the bitumen and the unsaturation of the molecules [8,31,108]. Remarkably, oxygen diffusion within the bitumen matrix is progressive and depends on temperature exposure and ageing time [49,168]. The amount at which these groups are formed is usually measured by Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy [130,141,168]. This method is explained in-deep in later sections of this manuscript. Furthermore, the C––O and S––O groups assembled during the oxidation generate an increase in the polarity of the host compounds, thus is more attainable for the association of these compounds with other polar compounds [29,138]. In this way, less soluble (in n-alkane medium) hydrocarbons are produced [138,165]. Due to bitumen chemistry changes, the physical behaviour response is reflected in rheological alterations and augmented viscosity [8,31,312].

The last stage of oxidative ageing is called the condensation or carbonization process. Essentially, the aromatic (benzylic carbon group) and weightier/larger bitumen molecules are constituted in this process [138,310]. The formation of these large molecules is due to polyaromatic oxidation reactions [12,55,255]. Fig. 13 summarizes these chemical reactions. In this way, the transformation of small molecules to macromolecules creates moiety networks and leads to the homolysis of alkyl side chains [3,8,311,313,314]. Consequently, this phase of oxidative ageing is the principal cause of the increase in the bitumen’s polarity, viscosity, and stiffness, together with the loss of flexibility and self-healing capacity [8,53,246,315].

Due to oxidative ageing, the bitumen develops molecular rearrangements that induce the evolution of surface properties [130,166]. Accordingly, new microstructures on the aged bitumen are formed by the products of chemical reactions, which are not soluble in the bitumen matrix and are partially detached from it [165,166]. These microstructures can be observed, estimated, and evaluated through morphology assessment [6,121,316]. Usually, these changes in the micro and nano structures of the aged bitumen are measured with the scanning electron microscope (SEM) [62,148,168,172]. This technique is widely described in later sections of this manuscript. Commonly, these new microstructures have distinctive shapes of bee, rods, squares, spheres, ellipsoids, beans, and flower or leaf structures [165,166]. Although there are numerous characteristic shapes, two stand out: “bee” and “flower or leaf” microstructures [63,184]. Notably, the bee-like structure is the one that most attracts the attention of researchers because it is remarkably susceptible to weathering, showing substantial changes in form, size and distribution after the ageing phenomena [21,157,316,317].

## 4.4. Photo-oxidation of bituminous materials

The previous section of the manuscript explained the oxidation mechanism in depth. Nonetheless, such a definition and description are only appropriate for oxidative ageing caused by temperatures, i.e., thermo-oxidative ageing. Otherwise, the oxidation induced by UV radiation (i.e., UV ageing or photo-oxidative ageing) involves additional effects and chemical reactions. Below, the photo-oxidation of bituminous materials is addressed in detail.

The inherent black appearance of bitumen allows the bituminous materials can effortlessly absorb the energy from the solar radiation during the daytime [43,83]. Accordingly, UV radiation is one of the principal causes of the ageing of bituminous materials during service life [17,113]. Moreover, the degradation of the bitumen generated by UV ageing is so elevated that it is considered one of the primary reducers of asphalt pavement performance [11,25,146]. The UV ageing causes an effect denominated photo-oxidation on the bitumen. Although photooxidation is closely similar to thermal oxidation, photo-oxidation induces more surface ageing and then more decline in properties of flexible pavements [10,63]. Nonetheless, state-of-the-art development has been focused on studying thermal ageing, whilst UV ageing has been taking second place in the tendency research topics [54,125,148]. The preceding could explain why there is no consensus in the literature on UV ageing phenomena in bitumen and bituminous mixtures.

When the bituminous materials are laid on the surface of the transport infrastructure, they are subjected to UV radiation under a particular frequency band in sunlight, which causes free-radical reactions and a progressive increment of specific functional groups such as carbonyl and sulfoxide groups [98,177,318]. In addition, the high energy induced by the UV radiation on the bitumen generates atomic ionization and molecular free radicals that can alter the proportion of SARA fractions, give rise to several chemical compounds (mainly low-weight molecules) and induce molecular cracking [20,42,81]. Likewise, exposure to the UV rays can excite the bitumeńs molecules and break the covalent bonds, leading to free radicals joining with available oxygen, thus developing a chain reaction of irreversible chemical changes [35,42,50]. Fig. 14 presents an overview of the UV ageing process. Although the UV degradation of bitumen depends on environmental factors (such as humidity, heat, and oxygen availability), UV ageing has a usual demeanour [50]. This behaviour can be summarized in five stages [311,319]; these are detailed in Table 3.

The UV light coming from the sun reaches the surface of the asphalt pavements and diffuses through the voids of the bituminous mixture, thus managing to affect the bitumen to a certain depth [61,112]. Hence, the UV radiation penetration depth is an essential variable that governs the UV ageing of bituminous materials [90,220]. Notably, the preceding is understood as the diffusion model to UV ageing of bitumen [59,111]. Fig. 15 shows this model and explains that the photo-oxidized bitumen has three distinct layers [35,86]:

• Direct UV ageing layer: UV radiation affects most of the bitumen molecules.

• Diffusion UV ageing layer: UV radiation affects a considerable part of the bitumen molecules. Besides, the affectation decreases with increasing depth.

• Fresh bitumen layer: UV radiation negligibly alters the bitumen molecules.

Fig. 15 illustrates the diffusion of UV radiation through the thickness of the bituminous film. The photo-oxidation of bitumen occurs powerfully at the pavement surface and diminishes non-linearly with depth. The preceding indicates: (i) there is a maximum thickness after which the bitumen is not affected by the UV light; and (ii) the UV ageing is more severe in the upper layers of the bituminous materials. Remarkably, the vertical gradient with which UV radiation diffuses depends on the exposure time [35,61]. As the ageing time increases, the ageing depth also extends [4,50,90]. However, this increase is not directly proportional [35,86]. As time passes, the UV light has a lower diffusion rate until reaching a maximum top [61,131,138]. Nonetheless, there is no consensus in the literature on the maximum depth in which UV radiation causes the ageing of bitumen. For instance, in the research [35], it was determined that the limit of affectation by UV radiation was around 4.5 µm. Meanwhile, in [59,61,111,112], it was evidenced that this depth is close to 10000 µm. These vast discrepancies between the results of the investigations can be attributed to substantial differences in the tests used to simulate and assess UV ageing; this topic is addressed in-depth in the following sections of this manuscript. The previous description of the diffusion model of UV radiation through bituminous materials occurs at two levels in asphalt pavements. These levels are at the pavement structure scale and the bituminous film (bitumen attached to the aggregates) scale. Fig. 16 shows both scales via an example using a typical flexible pavement. • At the first level, the distribution of the voids limits the UV radiation’s passage through the bituminous mixture depth [61,131,138]. In other words, the lack of interconnection between the voids restricts the penetration of UV light, thus limiting the thickness of the bituminous mixture that is aged by this process. Therefore, the higher the percentage of voids in a bituminous mixture, the greater its susceptibility to UV ageing [13,131].

• At the second level, the UV ageing depth is controlled by the thickness of bitumen that covers each aggregate. This is because the energy originating from UV irradiation (photons) has a limited capacity to pass across (in the form of an electromagnetic wave) the bitumen molecules. In fact, lower dominant wavelengths and higher frequencies develop more energy [54,167]. Therefore, due to the inherent energy of the UV light of a specific place, the bitumen ages to a particular thickness in each geographic latitude. Hence, to mitigate UV ageing, it is recommended to design bituminous mixtures with suitable bitumen quantity, which generates enough thickness to prevent UV radiation can affect the entire binder film. In any case, the bitumen dosage in a bituminous mixture must also satisfy the performance, economic and environmental criteria [320–322].

The effects generated by the UV ageing in the bitumen and bituminous mixtures are very similar to those caused by other types of ageing; save one difference, UV ageing induces more aggressive deterioration. Physically, UV ageing of bituminous materials occurs more intensely at the surface [90,123,164,246]. The main changes provoked by the UV radiation in the physical properties of the bituminous materials are listed below:

• Alteration of the micromorphology until developing a more solidlike state [7,133],

• Rheological changes based on an increase in the elastic component and a decrease in the viscous component [29,111,150]. The pre-ceding is reflected in the increment of complex modulus, absolute viscosity, dynamic viscosity, stiffness, rutting parameter, fatigue parameter, as well as a reduction in the temperature susceptibility and phase angle [34,113,133,163,167,220]. In this way, the rheological properties are severely deteriorated, especially at low temperatures [96,107,111,176,178].

• Augmented brittleness and hardness, which results in less resistance to cracking and fatigue failure [27,35,79,100]. Notably, the susceptibility to cracking develops with greater intenseness when UV ageing is generated at low temperatures [25,73,106,143].

• Increased resistance to permanent deformations accumulation, i.e., less susceptibility to rutting [29,110,125].

• Due to the above changes, there is also a higher softening point, flame resistance, and ignition temperature [121,135,167]. Nevertheless, it also appears lower penetration, ductility and self-healing capacity [45,58,89,111].

• In summary, UV ageing reduces the service life of flexible pavements [102,164,323].

## 5. UV ageing simulation

## 5.1. Protocols to simulate the UV ageing on bitumen

The rolling thin film oven test (RTFOT) and pressure ageing vessel (PAV) are employed in the laboratory to simulate bitumen’s short-term and long-term ageing, respectively [26,219,230,247]. Although these are currently the most widely used simulation tests, the RTFOT and PAV only operate with thermal ageing and do not consider UV ageing [31,92,157,220]. The preceding implies that the combined effect of RTFOT and PAV fails to properly emulate the ageing suffered by bitumens exposed to weathering (the typical situation for flexible pavements) [18,31,185,219]. Indeed, UV ageing is hugely more potent than achieved after these tests [220,247].

The RTFOT (standardized by ASTM D2872) can be summarized as injecting hot air at $1 6 3   ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C }$ into an open bottle covered with bitumen for 85 min while rolling [324]. Meanwhile, the PAV test (standardized by ASTM D6521) consists of submitting 50 g of bitumen recovered from RTFOT residue in a vessel to a pressurized air stream at 2.07 MPa for 20 h at a temperature between 90 and $1 1 0   ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ [325]. After each test, the lost bitumen mass is calculated by the difference between the pre-ageing and post-ageing states [324,325]. Table 4 synthesizes some of these other methods. Among those exposed, the thin film oven test (TFOT) stands out for being the predecessor to the RTFOT [12,247]. The main difference between them is that in the TFOT, the bottle with bitumen does not roll [13,84]. Otherwise, there is no standardized method to simulate the UV radiation in bitumen and thus induce the UV ageing [35,151,220]. Consequently, researchers use their custom-made protocols to cause photo-oxidative ageing of bitumen; some of these can be consulted in Table 5. Also, Fig. 17 shows an analysis of the dispersion of the data presented in Table 5. On the other hand, the ASTM D4798/D4798M and ASTM D4799/D4799M standards provide procedures for replicating weathering ageing of bituminous roofing and waterproofing materials by exposure to Xenon arc light and UV light [326,327]. Similarly, the ASTM G154 sets forth the practice of utilizing fluorescent UV lamp apparatus to ageing nonmetallic materials; however, this regulation does not even mention bitumen [328].

In the UV ageing protocols outlined in Table $^ { 5 , }$ researchers build their chambers or ovens, which contain custom-design UV lamps. Due to this type of equipment being self-made, significant differences in the simulation variables (i.e., temperature, duration, film thickness, intensity, and wavelength) are found among the different investigations published in the literature. The preceding makes it unfeasible to synthesize or generalize the results offered by state of the art regarding UV radiation’s influence on the behaviour of bitumen. In other words, each investigation is a case study, and its conclusions must be treated with the care typical of a case study. Hence, an internationally recognized agency must develop a standard to simulate the UV ageing of bitumen. Accordingly, Table 5 and Fig. 17 show no consensus on the protocols implemented to simulate the UV ageing of bitumen in the laboratory. Nonetheless, the procedure is set mainly in the way described below: the bitumen is previously aged by the TFOT, and subsequently, the residue of TFOT is placed on an iron plate with a film thickness of 2000 μm and following, the exposure to UV radiation is carried out using UV lamps with 8 $\tilde { \mathbf { W } } / \mathbf { m } ^ { 2 }$ of intensity and 365 nm of wavelength for 6 days at a working temperature of $6 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C } ,$

Although the UV exposure variables are freely selected in each investigation, these choices must be consistent with the geographical location to be simulated. It is evident in Figs. 5-6 that the UV radiation intensity is not similar worldwide. Similarly, intensity is not the only variable that changes from location to location. For example, temperature, duration, and wavelength received are also governed by climatological factors. On the other hand, the thickness of the bitumen depends on the mixture design and construction processes. The influence that these variables have on the UV ageing of bitumen is explained below:

• Many investigations do not even attempt to select and report the main wavelength of the applied UV radiation [178]. The preceding represents a substantial deficiency in the simulation processes because the molecules that constitute the bitumen react differently according to the wavelength [52,54]. Shorter wavelengths have higher photon energy and thus generate more potent UV ageing [26,73,117].

• At higher temperatures, durations and intensities of UV irradiation exposure, the transformations generated in the chemical structure of bitumen are more severe, and therefore, UV ageing also increases [10,26,58,90,220].

• Otherwise, as the bitumen film thickness increases, UV ageing is less notorious [13,178,179]; due to the greater the thickness, the more energy is needed to excite (and initiate a series of chemical reactions) such a depth of bitumen [13,35,121].

Other investigators propose cyclical UV ageing procedures. Notably, these approaches are less typical than those presented in Table 5. Next, some examples are exposed. In $[ 1 5 7 , 1 6 5 ]$ , two ageing cycles were implemented; the first consisted of 8 h of UV irradiation with a working temperature at $5 0   ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C } ,$ followed by 4 h of the dark $\operatorname { a t } 4 2   ^ { \circ } \mathsf { C } .$ Meanwhile, in [183], a modification of the previous procedure was made: 100 % moisture was added during the dark cycle. [48] offers a similar proposal using invariant temperature at $4 5   ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C } .$ [130] implement 500 cycles of 2 h with constant UV irradiation at 60 $\mathbb { W } / \mathbb { m } ^ { 2 }$ and $6 0   ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ;$ each cycle is composed of 2 min of watering and 118 min of drying with relative humidity at 70 %. On the other hand, in [34,177], 25 cycles of three steps were carried out: first, 4 h of UV radiation at 45 ${ } ^ { \circ } \mathbb { C } _ { \flat } ,$ second, 15 min of water spray, and finally, 3.75 h of condensation at $4 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C } .$ [159] utilized two similar steps: first, 51 min of UV light exposure, and second, 9 min of UV radiation and water spray; the authors used a constant temperature of $^ \circ 6 0   ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C }$ and up to 3000 h of exposure. Remarkably, in [120], the co-irradiation of UV with infrared radiation is evaluated. In general, water, dark, and another range of EMS are utilized to form a more accurate simulation regarding the expected environmental conditions [31].

Otherwise, because the ageing obtained in the field can be more harmful to bitumen than ageing simulated in the laboratory [86,166], several researchers have simultaneously carried out outdoor and indoor tests to correlate them [10,18,43,117]. Meanwhile, in the case study [137], it was defined that 1608 h of indoor UV ageing is analogous to 13 months of environmental ageing in Xi’an City. These substantial differences can be attributed to discrepancies in the protocols used to irradiate the bitumen under controlled conditions and each city’s climatic conditions behaviour. ## 5.2. Protocols to simulate the UV ageing on bituminous mixtures

As in the simulation of UV ageing for bitumens, two types of protocols stand out in bituminous mixtures: (i) utilization of chambers or ovens to generate indoor UV-accelerated ageing; and (ii) exposure to weathering to cause typical outdoor ageing. Tables 7 and 8 present a synthesis of some research that involves these simulation procedures. As expected, in the previous tables, there is no consensus regarding the methodological proposal by the authors examined. Hence, drawing general conclusions (beyond the boundaries imposed by a case study) becomes practically impossible. On the other hand, a noteworthy investigation was found in [127], in which the discrete element modelling was implemented to assess through computational/numerical evaluations the UV ageing of bituminous mixtures. Nonetheless, no similar bibliographic materials were encountered. In this way, the pre-ceding represents a gap in the literature and an opportunity to investigate to enhance state of the art.

In order to emulate environmental conditions more precisely, it is typical that moisture is also incorporated to simulate UV ageing of bituminous mixtures [18,97,167]. ## 6.1. Atomic force microscopy (AFM)

The AFM is a device that continuously records the topography of materials (in both air and liquid media) at a sub-nanometric scale [7,72,337,338]. The AFM uses a very sharp probe (stylus) that scans and interacts directly with the material’s surface, in this way, exploring the repulsive and attractive forces between the probe and the sample surface to create high-resolution dimensional topographic images (in two or three dimensions) of the surface [339–341]. With the analysis of the images obtained from the AFM, it is possible to obtain the topography and morphology [10,121]; however, it is also conceivable to determine adhesion forces, elasticity, roughness, stiffness, electrostatic force, nanoindentation, and nano-phase separation [337]. Notably, within the broad functionality of AFMs, the phase imaging mode stands out because it lets identify the three primary phases of bitumen morphology: (i) bee structures (i.e., catanaphase), (ii) dispersed phase (i.e., periphase) and (iii) the matrix (i.e., paraphase) [50,231,236,317,342].

The resolution of the images is so high and precise that it permits measuring the microstructural differences due to the chemical composition of the bitumens [10,342,343]. In this way, the microstructure parameters achieved by AFM can be employed to characterize the behaviour and chemical stability of the bitumens [63,140,157]. Consequently, the AFM is widely used to investigate the severity of ageing caused by UV radiation [52,72,146]. In general terms, the higher UV ageing, the more bee structures increase [106,157,165]. Nonetheless, the bitumen sample preparation processes for AFM study often require exposure to heat [165,316,317,342]. Therefore, it can cause undesirable thermal ageing that affects the photo-oxidative ageing measurements.

## 6.2. Dynamic shear rheometer (DSR)

The DSR is a device used to measure the mechanical response of the fluids subjected to different temperatures, frequencies, and shear stress levels [344–347]. Accordingly, the DSR is commonly utilized to analyse the rheological performance of bituminous materials such as bitumen and bituminous mastic under different ageing conditions [331,348–350]. The preceding is because the DSR can record viscous and elastic behaviour by determining several properties, namely shear stress, strain, phase angle, complex viscosity, elastic modulus, viscous modulus, and complex modulus [351,352]. Furthermore, from the results obtained by this equipment, it is possible to calculate other parameters such as DSR function, fatigue factor, non-recoverable compliance, rutting factor, and zero shear viscosity [36,43,353]. In this way, the DSR estimates the evolution of bitumen’s high-temperature rutting and low-temperature cracking properties throughout the ageing process [45,151,354,355]. Notably, several case studies reported in the literature state that UV radiation has the following effects on bituminous materials [16,29,90,111,142,178,237]: (i) as UV ageing progresses, the phase angle decreases and the complex modulus gradually increases; (ii) UV irradiation progressively raises the elastic and viscous modulus, but as the exposure time passes, the elastic behaviour predominates over the viscous behaviour; and (iii) photo-oxidative ageing generates an augment in rutting resistance but reduces the resistance to fatigue cracking.

## 6.3. Elemental analysis (EA) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS)

The EA is the set of techniques that determine the proportion of the chemical elements that compose a material [356–359]. This type of analysis provides an overview of the characteristics and chemical properties of the materials under study [232,360–362]. Although there are many procedures and equipment to perform the EA, the XPS stands out for its practicality and versatility [175,177,363]. XPS employs an Xray photons source with specific energy to trigger electrons at the innermost orbitals of atoms; in this way, the resulting energy spectrum offers peaks describing the electronic structure of the atoms present on the material’s surface [177,364–366]. Remarkably, the XPS is one of the most used techniques to carry out the EA of bituminous materials under various ageing conditions, intending to characterize the evolution of chemical changes [2,54,367]. For instance, the EA of the bitumen shows that it is mainly formed by carbon and hydrogen, followed by sulfur, oxygen, nitrogen, and some trace metals such as cobalt, iron, nickel, and vanadium [239,343]. Besides, due to the photo-oxidative processes induced by UV irradiation, the bitumen increases its oxygen proportion and diminishes its hydrogen fractions during UV ageing [51,52].

## 6.5. Gel permeation chromatography (GPC) and Thin-Layer chromatography with flame ionization detection (TLC-FID)

The previous sections of this manuscript showed that UV ageing causes chemical reactions that increase the molecular size of some bitumen components, altering the SARA fractions’ conformation [11,17,311]. In other words, a change from sol bitumen to gel bitumen occurs progressively with photo-oxidative ageing [34,99,254]. These chemical differences can be measured with chromatography tests, such as GPC or TLC-FID [72,142,146,160]. In the GPC test, a multi-solvent delivery system uses porous columns to retain the bitumen for a period denominated “eluting time” [48,369,371]. Macromolecules have a shorter eluting time than the smaller molecules; in this way, the SARA fractions are separated according to the ranges of eluting times required to pass through a particular solvent [72,239,371]. Regarding the TLC-FID test, three polar spreading agents (heptane, toluene, and dichloromethane) are mixed with ethanol and cyclohexane to obtain appropriate solvents to separate each SARA fraction [142,146]. In this test, the bitumen is diluted and coated on the chromarod, which uses four sharp peak signals to plot the chromatographic columns (the areas indicate the mass contents of bitumen components) [69,99].

## 6.6. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

SEM is a versatile technique that allows scanning and analyzing heterogeneous materials (both organic and inorganic) on a micrometer to nanometer scale [372–374]. The SEM employs low energy secondary electrons and backscattered electrons to measure topographical changes in the material’s surface $[ 7 5 { , } 3 7 2 { , } 3 7 5 { , } 3 7 6 ]$ . In this way, it is possible to obtain three-dimensional images showing the sample’s morphology in detail [372,377,378]. SEM has been widely used to evaluate changes in bituminous binders due to ageing, additive modification, and rejuvenation processes [62,379–381]. Several case studies have indicated that UV radiation causes significant damage to the bitumen morphology, evidenced by the evolution of typical surface morphology, such as wrinkles, cracks, pits, stomata, shallow grooves, and bumps $[ 7 5 , 1 0 4 , 1 0 5 , 1 4 8 , 1 6 8 ]$

## 6.8. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC)

As described in the next section of this manuscript, there is a growing interest in the literature to obtain modified bitumens resistant to UV ageing. However, additives and modifying agents can generate undesirable properties, such as inferior thermal stability (especially longterm). Hence, in several investigations, the thermal stability of bitumens is evaluated under different modification and ageing conditions implementing the TGA [98,123]. The TGA is a technique that monitors the mass changes of a sample (usually several grams) as a furnace ramps temperature (even up to $1 6 0 0 \; ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C ) }$ under a gas flow (stable or altering) [382–385]. This procedure allows obtaining different properties such as thermal behaviour, resistance to pyrolysis, activation energy, decomposition and reaction kinetics [123,386–388]. For bitumens, it usually draws a curve of mass lost ratio versus the temperature increases; in this way, low thermal stability is associated with abrupt and accelerated changes in that curve [98,123,148]. Within the variants of the TGA, the DSC stands out for its practicality and wide use in the study of petroleum-derived products [10,389–392]. This is because the DSC permits establishing correlations between the thermal performance and the physical properties of the substances; besides, it is the only direct method for determining the enthalpy of a specific process [393–395]. Notably, one of the most valuable properties of analyzing bitumen using DSC is the glass transition temperature (Tg). The Tg is the middletemperature point where glass transition occurs, i.e., when the material becomes brittle (less flexible) like “glass” and softens under the heating [48,244]. Thus, as the ageing of bituminous materials progresses, the Tg is lower [3,396]. Furthermore, another indicator of ageing that can be measured with the DSC is the transition of crystalline fractions [10,342]. As exposure to UV radiation increases, the content of crystalline structures also augments [50,148].

## 6.9. UV spectrophotometer test (UST)

In order to determine the depth to which UV radiation can penetrate the bitumen, [35] proposes implementing the UST. Fig. 20 shows this test in detail. The UST implement a custom device that operates following these steps:

• (i) select the type of UV radiation, i.e., wavelength and intensity.

• (ii) impact the UV radiation on the bituminous binder film.

• (iii) activate the UV detecting probe to measure the UV radiation that passes through the bitumen. If the signal is detected, the UV radiation manages to penetrate said thickness. Therefore, the test continues to evaluate a higher sample thickness.

• (iv) the thickness at which the signal is not detected indicates the maximum penetration to which UV radiation can penetrate.

## 6.10. Resume of tests to assess UV ageing

The literature review identifies that the evaluation of UV ageing of bitumen is more comprehensive and meticulous than its counterpart for bituminous mixtures. Specifically, for bituminous mixtures, only traditional properties such as Cantabro loss, dynamic modulus, indirect tensile strength, fatigue resistance, and accumulation of permanent deformation are typically assessed. Meanwhile, for bitumens, specialized tests are carried out to measure physical, chemical, rheological, microstructural, morphological, thermogravimetric, and surface topography changes.

## 7. Additives to mitigate the UV aging on bituminous materials

In order to preserve the road infrastructure and the financial investment, it is necessary to reduce or retard the ageing phenomena of bituminous materials [8,149,168,332]. However, although there is great interest in developing technologies to mitigate UV ageing, few research efforts have been made [115,128]. Remarkably, most of the investigation found in the literature focuses on evaluating the effectiveness of certain additives, such as antioxidants, inorganic nanoparticles, latex, layered silicates, polymers, tire rubber, ultraviolet absorbers, and warm-mix asphalt (WMA) additives [13,133,155,168]. Usually, the efficacy of these additives is measured simply by assessing the performance and behaviour of the bituminous materials before and after the modification procedure [117,118,137,318]. Additionally, the transmission electron microscope (TEM) and X-ray diffraction (XRD) tests are typically used to estimate the grade of incorporation of the modifying agents within the bituminous matrix [136,146]. Specifically, the TEM produces photomicrographs that permit establishing microstructural changes in the bitumen [146,397,398]. Meanwhile, through the XRD is possible to identify patterns of crystalline structures that provide detailed information on the degree of attached particles $[ 4 2 , 1 8 2 , 3 1 3 ]$ . The main anti-ageing additives are described below.

## 7.1. Layered double Hydroxides (LHDs)

Layered Double Hydroxides (LHDs) are a variety of clay, described as inorganic hydrotalcite-like compounds with host–guest supramolecular structures [154,162,163]. LDHs are metallic nanomaterials with a laminated structure [107,118,182]. Specifically, the LDHs are constituted by a multi-nestification layered structure composed of anions and laminate cations [119,141,142,144]. Typically, for the LHDs, the cations are metal hydroxide basal layers of brucite-like, and the anions are electrically balanced particles, along with hydration water molecules in the interlayer galleries [129,148,149]. Although the LDHs can be considerably different between each one, the general formula of these compounds is stated in [119,146,149] as follows: $\mathrm{Mg_{1-x}Al_{x}(OH)_{2}(CO_{3})_{x/}}$ 2mH2O; where $\mathbf { ^ { \circ } X } ^ { \flat }$ is the content variation of metallic elements (divalent and trivalent metal cations), and $`` \mathbf{m} ''$ is the quantity of crystal water.

The LHDs have been widely used as an additive to modify bitumen in the last years due to their excellent UV blocking performance [51,146,181]. In this way, the LDHs are an alternative to modifying the bitumens to extend the service life of asphalt pavements [107,141,144]. Although the specific effect of LDHs depends on their chemical composition, these influences can be summarized in betters thermal stability, flame retardancy, and UV photostability [148,154]. These improvements in the behaviour of bitumens are achieved due to two primary mechanisms: (i) the multistage layer interfaces of LDHs can effectively reflect and refract the UV rays, reducing the energy of UV radiation reaching the bituminous matrix [119,144,181,182]; and (ii) the metal elements on the layers and anions between the layers of the LDHs can chemically absorb the UV light and prevent it from being dispersed in nanophase, thus reducing photo-oxidative ageing [117,141,142,181]. Hence, the LHDs can control and mitigate the formation of carbonyl and sulfoxide groups in the bitumen during UV exposure [42,132,149,164]. Typical dosages of LDH are between 3 and 5 % by weight of bitumen [117–119,141]. Under these amounts, the LHDs augment the ductility, softening point, phase angle, and resistance to rutting and fatigue cracking [117,141,399]; meanwhile, the penetration and complex modules are decreased [91,118,119]. Overall, the LHDs mitigate the influence of UV irradiation in bituminous materials, i. $\mathbf { e } _ { \cdot , \cdot }$ drastically reducing its negative impacts [117,129]. Moreover, laboratory and field experiences confirm that the LDHs can reduce the ageing rate in bituminous mixtures exposed to weathering [51,181]. On the other hand, the LDHs have a minor impact on the contents of SARA fractions [154].

The bitumen molecules penetrate LDH interlayer galleries to create an expanded phase structure during the modifying process [145,146,172]. However, although LDHs are widely used as modifying agents, these additives have low chemical compatibility with bitumens [148,150,163,172]. The preceding is due to the enormous hydroxyl radicals onto LDHs laminate do not permit the uniformly and steadily dispersion of LDHs into the bituminous matrix $[ 1 0 7 , 1 7 5 , 1 8 2 ]$ . Therefore, various alternatives have been proposed in the literature to increase the compatibility between these two materials. On the one hand, use LHDs with appropriate particle size; in particular, 180 nm has been found as the proper powder mean diameter [42]. On the other hand, the introduction of organic and inorganic compounds in the chemical structure of the LDHs through etching treatments [32,150,175].

The layered structure and large surface area of LDHs allow that there can be easily modified with other substances [53]. Nevertheless, due to optical and physical properties, not all LDH modifying agents have the same compatibility with bitumen [149,161]. Overall, organic modifiers have higher compatibility with bitumen but develop lower UV ageing reducing capabilities than inorganic nanomaterials [148,172,173]. Besides, the nature of the base bitumen plays a fundamental role in the etching treatment efficiency [40,158]. Some of the additions typically employed to improve the LDHs behaviour and UV blocking performance in bitumen are: 2-hydroxybenzoic acid [148], crumb rubber (CR) [132], lignosulfonate [46,176], Mg-Al compounds [142,149], p-methycinnamic acid [145], sodium stearate [181,182], triethoxyvinylsilane [161,162], and Zn-Al compounds [142,149].

## 7.2. Polymers and Styrene-Butadiene-Styrene (SBS)

Polymers are materials composed of large molecular weight molecules (macromolecules) formed by the union via covalent bonds of monomers [133,400–402]. During the last years, polymers have been gaining popularity in the industry by their unique and matchless characteristics, i.e., low density, high strength, elevated tenacity, raised resistance to abrasion, and anti-corrosion properties [133,403–405]. There are many types of organic and inorganic polymers, each with particular features and applications [133,406,407]. Both classes of polymers are used in the transport infrastructure industry to achieve sustainability goals and extend the durability of the pavements [408–412]. Remarkably, in order to enhance the performance of bitumen for road applications, several types of polymers have been employed as modifying agents, for instance, ethylene butyl acrylate, ethylene vinyl acetate, polyethylene, polypropylene, polyvinyl chloride, styrene-butadiene rubber (SBR), styrene–butadienestyrene (SBS), styrene-(ethylene-co-butylene)-styrene, and styrene–isoprene–styrene [244,413–415]. Nonetheless, the polymer-modified bituminous binders suffer the ageing phenomena on the architecture of the whole material, i.e., both the polymer and the bitumen [331,416,417]. Therefore, not all polymers are resistant to the photo-oxidation process. Notably, some polymers in which there is a consensus about their benefits to minimising UV ageing of bituminous materials are CR [26], polysiloxanemodified-polyacrylates [133], SBR [418], and SBS [419]. However, the SBS is the polymer-like additive most utilised to improve the bitumen’s performance at high and low temperatures [5,24,33,70,136].

SBS is a triblock copolymer with thermoplastic elastomer properties that allow it to form a spatial three-dimensional network structure in bitumen by physical crosslink (with a star or linear structure) of polystyrene blocks and swelling of polybutadiene blocks after absorbing its lightweight components [7,39,136,267,420]. The typical SBS dosages are between 2 and 6 % by bitumen weight [7,47,85,109,350]. SBS modified bitumen provides superior high and low-temperature performance, thus giving bituminous materials with elevated resistance to rutting and fatigue cracking [173,236,240]. Furthermore, the SBS augments the resistance to UV ageing of bitumens via two mechanisms: mitigating the increase of carbonyl and sulfoxide groups and breaking the C––C double bond in butadiene structures [23,77,80,421]. Nonetheless, the influence of SBS on the UV irradiation resistance depends on the nature of the base bituminous binder [27,70,136]. Overall, the SBS modified bitumen has two central problems: (i) prolonged exposure to UV light can deteriorate the SBS copolymer, thus affecting the long-term performance of the bituminous materials [28,47,70,136,171,173]; and (ii) the outcome of inferior thermal stability (i.e., lowers storage stability and Tg) causes the bitumen to undergo accelerated light-oxidative ageing at high temperatures (approximately from $7 0  { \mathrm { ~ \textrm ~ { ~   ~ } ~ } } ^ { \circ }  { \mathrm { C } } )$ [5,24,134,136,151,419]. Therefore, there are possible scenarios in which the SBS fails to produce UV blocking effects correctly. Consequently, to diminish these undesired impacts, there is a broad trend in the literature to use SBS in combination with other additives, for instance, antioxidants [134], CR [422], flame retardants (decabromodiphenyl ether, antimony trioxide, and zinc borate) [135], graphene oxide [168], GW-944 [125], montmorillonite [7], nano-TiO2 [60], nano-ZnO [77], polyphosphoric acid [47], organic expanded vermiculite [39], organic layered silicate [136], organomontmorillonite [15], Trinidad Lake asphalt [80], UV absorbers [24], zinc dialkyldithiophosphate [5], among others.

## 7.3. UV absorbers and antioxidants

UV absorbers are chemical compounds that can absorb UV radiation, preventing the structure of the bitumen from receiving it [70,122,148,163]. Meanwhile, antioxidants are substances (which can be synthesized or extracted directly from raw materials) that reduce the reformation and restructuring of functional groups, thus mitigating the photo-oxidative ageing of bitumen [423–426]. The UV absorbers and antioxidants are usually employed simultaneously to diminish the UV ageing of bituminous materials $[ 5 , 1 1 4 ]$ . Nonetheless, the photostability generated by these materials depends on the origin and chemical composition of the base bitumen [122,128,143]. Therefore, this combination does not always benefit the modified bitumen’s performance and UV blocking capacity [114]. Similar to other anti-ageing technologies, UV absorbers and antioxidants decrease the susceptibility of bitumens to physical and rheological changes induced by UV radiation [126,148,152]. Furthermore, the literature review reveals that these materials have no standard dosage to modify bitumen due to the wide variability of combinations between chemical agents. Some examples of typical UV absorbers are bumetrizole [134], octabenzone [24], polysiloxane-modified-polyacrylates [133], tinuvin770 [156], among others. Likewise, some common antioxidants are irgafos168 [427], irganox1010 [126], and zinc dialkyldithiophosphate [428].

## 7.4. Warm-Mix asphalt (WMA) additives

WMAs are bituminous mixtures fabricated at $2 0  –  4 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ lower than the traditional HMAs, thus achieving economic and environmental benefits [429–431]. In order to accomplish such a reduction in mixing and compaction temperatures, it is necessary to modify the bitumens with WMA additives, which can be chemical, organic, or water-bearing additives [432–435]. Notably, WMAs have two anti-ageing effects. On the one hand, by requiring lower production temperatures, the bitumen suffers less thermal ageing and therefore, the volatilization of the lightweight components decreases [155,186,436]. On the other hand, the WMA additives usually incorporate chemical components that mitigate photo-oxidation mechanisms in the bitumen matrix [115,184,437]. Overall, the WMA additives diminish the negative behaviour that UV radiation induces in bitumen [23,68,165]. However, there is still no consensus in the literature about the influence of WMA additives in the UV ageing of bituminous materials [21].

## 7.5. Other technologies

Although most of the anti-ageing technology found in state of the art to prevent the photo-oxidative ageing of bituminous materials are LDHs, polymers, UV absorbers, antioxidants and WMA additives, other materials have also been explored to a lesser extent. For instance, bio-oils extracted from biomass [48], carbon black [82], graphene oxide [168], lanthanum oxide [64], rejuvenators [309], thermochromic powders [43], Trinidad Lake asphalt [140], and waste engine oil [72].

## 8. Summary and conclusions

A comprehensive review of the literature regarding UV ageing on bituminous materials was carried out in this research. Initially, a bibliometric analysis was conducted considering the investigations published in the last decade. Next, the concepts of the electromagnetic spectrum, bitumen chemistry, ageing phenomena, oxidation, and photooxidation of bituminous materials were addressed. Succeeding, the protocols to simulate the UV ageing on bitumen and bituminous mixtures were described. Besides, the UV ageing evaluation test and procedures were explained. Finally, this manuscript presents an overview of the main additives utilized to mitigate light-oxidative ageing. Based on the above, the following conclusions were drawn:

• Most investigations regarding UV ageing of bituminous materials have been conducted in PR China.

• Most of the research focuses on the study of UV ageing on bitumen, disregarding the bituminous mixtures. Furthermore, the few studies that concentrate on mixtures usually do not consider the aspects associated with the chemistry of the aggregates, the voids in the mixture, and the ratio of filler. Therefore, the preceding represents a gap in the literature because the conclusions made in the laboratory cannot be correctly extrapolated on the durability of asphalt pavements.

• In the literature, few investigation efforts have been developed for computational modelling of the UV ageing of bituminous materials. The preceding represents a significant research gap.

• There is no standard to simulate the UV ageing of bituminous materials. Besides, the protocols proposed by the researchers are significantly different. Hence, there is no consensus on the effects of UV radiation on the performance of bituminous binders and mixtures.

• There is a broad spectrum of tests and indices to evaluate UV ageing. Nevertheless, the use of these and their configuration is specific to each case study, which causes the appearance of multiple contradictions between the authors.

• The main additives to mitigate UV ageing are LHDs, SBS, UV absorbers, and antioxidants. These external agents improve the rheological performance and resistance to oxidation. Notably, anti-UV ageing technologies respond differently depending on the origin of the bitumen.

## 8.1. Challenges and perspectives

Throughout the manuscript, it was possible to demonstrate that the investigative development regarding UV ageing has focused on studying bitumens. Accordingly, relatively few investigations have been conducted on bituminous mixtures. This inequity in the literature is the main challenge to be addressed in future research efforts. Primarily, it is required that the theoretical background and conceptual models are scrutinised in succeeding studies. Thus, allowing the understanding of the stages, mechanisms, and processes related to the UV ageing of bituminous mixtures.

On the other hand, it is necessary to establish an international standard to simulate and evaluate UV ageing in bituminous materials. Ideally, separate standards should be conceived for bitumens and bituminous mixtures. In turn, each regulation should include laboratory simulation variables (i.e., temperature, duration, film thickness, intensity, and wavelength) that correspond to the specific context of the geographic region where the study is carried out. Similarly, in future research, it is necessary to reach a consensus on which physical, chemical, rheological, microstructural, morphological, thermogravimetric, and surface topographic properties are representative and proper for measuring the changes caused in the bituminous materials by the UV ageing phenomenon.

Due to the substantial variability of materials, environmental conditions, and traffic loads, it seems impossible at this point of the state-of-the-art development to create reliable computational models to describe the UV ageing of bituminous materials. Nonetheless, artificial intelligence techniques such as neural networks and evolutionary computation could provide sufficiently robust theoretical frameworks to initiate this branch of literature. However, to achieve these pursuits, it is crucial to have an extensive database that can capture the broad spectrum of the variables involved. Therefore, the global research community should make a collaborative effort to create and open-share these datasets.
