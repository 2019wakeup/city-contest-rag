# High-viscosity modified asphalt mixtures for double-layer porous asphalt pavement: Design optimization and evaluation metrics

## a b s t r a c t

Porous asphalt mixture has been extensively exploited as a surfacing layer on highways because it contributes to controlling pavement runoff, enhancing driving safety, mitigating urban-heat island, and reducing tire-pavement noise. This study proposes a methodology to design two types of highviscosity modified porous asphalt mixtures with target air voids of 20% and 22% (PAC-10 and PAC-16) for the double-layer porous asphalt pavement, involving in fabricating high-viscosity asphalt, formulating aggregate gradations, and optimizing asphalt-aggregate ratio. Firstly, high-viscosity asphalt was manufactured by incorporating high-viscosity modifier into the base asphalt by 12% and 15% (Base-12, Base-15) and crumb rubber-modified asphalt by 8% (CR-8). The physical properties, 60 C dynamic viscosity, rotational viscosity and viscoelastic properties of high-viscosity asphalt were characterized. Next, based on orthogonal design method, two aggregate gradations were optimized for PAC-10 and PAC-16 mixtures. Furthermore, the Draindown and Cantabro tests were carried to determine the optimum asphalt-aggregate ratios. Finally, the performance metrics of air void content, permeability coefficient, high-temperature and low-temperature performance, and moisture susceptibility was evaluated on PAC-10 and PAC-16 mixtures. The results show that with the addition of high-viscosity modifier into asphalt binder, the softening point, ductility, 60 C dynamic viscosity, rotational viscosity, complex shear modulus, phase angle, and rutting factor of the binder are increased while the penetration is decreased. The formula of voids volume of the mixture as a function of aggregate gradation is developed, which facilitates the determination of aggregate gradation. Furthermore, the optimum asphalt-aggregate ratio is found to be 4.7% for PAC-10 mixture and 4.1–4.4% for PAC-16 mixture. Both PAC-16 and PAC-10 mixtures with CR-8 are recommended to construct the double-layer porous asphalt pavement.

## 1. Introduction

<small><span class="docvortex-page-footnote" data-block-type="page_footnote" style="color:#6b7280">⇑ Corresponding author.</span></small>

Porous asphalt mixture is an environmentally friendly composite material with a design air voids volume of at least 20% and gap aggregate gradation [1]. Because of the high air void content and the interconnected void structure, porous asphalt mixture contributes to the environmental benefits of stormwater management, pollution control, improvement of skid resistance of the road and driving safety, mitigation of urban-heat island, and low tirepavement noise [2–8]. Nowadays, more than 90% of the main highway network in the Netherland has been surfaced with porous asphalt mixtures [9]. Conventional single-layer porous asphalt pavement has been reported to have an average service life of 11–12 years. In the late 1900 s, the double-layer porous asphalt pavement was proposed in the Netherlands [10]. Within the double-layer pavement structure, the upper layer embraces fine aggregates with smaller air voids and the lower layer incorporates coarse aggregates with larger air voids. With the novel structure, the double-layer porous asphalt pavement promotes the environmental and social benefits. Currently, this form of porous asphalt pavement has been implemented in several other countries [11]. However, the higher voids volume in the double-layer porous pavement induces high sensitivity to damage due to traffic and environmental effects, resulting in relatively low strength, inferior moisture stability and susceptibility to permanent deformation and raveling of porous asphalt mixture [12].

To address these issues, high-viscosity asphalt is proposed for improving overall properties of porous asphalt mixture. Compared with traditional asphalt, high-viscosity asphalt exhibits greater viscosity, toughness, and tenacity [13–15]. The high-viscosity asphalt enhances the adhesion characteristics with aggregates, which pre-vents fragmentation of asphalt mixture. High-viscosity asphalt is formulated by physical or chemical cross-linking between the modifier and asphalt binder. At present, the modifiers used for high-viscosity asphalt involves thermoplastic elastomers, fibers, nanometer, and cement [16–22]. These modifiers significantly boost the properties of asphalt binder and accordingly improve high-temperature stability and resistance to low-temperature cracking of porous asphalt pavement [23,24]. Among these modifiers, Tafpack-Super (TPS) is the most commonly utilized modifier in high-viscosity asphalt [25,26] The practical experience in Japan revealed that TPS modifier is the optimal modifier for porous asphalt pavement, leading to enhanced high-temperature stability, low-temperature cracking resistance and fatigue resistance of the pavement [27]. Except TPS modifier, crumb rubber (CR) reclaimed from end-of-life tyres, is an interesting and environmentallyfriendly alternative. CR-modified asphalt presents greater viscosity compared to conventional asphalt due to the digestion process of the rubber in asphalt [28–30]. It is claimed that CR-modified asphalt is widely used in porous asphalt pavement in Europe and the United States [31]. Shirini and Imaninasab demonstrated that CR-modified asphalt could improve rutting resistance and cohesion of porous asphalt mixture [32,33]. However, limited study has devoted to formulating and evaluating high-viscosity asphalt modified by both TPS and crumb rubber.

Another critical factor that influences performance of porous asphalt mixture is aggregate gradation. It is accepted that a strong skeleton in the mixture would benefit its resistance to repeated traffic loading. Thus, a favorable aggregate gradation is necessary for extension of service life of the mixture. The existing gradation design methods include the Marshall volume design method, the Bailey design method, and the intermittent gradation design method. The Marshall volume design method describes that the sum of volume in fine aggregates, mineral fillers, asphalt binder, and the voids is equal to the volume of the main skeleton gap [34,35]. Accordingly, the skeleton of coarse aggregates could be fully utilized, and meanwhile, fine aggregates could effectively fill the voids. The Bailey design method was proposed by Robert D. Bailey for asphalt mixture grading design in the early 1980 s [36,37]. The breakthrough of this method is to redefine the boundary point of coarse aggregates, and the nominal maximum particle size of 0.22 times is set as the first control sieve, which is different from the traditional dividing method. The intermittent gradation design is based on the particle interference theory. In order to achieve the maximum density, the spaces between the aggregates of the previous stage are filled by the aggregates of the next stage, and the remaining spaces are filled by the particles of the next stage. To avoid interference, the particle size used to fill the void is lower than the distance between gaps [38]. In this study, orthogonal design method is proposed to design a suitable aggregate gradation for porous asphalt mixture.

This study aims to formulating two types of high-viscosity modified porous asphalt mixtures (PAC-10 and PAC-16) for the doublelayer porous asphalt pavement, which involves in three parts: asphalt binder design, determination of aggregate gradation, optimization of asphalt-aggregate ratio. Firstly, three types of highviscosity asphalt are prepared and characterized. Next, based on orthogonal design method, two aggregate gradations were designed for PAC-10 and PAC-16 mixtures. Furthermore, the optimum asphalt-aggregate ratio is determined according to the results of the Draindown and Cantabro tests. Finally, the performance metrics of both porous asphalt mixtures were evaluated. Fig. 1 illustrates research program of this study.

## 2. Materials and methodology

## 2.1. Materials

The base asphalt binder (PG64-22) as well as crumb rubber (CR)-modified asphalt were selected to prepare high-viscosity asphalt binder in this study. The CR-modified binder was fabricated by adding 20% crumb rubber into the base binder, in which the rubber particles were fully dissolved and swelled with the special production process. Table 1 summarizes conventional properties of both base asphalt binder and CR modified binder. The aggregates used in this study were crushed Basalt. Conventional properties of the aggregates are shown in Table 2.

High-viscosity modifiers were used for high-viscosity asphalt, as shown in Fig. 2. They are spherical particles with yellow colors, density of 0.55 g/cm<sup>3</sup>, and size range of 2–3 mm. High-viscosity modifiers are mainly composed of thermoplastic rubber, resin, anti-aging additive, plasticizer and other components, which were produced in China.

## 2.2. Binder preparation

The high-viscosity asphalt was prepared as follows. Firstly, the base and CR-modified asphalt binders were heated to 160–170 ℃ and 170–180 ℃, respectively. Next, the high-viscosity modifier with appropriate dosage was added to the melted asphalt, which was then blended with a high-speed shear apparatus at 4000 r/ min for 30 min. Finally, the sample was kept at 180 ℃ for 30 min. As shown in Table 3, three types of high-viscosity asphalt (Base-12, Base-15, CR-8) were prepared by incorporating 12% and 15% high-viscosity modifier into the base binder and 8% modifier into CR-modified binder by weight, respectively.

## 2.4. Orthogonal design of aggregate gradation

In this study, the double-layer porous asphalt pavement was constructed with two asphalt mixtures, including PAC-10 with target air voids of 20% in the upper layer and PAC-16 with target air voids of 22% in the lower layer of the pavement. To maximize functionality of the double-layer porous asphalt pavement, aggregate gradation in the upper layer was finer than that in the lower layer. The maximum nominal particle size was assumed to be 9.5 mm and 13.2 mm in PAC-10 and PAC-16, respectively. In orthogonal design, the 9.5 mm, 4.75 mm, 2.36 mm, and 0.075 mm key sieving passing percentages in PAC-10 and the 13.2 mm, 9.5 mm, 4.75 mm, 2.36 mm, and 0.075 mm key sieving passing percentages in PAC-16 were considered as variables. The orthogonal parameters with levels are listed in Table 4. As noticed that total 9 and 16 groups of aggregate gradations were arranged for PAC-10 and PAC-16, respectively.

With orthogonal aggregate gradation, trial mixtures of PAC-10 and PAC-16 were designed based on the standard JTG F40-2004. The asphalt film thickness of 12 lm was adopted to calculate the asphalt-aggregate ratio, which is summarized in Table 4. It can be seen that the initial asphalt-aggregate ratio was estimated to be 3.5–6.7% and 3.7–5.9% in PAC-10 and PAC-16, respectively. Five Marshall specimens with the height of 63.5 mm and diameter of 101.6 mm were prepared for each mix design. For each specimen, the apparent specific density $( \mathbf { G _ { \mathrm { m b } } } )$ and the theoretical maximum specific density $( \mathbf { G _ { \mathrm { m m } } } )$ were measured, which were used to calculate air void content $( \mathbf { V _ { a } } ) .$ By establishing the relationship between $\nabla _ { \mathrm { a } }$ and aggregate gradation, aggregate gradation is optimized for asphalt mixture with target air voids.

## 2.5. Schellenberg Draindown and Cantabro tests

After optimizing aggregate gradation, the Schellenberg Draindown and the Cantabro tests were employed to determine the optimum asphalt content and associated asphalt-aggregate ratios for porous asphalt mixture. According to the JTG E20-2011, the leakage loss rate was tested by Schellenberg Binder Draindown Test, meanwhile the flying loss rate was measured by the Cantabro Test. The leakage loss rate is used to characterize the resultant mixture drainage property while the flying loss rate is adopted to assess the bond strength between asphalt and aggregates, indicating resistance to disintegration or raveling of the mixture. Both tests have been involved in mix design of porous asphalt mixture, which is widely implemented in Australia, South Africa, and some European countries [39].

The detailed steps for optimizing asphalt-aggregate ratio were as follows. (1) The Marshall samples of PAC-10 and PAC-16 with five asphalt-aggregate ratios were fabricated, in which asphaltaggregate ratios varied by ± 0.5% and ± 1.0%. (2) Cantabro test of the sample was carried out to get the relationship between the flying loss rate and asphalt-aggregate ratio while Draindown test was taken to get the relationship between the leakage loss rate and asphalt-aggregate ratio. (3) According to Chinese specification JTG F40-2004, the leakage loss value of porous asphalt mixture should be smaller than 0.3% while the flying loss be smaller than 20%. On the curves of the flying loss rate and the leakage loss rate, the corresponding limits of asphalt-aggregate ratios were identified. (4) The maximum asphalt-aggregate ratio within the reasonable range was selected as the optimum asphalt-aggregate ratio of the mixture.

## 2.6.1. Air void content

Since air void content plays an important role in affecting performance and functionalities of porous asphalt mixture, the total air void content and connected air void content of the sample were studied. Air void content of PAC-10 and PAC-16 samples was investigated by following the standard JTG E20 T0708. The dimension and weight of dry samples were firstly recorded and the weight of the sample immersed in the water was then measured. Both total air void content $( \mathbf { V _ { a } } )$ and connected air void content $( V )$ were calculated.

## 2.6.2. Permeability test

Given three slab specimens with dimensions of 300 mm $\times 3 0$ 0 mm  50 mm, an adapted falling-head permeameter was used in the laboratory in order to evaluate the permeability of porous asphalt mixtures. Fig. 3 shows the permeameter prototype and the samples. In the test, the permeameter was placed on the top surface of the sample and the gap between them was sealed with silicon. The 500 mL of water flowed into the slab specimen from the water tank and the water seepage time and the amount of water were recorded. The coefficient of permeability $( C _ { w } )$ was finally obtained.

## 2.6.3. High-temperature rutting test

According to JTG E20 T0719, the wheel tracking test was carried out to assess the ability to resist permanent deformation of porous asphalt mixtures. Three slab specimens with dimensions of 300 mm  300 mm  50 mm were fabricated for each asphalt mixture. Before testing, the sample was kept in the testing apparatus at $6 0   \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ for 5 h. The wheel load with 0.7 MPa was applied on the sample for 1 h and the loading rate was 42 times per minute. Rutting depth of each sample was recorded in the wheel tracking test. Meanwhile, dynamic stability was calculated to evaluate high temperature performance of asphalt mixture.

## 2.6.4. Low-temperature bending test

The cracking resistance of porous asphalt mixtures at low temperature was evaluated by ultimate strain at bending failures. Standard test method JTG E20 T0715 was followed to implement this test. The test was conducted by applying the load in the center of the beam specimen (250 mm  30 mm $\times \; 3 5$ mm) $\mathrm { a t }   - 1 0 \; \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ and loading rate was 50 mm/min. Maximum load $( P _ { B } )$ and the corresponding mid-span deflection (d) of the beam specimen were recorded. Flexural strength $( \bf { R } _ { \mathrm { { B } } } ) ,$ maximum tensile strain $\left( \varepsilon _ { B } \right)$ , and bending stiffness $( S _ { \mathrm { B } } )$ of the specimen were calculated.

## 2.6.5. Moisture susceptibility test

To study the moisture susceptibility of porous asphalt mixture, indirect tensile strength (ITS) test was employed according to JTG E20 T0729. For each mixture, Marshall samples were divided into control group and conditioned group. Four conditioned samples were retained in water for 0.5 h under vacuum environment and then undergo four freezing-thawing cycles, which consists of freezing process at 18 ℃ for 16 h and thawing procedure at 60 ℃ for 24 h. After that, both control and conditioned samples were soaked in a water bath of 25 ℃ for 2 h. In ITS test, the indirect tensile strength of both control and conditioned samples was measured. Accordingly, the indirect tensile stress ratio (ITSR) of the sample was evaluated as the ratio of the test results of conditioned samples to the control samples.

## 3. Results and discussion

## 3.1. Binder performance

## 3.1.1. Physical properties

Fig. 4 shows the basic physical performance of various asphalt binders, i.e., softening point, ductility and penetration depth. It is observed that as the dosage of high-viscosity modifier increases, the softening point and ductility of asphalt binders increase while the penetration decreases. As the amounts of high-viscosity modifier are 12% and 15%, the softening point is increased by 83% and 89%, the ductility is enhanced to 20.5 cm and 39.5 cm, the penetration depth is decreased by 38% and 42% for Base-12 and Base-15, respectively when comparing with the base binder. Considering CR-modified asphalt binder, the incorporation of 8% highviscosity modifier results in 40% higher softening point, 146% higher ductility, and 10% lower penetration of high-viscosity asphalt. The results indicate that high-viscosity modifier acts to improve high-temperature characteristic of asphalt binders. Meanwhile, the mixing of high-viscosity modifier could improve ductility and flexibility of asphalt binders at low temperature. In other words, the modifier could improve both high- and lowtemperature performance of asphalt binder simultaneously. The decreased penetration implies high-viscosity asphalt binder is relatively stiffer and could effectively improve the bonding and increase the strength of the mixture. This improvement might be explained by that the high-viscosity modifier forms a polymer network structure between the polymer in the modifier and the asphalt molecules. Furthermore, compared to Base-15, CR-8 shows 8% lower softening point, 36% lower ductility and 7% higher penetration. Thus, Base-15 presents better high-temperature and lowtemperature performance and is less stiff than CR-8. This might be explained by that crumb rubber affects the formation of network of high-viscosity modifier in the binder. It is concluded that high-viscosity modifier could improve the softening point, ductility and stiffness of both base and CR-modified asphalt binders.

## 3.1.2. 60 C dynamic viscosity

Fig. 5(a) illustrates 60 C dynamic viscosity of studied asphalt binders. It is noticed that Base-15 exhibits the highest dynamic viscosity value, followed by CR-8, Base-12, CR-modified asphalt binder and the base asphalt binder. With the increased concentration of high-viscosity modifier, the dynamic viscosity is enhanced by 150 times, 476 times, and 4 times for Base-12, Base-15, and CR-8, respectively. The incorporation of highviscosity modifier greatly increases dynamic viscosity of both base and CR-modified asphalt. According to the specification in Japan, the high-viscosity asphalt with dynamic viscosity of more than 20000 Pahs at 60℃ is regulated to improve the performance of porous asphalt pavement [40]. Due to the overload phenomenon of asphalt pavement in China, dynamic viscosity of more than 50000 Pahs at 60℃ is required. Hence, both Base-15 and CR-8 binders satisfy the technical requirement, which could potentially ensure the bonding ability between asphalt and aggregates and prolong service life of porous asphalt pavement.

## 3.1.3. Rotational viscosity

The rotational viscosity is measured to characterize the workability of asphalt binder, and the higher the viscosity, the higher the workability of the mixture. Fig. 5(b) illustrates the rotational viscosities of high-viscosity asphalt binders in a series of temperatures. The data indicates that CR-8 presents the highest viscosity value, followed by Base-15 and Base-12 binder. The remarkable increase in viscosity of CR-8 might be attributed to the fact that crumb rubber is almost insoluble and toughening, which enhances the friction of asphalt molecules in the binder.

Moreover, viscosity of three binders presents similar trend with temperature. As temperature rises from 135 ℃ to 190 ℃, the viscosity is reduced by 87%, 89%, and 90% for Base-12, Base-15, and CR-8, respectively. It is accepted that the rotational viscosity of high-viscosity asphalt binder shall not exceed 2.5 Pahs at 170 ℃, according the most recent specification in China. Obviously, both Base-12 and Base-15 binders meet the requirement while CR-8 might need higher construction temperature.

Viscoelastic performance is assessed by conducting temperature sweep tests from 46 to 88 ℃ with the increment of 6 ℃. The results are summarized in Fig. 6. Obviously, there is negligible difference in low temperature cracking resistance between PAC-10 and PAC-16 when using CR-8. Given PAC-10, the use of CR-8 increases flexural strength by 92% and maximum tensile strain by 30% in comparison with the utilization of Base-12. Considering PAC-16, the adoption of CR-8 leads to 117% higher flexural strength and 22% more tensile strain than the usage of Base-12. Hence, the combination of highviscosity modifier and crumb rubber improves viscosity of asphalt, contributing to the increased strength and flexibility of porous asphalt mixture than the individual high-viscosity modifier.

Fig. 10(b) illustrates the bending stiffness of porous asphalt mixtures at low temperature. It is noticed that PAC-16 with CR-8 exhibits the maximum stiffness, followed by PAC-10 with CR-8, PAC-10 with Base-12, and PAC-16 with Base-12. Compared to PAC-10, PAC-16 presents 23% lower stiffness when using Base-12 and 3% larger stiffness when using CR-8. In comparison with Base-12, the use of CR-8 leads to higher stiffness by 34% and 79% in PAC-10 and PAC-16, respectively. As a result, porous asphalt mixtures with CR-8 are harder and brittle, which has adverse effect on the flexibility of asphalt mixture at low temperature. However, CR-8 has enhanced viscosity of asphalt and has positive effect on low-temperature performance. In summary, both PAC-16 and PAC-10 with CR-8 are recommended to be applied to porous asphalt pavement in cold climate.

## 3.4.5. Moisture stability

Fig. 11 displays the indirect tensile strength ratio (ITSR) of porous asphalt mixtures and the error bars are the standard deviation of three test results. It is clear that PAC-16 with CR-8 features the maximum ITSR value, followed by PAC-10 with CR-8, PAC-10 with Base-12, and PAC-16 with Base-12. According to Chinese specification, the ITSR of porous asphalt mixture is specified at 85% to ensure great resistance to moisture damage. Thus, except PAC-16 with Base-12, other asphalt mixtures meet the specification requirement. The results also indicate that the use of CR binder greatly improves moisture stability of porous asphalt mixture. This might be attributed to that the adding crumb rubber and highviscosity modifier promotes the bonding characteristics between asphalt and aggregate. Accordingly, PAC-10 and PAC-16 with CR-8 shows remarkable resistance to moisture exposure condition.

## 4. Conclusions

This study proposes a methodology for design and evaluation of two types of porous asphalt mixtures (PAC-10 and PAC-16) with different target air voids, contributing to the future double-layer porous asphalt pavement. The methodology focuses on three main contributions that reflect different roles in the mixture: highviscosity asphalt, aggregate gradation and optimum asphaltaggregate ratio. With the optimized mixture compositions, Marshall samples of high-viscosity modified asphalt mixtures were prepared. The engineering performance of both PAC-10 and PAC-16 mixtures were evaluated. The following conclusions are drawn:

(1) After the addition of high-viscosity modifier into both base and CR-modified asphalt,

the softening point, ductility, 60 C dynamic viscosity and rotational viscosity increase while the penetration decreases.

(2) As the content of high-viscosity modifier increases, the complex shear modulus, phase

angle, and rutting factor of asphalt increase. Asphalt binder with both CR and high-viscosity modifier presents higher values of G\*, d and G\*/sind than the base asphalt binder modified with high-viscosity modifier.

(3) Two aggregate gradations are optimized for PAC-10 and PAC-16, which are utilized in the upper and lower layer of the double-layer porous asphalt pavement. The gradation in PAC-10 is finer than that in PAC-16. Furthermore, the optimum asphalt-aggregate ratios are identified to be 4.7% in PAC-10 and 4.1–4.4% in PAC-16.

(4) PAC-16 exhibits higher air void content, connected air void content, and coefficient of permeability than PAC-10.

(5) The incorporation of high-viscosity modifier significantly enhances rutting resistance, low-temperature performance, and moisture stability of porous asphalt mixture.

In summary, PAC-16 with CR-8 and PAC-10 with CR-8 are recommended to construct the double-layer porous asphalt pavement. Further study is suggested to optimize structure design and analyze infiltration characteristics and clogging behavior as well as long-term performance of the double-layer porous asphalt pavement.
