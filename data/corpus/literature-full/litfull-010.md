# Review of the Superpave performance grading system and recent developments in the performance-based test methods for asphalt binder characterization

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Asphalt binder Superpave Performance grading Rutting Fatigue cracking Thermal cracking Workability

## A B S T R A C T

Asphalt binder plays an important role in the overall resistance of an asphalt mixture to pavement distresses. The rheological properties should be fully characterized before it is put into field use. Usually, a grading system is employed to evaluate and select the appropriate asphalt binder. The Superpave performance grading (PG) system made the first attempt to directly relate the measured physical properties of asphalt binders to field performance with due consideration of pavement conditions. Conceptually, this approach represents a significant substantial advancement over the traditional penetration and viscosity grading systems. Novel tests and specifications were devised in the Superpave PG system to address the performance requirements. This paper provides a review of the Superpave PG system with respect to workability, rutting resistance, fatigue cracking resistance, and thermal cracking resistance. Although the Superpave PG system is purported to be superior to the traditional grading systems, some deficiencies have been reported regarding the test methods and corresponding evaluation indicators. The Superpave PG system can be further refined if these deficiencies can be addressed effectively. Therefore, the second objective of this paper is to summarize the recent developments in performance-based test methods for asphalt binder characterization. Potential specification tests can be selected from those performancebased test methods in order to refine the Superpave PG system.

## 1. Introduction

Asphalt mixture is a composite material that consists of asphalt binder, aggregates and filler. It is widely utilized as a paving material for construction of urban roads and highways. Asphalt pavements may experience several types of distresses, such as rutting, fatigue cracking, and thermal cracking, due to the combined effect of traffic loads and environmental condition [1,2]. Those distresses deteriorate the pavement condition and reduce the service life of asphalt pavements. As the bonding agent between aggregate particles, asphalt binder plays an important role in the overall resistance of asphalt mixture to pavement distresses. Therefore, the rheological properties of asphalt binder should be fully characterized before it is placed into field use.

Usually, a grading system is employed to evaluate the performance and select the appropriate asphalt binder. There are three main grading systems currently in worldwide use: penetration grading system, viscosity grading system, and Superpave performance grading (PG) system [3–6]. The penetration grading system and the viscosity grading system take the penetration test and the viscosity test as the key characterization test, respectively [3,4]. The penetration test is empirical in nature and performed at a constant temperature of 25 ◦C. The non-Newtonian behavior of asphalt binders tested at 25 ◦C influences the penetration test results due to the varying shear rate observed during the test [1,7]. Unlike penetration depth, viscosity is a fundamental engineering parameter rather than an empirical parameter. However, original asphalt binders of the same grade may exhibit substantially different behaviors after pavement construction because of the viscosity variations induced by short-term aging [1,7]. It has been identified that the penetration grading system and the viscosity grading system have three major limitations, which are summarized as follows [8]:

(1) Both grading systems use many empirical tests. There is no direct relationship established between the measured physical properties and field performance. Filed experience is required to interpret the test results;

(2) Neither the penetration grading system nor the viscosity grading system takes the long-term aging into consideration. Simulation of long-term aging in the laboratory is crucial to the investigation of fatigue cracking and thermal temperature cracking of asphalt pavements after a long service period; and

(3) The pavement conditions, which include temperature, traffic speed, traffic volume, and pavement structure, are not fully considered. However, the pavement conditions have a great effect on the pavement performance.

With the awareness of the limitations of these grading systems, the Superpave PG system was developed in the United States as part of the Strategic Highway Research Program (SHRP) in the early 1990s [9]. The Superpave PG system consists of two temperature limits, for example PG 70− 22, in which the upper temperature and the lower temperature are determined by the average seven-day maximum pavement design temperature (◦C) and the minimum pavement design temperature (◦C), respectively [5,6]. The Superpave PG system made the first attempt to directly relate the measured physical properties of asphalt binders to the field performance of asphalt pavements with due consideration of aging and pavement conditions. Novel tests and specifications were devised to address the performance requirements, including rutting resistance, fatigue cracking resistance, and thermal cracking resistance [10–19]. It has been demonstrated by many researchers that use of the Superpave PG system can characterize asphalt binders more accurately and comprehensively.

The penetration depth versus time curves of two asphalt binders are presented in Fig. 1 as an example to demonstrate the advantages of the Superpave PG system over the traditional grading systems based on laboratory test results. Of these two asphalt binders, asphalt binder A is a conventional asphalt binder, and asphalt binder B is an SBS-modified asphalt binder. Fig. 1 shows that asphalt binders A and B have close penetration depth at an intermediate temperature of ${ \tt 2 5 ~ ^ { \circ } C } .$ When the penetration grading system is adopted, one may erroneously expect that the two binders have the same characteristics at low and high temperatures because the penetration grading system does not provide information for the entire range of typical pavement temperatures. However, the Superpave PG system tells a different story. According to the PG specification, asphalt binders A and B are graded as PG 64− 10 and PG 76− 22, respectively. The different PGs indicate that asphalt binder B outperforms asphalt binder A in terms of rutting resistance and thermal cracking resistance. This example explicitly highlights the advantages of the Superpave PG system over the traditional grading system. Although the penetration grading system and the viscosity grading system are still being widely used around the globe, more and more countries realize the advantages of the Superpave PG system and have the desire to implement the Superpave PG system for asphalt binder grading and selection [8,20–29].

There is still room for refinement of the Superpave PG system although it represents an important advancement over the traditional grading systems. With the implementation of the Superpave PG system in the United States, a number of state highway agencies identified some critical gaps with respect to the test methods and evaluation indicators [30–34]. Most of the gaps were related to the performance evaluation of modified asphalt binders because the Superpave PG system was primarily developed based on the study of neat asphalt binders with no polymer additives [35]. Many new test methods were therefore developed to tackle with these gaps due to polymer modified asphalt binders. As for the Superpave rutting parameter $| G ^ { \star } | / \sin \delta \left( | G ^ { \star } | \right)$ is the dynamic shear modulus and δ is the phase angle), a good summary of its main problems can be found in Delgadillo et al., which include fully reversed load used in testing, derivation from total dissipated energy based on linear viscoelastic theory, insufficient number of cycles for testing, and grade bumping for traffic speed and volume [34]. The middle two problems with |G\*|/sin δ also apply to the Superpave fatigue parameter | $G ^ { \star }$ sin δ. In addition, Bahia et al. pointed out that fatigue in asphalt pavements started and propagated in the asphalt binder; however, $| G ^ { \star } |$ sin δ was derived based on the dissipated energy concept and measured in the linear viscoelastic range using small strains [36]. In this sense, | G\*|sin δ may not be able to reflect the true damage induced in the asphalt binder. Their study showed a poor correlation between $| G ^ { \kappa } | / \sin$ δ and mixture fatigue indicators and presented a need to control a binder-associated damage in the binder specification [36]. In terms of thermal cracking evaluation, the BBR specification was found in many cases to underestimate the low temperature performance of some modified asphalt binders in spite of improved performance indicated by field experience [35].

Thus, the importance of the Superpave PG system is not the specific test methods, but rather the idea that properties which relate directly to performance should be used to classify and characterize asphalt binders. Seen in this way, it is logical to expect enhancements and improvements would occur over time as properties that more directly relate to performance are identified. In the past many years, continuous efforts have been devoted into a realistic modification of the Superpave PG system in order to establish a more uniform and more mechanistic asphalt binder testing scheme. Many performance-based/related test methods were developed as potential amendments to the Superpave PG system. A successful example is the multiple stress creep recovery (MSCR) test, which has been adopted in the latest Superpave PG system to grade the high temperature performance of asphalt binders [37,38]. It is important to have a good understanding of the recently developed performance-based test methods which point out the evolution direction of the Superpave PG system.

The objective of this paper is to make a comprehensive review of the Superpave PG system and summarize the recent developments in performance-based test methods for asphalt binder characterization. In preparing this paper, the authors reviewed a wealth of papers, reports, and summaries. It was found that there is a distinct lack of a comprehensive review of the up-to-date test methods regarding all of the Superpave requirements, including workability, rutting resistance, fatigue cracking resistance, and thermal cracking resistance. Such a literature review provides readers with an insight into the idea behind the Superpave PG system and how the PG system is evolving, and is therefore useful for the implementation of the Superpave PG system in potential countries. This review paper is intended to fill this gap.

This paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the basic logic and the characterization tests in the Superpave PG system. Sections 3 describes the performance-based test methods newly developed in recent years after the implementation of the Superpave PG system. Section 4 presents a discussion on the recent developments in performance-based test methods. The final section summarizes the major findings of this paper.

## 2. Superpave PG system

## 2.1. Basic logic behind Superpave PG system

The basic logic behind the Superpave PG system is that the properties of an asphalt binder should be linked to the climatic conditions of the area where the asphalt pavement will be built. Before pavement construction, the air temperatures of target region are firstly collected, and pavement temperatures are then estimated from the collected data of air temperatures using an algorithm provided by the long-term pavement performance (LTPP) program $[ 5 , 6 ]$ . According to AASHTO M 320 and ASTM D6373, the seven-day maximum pavement design temperature and the minimum pavement design temperature are identified and utilized to determine the upper and lower temperature limits. An asphalt binder must pass a set of characterization tests within the determined temperature limits so that it can be applied in pavement construction. It should be noted that most states in the US nowadays adopt the algorithm developed by Mohseni et al. to select asphalt binder performance grades [39]. The new algorithm uses degree days over $1 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C }$ as the climatic value in a transfer function for high temperature grade. The transfer function was developed by using the integrated climatic model and the mechanistic-empirical pavement design (MEPDG) rutting concepts [40].

In addition to the climatic conditions, aging is another important factor emphasized in the Superpave PG system. Characterization tests are performed on original asphalt binder, rolling thin film oven (RTFO) residue, and pressurized aging vessel (PAV) residue, which simulate the conditions of no aging, short-term aging, and long-term aging, respectively [5,6]. When an asphalt binder is subjected to short-term aging, a moving film of this binder is heated in an oven at a temperature of 163 $^ \circ \mathrm { C }$ for 85 min [16,17]. The allowable mass change is no larger than 1% after the test is completed. As for the long-term aging treatment, RTFO residue is placed in a vessel pressurized with air to 2.1 MPa and aged at a specified conditioning temperature for 20 h [18,19]. The conditioning temperature can be selected as 90, 100, or $\begin{array} { r } { 1 1 0 \; ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } , } \end{array}$ depending on the high temperature grade. After aging treatment, the dynamic shear rheometer (DSR) and the bending beam rheometer (BBR) can be employed to perform rheological tests on the aged specimens. As a result, the properties of an asphalt binder throughout its service life can be fully characterized using the three aging conditions.

Another unique feature of the Superpave PG system lies in the way of establishing criteria and requirements at the three aging conditions. In the penetration grading system and the viscosity grading system, it is common practice to perform a test at a constant temperature and vary the specified value [3,4]. As an example, AC–2.5 and AC–5 have varying specified viscosities at the same temperature of $6 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C } .$ In contrast, the specified value keeps constant in the Superpave PG system while the test temperature varies at which this value must be achieved $[ 5 , 6 ]$ . For instance, PG 64− 16 and PG 70− 22 meet the same performance requirements, but do so over different temperature ranges.

In summary, the Superpave PG system differs from the traditional grading systems by relating the asphalt binder properties to field performance with due consideration of climatic conditions and aging and adopting a new way of establishing specifications and criteria.

## 2.2. Characterization tests in Superpave PG system

The kernel of any grading system lies in the characterization tests. As introduced earlier, the Superpave PG system employs a series of specially designed tests to characterize the asphalt binder properties. Design of these tests and specifications is driven by the performance requirements that are encountered during the production of asphalt mixtures as well as the construction (lay-down) and service of asphalt pavements. Three major performance requirements are addressed by these characterization tests, which are rutting resistance, fatigue cracking resistance, and thermal cracking resistance. Linear viscoelastic theory was applied to establishing the evaluation indicators. In addition to the performance requirements, safety and workability are also crucial factors when selecting appropriate asphalt binders. The following four subsections presents a description of all characterization tests in the Superpave PG system.

## 2.2.1. Test methods for evaluating safety and workability

Safety and workability are crucial factors for selection of appropriate asphalt binders. In the Superpave PG system, safety is quantified by the flash point, which is measured by the Cleveland open cup tester [41,42]. The flash point should be no less than $2 3 0 \; ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } \; [ 5 , 6 ]$

The workability requirement is used to ensure that an asphalt binder is sufficiently fluid so that it can be pumped and mixed easily during the pavement construction [8]. In the Superpave PG system, the rotational viscometer (RV) is adopted to determine the rotational viscosity of an asphalt binder at a high temperature $( \mathrm { i . e . , } ~ 1 3 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ [43,44]. A resisting torque between 10 and 98% of the full-scale instrument capacity is desired during the rotational viscosity test, which can be adjusted through the selection of appropriate rotation speed and/or measuring geometry. The expected rotational viscosity should be no larger than 3 Pa⋅s $[ 5 , 6 ]$

## 2.2.2. Test method for evaluating rutting resistance

The DSR test at high temperatures is used to evaluate the rutting resistance of an asphalt binder. Both unaged and RTFO aged specimens are subjected to sinusoidal loading at a constant angular frequency of 10 rad/s $[ 1 0 , 1 1 ]$ . The output parameters of the DSR test are dynamic shear modulus and phase angle. Of these two parameters, the dynamic shear modulus indicates the stiffness and the phase angle describes the lag between shear stress and shear strain. A hysteresis loop forms in the stress–strain domain, and the enclosed area is an indicator of dissipated strain energy (DSE). For an asphalt binder subjected to stress-controlled sinusoidal loading, the DSE within one loading cycle can be calculated by $\operatorname { E q . }$ (1):

$$
W = \pi \tau _ { 0 } \gamma _ { 0 } s i n \delta\tag{1}
$$

where: W is the DSE; $\tau _ { 0 }$ is the shear stress amplitude; and $\gamma _ { 0 }$ is the shear strain amplitude.

Based on the DSE concept, the Superpave rutting parameter was proposed with an assumption that all strain energy is dissipated to resist permanent deformation [7,45]. As the shear strain amplitude equals the ratio of shear stress amplitude to dynamic shear modulus, Eq. (1) can be rearranged as Eq. (2). For a given shear stress amplitude, $\| G ^ { \dot { \kappa } } \| .$ /sin δ can be used to compare the DSE generated in different materials and is therefore employed as the rutting parameter in the Superpave PG system.

$$
W = \pi \tau _ { 0 } ^ { 2 } \frac { s i n \delta } { | G ^ { * } | }\tag{2}
$$

Generally, the lower the DSE is, the less permanent deformation an asphalt binder accumulates during the sinusoidal loading. As a result, an asphalt binder with a larger value of $| G ^ { \star } |$ /sin δ is preferred. The Superpave PG system limits the value of $| G ^ { \star } |$ /sin $\delta$ to a minimum of 1 kPa for original asphalt binder and to a minimum of 2.2 kPa for RTFO aged asphalt binder [5,6]. Two high temperature grades can be obtained accordingly, and the lower one is adopted as the final high temperature grade.

## 2.2.3. Test method for evaluating fatigue cracking resistance

The DSR test at intermediate temperatures is adopted to measure the ability of an asphalt binder to resist fatigue cracking. Sinusoidal loading is imposed on PAV aged specimens to obtain the dynamic shear modulus and phase angle at an angular frequency of 10 rad/s [10,11]. An intermediate temperature is selected because it is at these temperatures fatigue cracking is likely to occur in asphalt pavements. In the Superpave PG system, the intermediate temperature is dependent on the pre-determined high temperature grade and low temperature grade, as presented in Eq. (3). The PAV aging is necessary because the critical stage for an asphalt pavement to produce fatigue cracking is in its later period of service life when it has experienced long-term aging [1].

$$
T_{I} = \frac{T_{H} + T_{L}}{2} + 4\tag{3}
$$

where: $T _ { I }$ is the intermediate temperature; $T _ { H }$ is the high temperature grade; $T _ { L }$ is the low temperature grade; and the subscripts $I ,   H ,$ and L stand for intermediate, high, and low, respectively.

Analogous to the derivation of $| G ^ { \star } | / \sin \delta ,$ the Superpave fatigue parameter was also developed based on the DSE concept shown in Eq. (1). When an asphalt binder is subjected to strain-controlled sinusoidal loading, Eq. (1) can be rewritten as follows for a given shear strain amplitude:

$$
W = \pi \gamma _ { 0 } ^ { 2 } | G ^ { * } | s i n \delta\tag{4}
$$

An assumption is made that the whole strain energy is dissipated to overcome fatigue cracking [7,45]. Therefore, |G\*|sin δ gives an indication of the fatigue cracking resistance of tested asphalt binder. According to Eq. (4), a smaller value of $| G ^ { \star } |$ sin δ results in less dissipation of strain energy. Therefore, the Superpave PG system limits the value of | $G ^ { \star }$ |sin δ to a maximum of 5000 kPa for PAV aged asphalt binder [5,6].

## 2.2.4. Test methods for evaluating thermal cracking resistance

The BBR and the direct tension (DT) tester are the two apparatuses commonly adopted to measure the ability of an asphalt binder to resist thermal cracking and to determine the low temperature grade [12–15]. PAV aged asphalt binder samples are prepared because long-term aging represents the most critical condition for an asphalt pavement at low temperatures [7]. The BBR test is essentially a creep test, which is performed on small asphalt binder beams in the configuration of threepoint bending. A constant load of 980 mN is applied to the midpoint of the asphalt binder beam for a duration of 240 s, and the resulting deflection is recorded in real-time [12,13].

It is intuitive that an asphalt binder will crack when a load is applied at very low temperatures if its stiffness becomes too large. Moreover, as a viscoelastic material, an asphalt binder has the ability to relax applied stresses if given enough time [46]. Thus, the creep stiffness S(t) and the slope of log creep stiffness versus log time m(t) are utilized concurrently to estimate the low temperature properties of asphalt binders, which are calculated as Eqs. (5) and (6). The low temperature grade is then determined by the creep stiffness at $6 0 \; \mathrm { s } \left( \mathrm { i . e . } , S ( 6 0 ) \right)$ and the m–value at $6 0 \mathrm { ~ s ~ } ( \mathrm { i . e . } ,$ m(60)). In the Superpave PG system, S(60) is limited to a maximum of 300 MPa and m(60) is limited to a minimum of 0.3 [5,6].

$$
S ( t ) = \frac { P L ^ { 3 } } { 4 b h ^ { 3 } \delta ( t ) }\tag{5}
$$

$$
m ( t ) = \frac { \mathrm { d } l o g [ S ( t ) ] } { \mathrm { d } l o g ( t ) }\tag{6}
$$

where: P is the applied constant load; $L , b ,$ and h are the length, width, and depth of test specimen, respectively; and δ(t) is the measured deflection of test specimen.

Though most agencies do not adhere to the formal requirement, AASHTO M 320 requires that when S(60) falls in the range of 300–600 MPa, the DT test should be employed to determine the fracture properties of PAV aged asphalt binder [5,6]. The uniaxial DT test is performed on a dog-bone-shaped specimen at a constant strain rate of 3%/ min, which is capable of producing an elongation rate of 1 mm/min [14,15]. The DT test is terminated when the specimen fractures or after the elongation of the specimen reaches 10%, whichever comes first. The tensile stress and tensile strain generated in the test specimen are monitored during the test. The maximum tensile stress and the associated tensile strain are identified as the failure stress and failure strain, respectively, when the test specimen fractures. In accordance with the Superpave PG system, the failure strain should be no less than 1% [5,6]. It should be noted that the m–value requirement should always be satisfied regardless of the test type.

## 2.3. Recent development in Superpave PG system: MSCR test

## 2.3.1. Test protocol and analysis

It has been demonstrated by many researchers that |G\*|/sin δ fails to correctly grade the high temperature performance of modified asphalt binders [30,34,47]. On the basis of the repeated creep and recovery (RCR) test [36], the MSCR test was originally designed by D’Angelo et al. in order to develop a new high temperature specification parameter that was performance-based and blind to modification type [48]. For a better understanding of the stress–strain dependency of modified asphalt binders, the MSCR test was conducted on a single asphalt specimen obtained from RTFO residues at 11 stress levels ranging from 25 Pa to 25,600 Pa. The creep and recovery test at each stress level was repeated for 10 cycles, and no rest period was added between adjacent cycles. Within each cycle, a creep load was maintained for a duration of 1 s and then removed to allow for possible strain recovery for $9 \; s .$ The induced strain was recorded in real-time using a DSR, as illustrated in Fig. $2.\mathrm{In}\ \mathrm{Fig.}\ 2,\gamma_{1}$ is the shear strain at the start of the creep portion, $\gamma _ { 2 }$ is the shear strain at the end of the creep portion, and $\gamma _ { 3 }$ is the shear strain at the end of the recovery portion.

As is known, a specification test should be easy and quick to run. Therefore, when adopted in the AASHTO and ASTM standards, the MSCR test is only performed at two stress levels, namely 0.1 and 3.2 kPa, in order to effectively reduce the test duration [49,50]. Similar to the original MSCR test protocol, 10 cycles are used at each stress level in the standard MSCR test, which result in a total of 20 cycles lasting for 200 s. For performance-graded asphalt binders, the high temperature grade determined by $| G ^ { \star } |$ /sin δ is typically specified as the test temperature of the MSCR test [49,50].

Since then, the standard MSCR test has been widely utilized to characterize the rutting resistance of asphalt binders. However, many researchers have reported that the strain response could have significant variations from cycle to cycle for some modified asphalt binders [51–53]. As pointed out by Golalipour et al., before calculating the strain response, pre-conditioning cycles should be included in the MSCR test so that a steady state can be reached, in which the rate of strain accumulation keeps constant with the growth of creep-recovery cycles [51]. With the development of the MSCR test, 10 more cycles are added at the stress level of 0.1 kPa following the guidelines detailed in AASHTO T 350 and ASTM D7405 [54,55]. ## 3. Recent developments in performance-based test methods

Although the Superpave PG system outperforms the penetration grading system and the viscosity grading system, some deficiencies have been reported regarding the test methods corresponding to workability and the three performance requirements $( \mathbf { i . e . } ,$ rutting resistance, fatigue cracking resistance, and thermal cracking resistance). Researchers in the pavement society have been endeavoring to remedy or reduce these deficiencies by developing new test methods. The following four sections describe the deficiencies existing in the current Superpave test methods and present the performance-based test methods newly developed in recent years. Those new test methods were selected based on the following criteria: (1) whether the test method has received substantial attention and been largely investigated by researchers around the world; and/or (2) whether the test method provides a basis for the currently widely accepted test method; and/or whether (3) whether the test method has significant improvement compared with previous test methods.

## 3.1. Test methods for determining mixing and compaction temperatures

The workability of asphalt binders is related to the mixing and compaction temperatures of asphalt mixtures. The conventional method of determining the mixing and compaction temperatures is to measure the rotational viscosity of an asphalt binder at different temperatures using an RV [43,44]. A temperature-viscosity relationship can be established to estimate two temperatures at the viscosities of $0 . 1 7 \pm$ 0.02 and $0 . 2 8 \pm 0 . 0 3$ Pa⋅s, which are then utilized for the mixing and compaction of asphalt mixtures [57]. According to the Superpave PG system, the rotational viscosity of an asphalt binder should be limited to a maximum of 3 Pa⋅s in order not to yield excessively high temperatures for mixing and compaction [4,5].

It has been reported that the conventional method is very likely to yield unreasonably high mixing and compaction temperatures for modified asphalt binders [58,59]. This result occurs because most of the modified asphalt binders exhibit shear thinning or pseudo-plastic behavior, in which the measured viscosity depends on the applied shear rate [60,61]. Excessively high temperatures for mixing and compaction can result in performance degradation of asphalt binders and emission problems during the production and placement of asphalt mixtures in the field. As a consequence, many highway agencies defer to the polymer suppliers to set mixing and compaction temperatures. Although asphalt binder suppliers have their own methods to provide adequate guidance to users, there are apparent inconsistencies in the recommended temperatures by different suppliers [62]. Thus, a reliable and standardized method applicable for both unmodified and modified asphalt binders is required for determination of the mixing and compaction temperatures. In recent years, some new test methods have been developed, such as the zero shear viscosity (ZSV) method [36], the high shear rate viscosity (HSRV) method [58], the steady state flow (SSF) method [63], the phase angle method [62], and the rheogrambased test method [64]. The following subsections describe these new test methods in detail.

## 3.1.1. Zero shear viscosity method

The conventional method of estimating the mixing and compaction temperatures assumes asphalt binders as Newtonian fluids with negligible dependency on shear rate. However, Bahia et al. found that this assumption was invalid for the majority of modified asphalt binders tested in their study and concluded that the shear rate dependency should be considered because of its significant impact on the binder behaviors [36,59]. In the National Cooperative Highway Research Program (NCHRP) report 459, Bahia et al. introduced the concept of ZSV and recommended its use in determinization of the mixing and compaction temperatures [36]. They argued that the shear rate during compaction was very low in the Superpave gyratory compactor (SGC). According to their method, the ZSV values at different temperatures were determined from rheological models and then used to predict the mixing and compaction temperatures corresponding to viscosities of 3 and 6 Pa⋅s, respectively [36]. The new target viscosity values were developed based on the criterion that the compaction temperature should be less than $1 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C } .$

In order to simplify the determination of ZSV, Bahia et al. proposed to replace the ZSV with the low shear viscosity (LSV) measured at 0.001 $s ^ { - 1 } \; [ 3 6 ]$ . They believed that the same results of mixing and compaction temperatures could be obtained when this shear rate was adopted. To further simply the process of viscosity measurements, Bahia et al. attempted to use the viscosities measured at $6 . 8 ~ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ to calculate the mixing and compaction temperatures [36]. The shear rate $6 . 8 ~ s ^ { - 1 }$ was selected because it corresponds to a rotational speed of 20 rpm with Brookfield Spindle 27 which is commonly used in the RV testing. New target viscosity values were established, namely $0 . 7 5 \pm 0 . 0 5$ and 1.4 ± 0.1 Pa⋅s for mixing and compaction temperatures [36]. This simplification was considered a good approximation of the ZSV method when the shear rate dependency measurements were unavailable. According to NCHRP report 459, a reduction of approximately $4 0   ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C }$ in the mixing and compaction temperatures were produced when the proposed method was applied instead of the conventional method [36].

The ZSV method was later investigated by a number of studies [62,65–67]. Yang and Haddock collected field data to verify the applicability of the ZSV method [65]. A total of eight asphalt binders were tested, including four Newtonian asphalt binders and four non-Newtonian asphalt binders. Their study showed that the ZSV method appeared to be a viable method for non-Newtonian asphalt binders but did not appear to work well for Newtonian asphalt binders. It was also found that use of the mixing and compaction temperatures predicted by the ZSV method did not change the optimum binder contents for asphalt mixtures containing modified binders. West et al. questioned the use of low shear rate in the ZSV method and pointed out the difference between the one-dimensional vertical strain rate of asphalt mixture and the strain rate of the binder films [62]. A shear rate of $5 0 6 ~ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ was estimated for the first gyration compaction, and the shear rate slightly reduced with the continuation of compaction. They argued that the ZSV method might not accurately characterize the shear thinning behavior of modified asphalt binders and results for some binders yielded unrealistically low mixing and compaction temperatures.

## 3.1.2. High shear rate viscosity method

With the awareness of the shear thinning behavior of modified asphalt binders, Yildirim et al. proposed the HSRV method to determine the temperatures for mixing and compaction [58]. This method was developed based on the idea that very high temperatures are not necessarily required at the high shear rates occurring during mixing and compaction because the shear viscosity decreases with the growth of shear rate. Fig. 3 provides a comparison of two asphalt binders to depict the idea behind the HSRV method based on the discussion in Yildirim et al. [68]. In Fig. 3, Binder X is an unmodified asphalt binder whose shear viscosity is independent of shear rate, and Binder Y is a polymer modified asphalt binder which exhibits typical shear thinning behavior. The shear viscosity of Binder X at $6 . 8   s ^ { - 1 }$ is the same as that at the shear rate during compaction. In contrast, a much lower shear viscosity of Binder Y is observed at the shear rate during compaction in comparison to the shear viscosity at $6 . 8   \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ , which reduces the required compaction temperature. Therefore, the conventional method overestimates the compaction temperature of modified asphalt binders.

In the study conducted by Yildirim et al., a practical shear rate on asphalt binders during compaction in the SGC was found to be approximately $4 9 0 ~ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ and the average value for standard error was approximately $5 3 ~ s ^ { - 1 }$ [68]. The HSRV method was then developed which consisted of three steps [58]. Specifically, an RV was first employed to measure the rotational viscosities of an asphalt binder at shear rates ranging from 0.1 to $9 3   s ^ { - 1 }$ at 135 and $1 6 5 \; ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C } .$ After that, the measured data of shear rate versus viscosity were extrapolated to the shear rate of $4 9 0   \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ using the Ostwald-de Waele power-law model. The extrapolated viscosity data were then presented on a log–log plot of viscosity versus temperature to obtain the mixing and compaction temperatures based on the traditional viscosity criteria. Yildirim et al. reported that use of the HSRV method resulted in a reduction of about 14 to $^ \textrm { \scriptsize 3 8 } ^ { \scriptsize \textrm { \scriptsize 0 } } \mathbf { C }$ in mixing temperature and a reduction of 10 to $^ \textrm { \scriptsize 2 7 } ^ { \scriptsize \textrm { \scriptsize 0 } } \mathrm { C }$ in compaction temperature [58]. In 2006, Yildirim et al. applied the HSRV method to 50 modified asphalt binders in 9 PGs with the high shear rate selected as $5 0 0   \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ [69]. A new range of viscosity values was established which centered on 0.275 and 0.550 Pa⋅s for mixing and compaction temperatures. It was reported that the HSRV method coupled with the new viscosity criteria lowered the mixing and compaction temperatures from 13 to $5 2 \; ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C }$ in contrast to the conventional method [69].

The applicability of the HSRV method was later evaluated by West et al. [62]. An RV was employed to perform viscosity measurements on fourteen asphalt binders at temperatures ranging from 120 to $1 8 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ with an increment of $1 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C } .$ At each temperature, viscosity data at different shear rates were extrapolated using the Cross-Williamson model to estimate the viscosity at $5 0 0 ~ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ . ## 3.1.3. Steady shear flow method

In addition to the HSRV method, a new method, referred to as SSF method, was developed by Reinke to calculate the mixing and compaction temperatures [63]. Reinke performed this method using a DSR with 25-mm parallel plates and 0.25- or 0.5-mm gap. Creep test was performed on selected asphalt binders over a range of shear stresses at four temperatures 76, 82, 88, and ${ \tt 9 4 ~ ^ { \circ } C } .$ Each creep test was not terminated until the test specimen reached a steady state or the test time lasted for 12 min, whichever came first. It was believed that after a high shear stress was reached, the viscosities of modified asphalt binders did not change or changed very little with further increase of shear stress [71]. Therefore, the viscosities measured at a high shear stress (e.g., 409 Pa) were plotted against temperatures on a chart of double logarithm, and the results were extrapolated to $1 8 0 \; ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ by straight line. The mixing and compaction temperatures were then determined as the temperatures corresponding to the viscosities of $0 . 1 7 \pm 0 . 0 2$ and $0 . 3 5 \pm 0 . 0 3$ Pa⋅s. It should be noted that the viscosity criterion for compaction temperature differs from the conventional one. The test results of a PG 64–34 SBS-modified asphalt binder were provided in their study to make a comparison between the SSF method and the conventional method [63]. The SSF method yielded a mixing temperature of $1 5 2 . 6 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ and a compaction temperature of $1 4 3 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ which were substantially smaller than the counterparts obtained from the conventional method (i.e., 195 and $1 8 5 \; ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C ) }$

The SSF method was also evaluated by West et al. [62]. Viscosities of fourteen asphalt binders were measured over a series of shear stresses from 0.33 to 500 Pa at three temperatures $( \mathrm { i . e . } , 7 6 , 8 2 ,$ and $\mathbf { 8 8 } \; ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C } ) ,$ and the three viscosity values measured at 500 Pa and different temperatures were used to make extrapolations. The test results showed that the SSF method substantially lowered the mixing and compaction temperatures for both unmodified and modified asphalt binders, and the temperature differences between the SSF method and the conventional method were greater for modified asphalt binders [62]. Correlation of the mixing temperatures estimated by the SSF method with those provided by the asphalt binder suppliers yielded a $\mathrm{R}^{2}    of   0.701$ [62]. Despite the reported success of the SSF method by Reinke and West et al., Saboo and Kumar questioned the use of straight-line extrapolation [64]. They believed that the rheology of modified asphalt binders was highly dependent on the molecular structure and therefore sensitive to temperature. A novel test method was developed by them, which is described in subsection 3.1.5.

## 3.1.4. Phase angle method

Another method named phase angle method was developed by Casola based on the use of phase angle master curve [62]. This method was developed based on the idea that phase angle is a consistency property which characterizes the viscoelastic nature of asphalt binders. As presented in NCHRP report 648, the frequency sweep test was performed at four temperatures $( \mathbf { i . e . } ,$ 50, 60, 70, and $\mathbf { 8 0 } ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C } )$ using a DSR with 25-mm parallel plates and 1-mm gap. The target strain amplitude was 12% and the target angular frequencies ranged from 0.001 to 100 rad/s. The phase angle master curve was constructed at a reference temperature of $\left[ 8 0 \; ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C } . \right.$ The angular frequency corresponding to the phase angle of $8 6 ^ { \circ }$ , regarded as the transition point from viscous behavior to viscoelastic behavior, was then identified from the phase angle master curve. Afterwards, the mixing and compaction temperatures were pre-dicted by two empirical equations shown in Eqs. (14) and [62].

## 3.1.5. Rheogram-based test method

As mentioned before, Saboo and Kumar questioned the rationality of the straight-line extrapolation used in the SSF method and argued that selection of the frequency corresponding to $8 6 ^ { \circ }$ in the phase angle method lacked of practical significance [64]. They believed that compaction should be dependent on the normal force and energy rather than the shear rate and thus it was invalid to reduce the compaction temperature of modified asphalt binders by using the shear-thinning concept.

A novel method of predicting the mixing temperature was proposed based on the concept of temperature-independent rheogram [64]. In their study, the steady shear viscosity (SSV) test was performed using a DSR at five temperatures ranging from 40 to $8 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C }$ and at shear rates of $0 . 1 { - } 1 0 0 \; \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ . The 25-mm parallel-plate geometry was adopted with $0 . 5 -$ mm $\mathtt { g a p } .$ In addition, an RV was employed to measure the rotational viscosity at 135 and $1 6 5   ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ using the No. 21 spindle at 20 rpm standard rotational speed. The SSV test results were utilized to construct the rheogram by plotting η/η<sub>0</sub> versus $\eta _ { 0 } \dot { \gamma _ { \star } }$ where η and η<sub>0</sub> represent the measured shear viscosity at a certain shear rate $\dot { \gamma ^ { ' } }$ and the ZSV, respectively [70]. The rheogram could be well described by the Carreau-Yasuda model. The ZSV values measured by a DSR and the rotational viscosities measured by an RV were then modeled with respect to temperature by the Arrhenius equation in order to predict the ZSV at different temperatures. With the determined parameters of the Carreau-Yasuda model and the Arrhenius equation, the shear viscosity at different temperatures and shear rates could be predicted. As a result, the mixing temperature could be obtained when the viscosity criterion and shear rate were specified.

The rheogram-based test method was applied by Saboo and Kumar to four asphalt binders including two unmodified and two polymer modified asphalt binders [64]. It was found that the polymer modified asphalt binders behaved in a shear thinning manner across all shear rates. Unmodified asphalt binders, on the other hand, only exhibited shear thinning at very high shear rates [64]. The effect of shear rate on the mixing temperature was also investigated and was found to decrease for practical shear rates varying from laboratory to field [64]. Such a finding highlighted the importance of reliable calculation of the shear rate during compaction in the SGC and in the field.

A brief summary of all aforementioned test methods is presented in Table 3. It can be observed from Table 3 that these test methods vary greatly in test protocol and viscosity criterion. Therefore, there is a need to compare them in order to find out the best option. NCHRP report 648 was one of the few studies that made such a comparison. It provided a detailed evaluation of the HSRV method, the SSF method, and the phase angle method through testing of fourteen asphalt binders [62]. Table 4 lists the mixing and compaction temperatures estimated by different test methods. It was reported in NCHRP report 648 that the results from the HSRV method was similar to those from the conventional equiviscous method and thus, the HSRV method showed only limited improvement over the existing approach. NCHRP Report 648 recommended the use of the SSF method and the phase angle method because both methods yielded lower mixing and compaction temperatures compared with the conventional method.

In addition to NCHRP report 648, Wen et al. also evaluated the applicability of the HSRV method, the SSF method, and the phase angle method [71]. Three questions were raised about the high temperature characterization of modified asphalt binders: (1) the practical shear rate and proper viscosity criterion; (2) tests in the SSF method and the phase angle method were performed at temperatures much lower than the actual mixing and compaction temperature; and (3) only polymer modified asphalt binders were selected for testing [71]. Asphalt binders with polymeric, warm-mix, and rubber particulate additives were tested in their study using an RV and a DSR integrated with a concentric cylinder system. The concentric cylinder system was able to avoid draindown and spillage of asphalt binders at high temperatures and high shear rates. With the use of the concentric cylinder system, the viscosity at 490 or $5 0 0   s ^ { - 1 }$ in the HSRV method and the viscosity at 409 or 500 Pa at high temperatures in the SSF method could be directly measured without the need for extrapolation. Two important conclusions could be drawn from their study: (1) at temperatures of 135 and 165 ${ } ^ { \circ } \mathbb { C } _ { \flat }$ large differences existed between the viscosities of modified asphalt binders measured at 500 Pa using the concentric cylinder system and those predicted by the SSF method, as shown in Table 5; and (2) the phase angle of most asphalt binders could not reach 86◦, which made it impractical to predict the mixing and compaction temperatures using Eqs. (14) and (15) [71]. These conclusions are in fact contradictory to the recommendations from West et al.

It can be concluded from the existing studies that there is still a lack of reliable test method for estimating the mixing and compaction temperatures for modified asphalt binders. The study conducted by Wen et al. showed the advantages of using a concentric cylinder system under high temperature testing. Thus, the concentric cylinder system can be utilized as a standard geometry in developing a new test method in future studies. As the shear rate plays a vital role in estimation of the mixing and compaction temperatures, more efforts should be devoted to investigating the practical shear rate during compaction. Some related studies on this topic can be found in the literature [72,73].

## 3.2. Test methods for evaluating rutting resistance

Both field data and laboratory testing have demonstrated the ineffectiveness of the Superpave rutting parameter |G\*|/sin δ in capturing the high temperature performance of modified asphalt binders for rating their rutting resistance [36,74]. Several main problems with |G\*|/sin δ have been identified and summarized in Delgadillo et al. [34], as pre-sented in the introduction. In the past two decades, various attempts have been made to seek for a better alternative to |G\*|/sin δ. Many new test methods and/or rutting indicators were developed, among which the repeated creep recovery (RCR) test [36], the Shenoy parameter [75], the ZSV [76], the LSV [77], and the MSCR test [48] attracted a lot of attention from both academia and industry. ## 3.2.1. Repeated creep recovery test

As noted in Subsection 2.3.1, the RCR test established the basis for the later development of the MSCR test [47]. The RCR test was originally considered during the SHRP program, but dismissed because of a preference to LVE measures. It gained new traction in NCHRP report 459 [36]. Bahia et al. recommended to use the creep-recovery loading in place of the cyclic loading for better separation of plastic deformation from total strain [36]. In this way, the ability of an asphalt binder to resist permanent deformation could be more accurately characterized.

The RCR test consists of 100 creep-recovery cycles, and each creeprecovery cycle has a 1-s creep portion and 9-s recovery portion. A single shear stress between 30 and 300 Pa was suggested at each creep portion by Bahia et al. [36]. Delgadillo et al. applied the RCR test to three asphalt binders that were modified using different techniques but had the same PG $( \mathbf { e . g . } ,$ PG 64− 28) [34]. In spite of the same grade, the accumulated permanent deformation of the three binders diverged greatly from each other, which indicated the failure of $| G ^ { \star } | / \sin \delta .$

According to Bahia et al., the well-known four-element Burgers model could be used to model the shear strain response at cycles 50 and 51 after completing the RCR test, as shown in Eq. (16) [36]. It was found that a minimum of 50 cycles were necessary to reach a nearly steady state with a certain security [34,36].

$$
J(t) = J_0 + J_1\left(1 - e^{-\frac{t}{\xi_1}}\right) + \frac{t}{\eta_0}\tag{16}
$$

where: J(t) is the creep compliance; $J _ { 0 }$ is the instantaneous creep compliance; $J _ { 1 }$ is the retardation strength; and $\xi _ { 1 }$ is the retardation time. Derived from the four-element Burgers model based on the RCR test, the viscous component of creep stiffness was proposed to characterize the ability of an asphalt binder to resist rutting, which is denoted as $G _ { \gamma }$ and calculated by Eq. (17).

$$
G _ { \nu }   =   \frac { \eta _ { 0 } } { t }\tag{17}

The RCR test results were reported to have a good correlation with the mixture rutting performance in NCHRP report 459 [36]. As shown in Fig. $4 ,$ an increase in the correlation of determination $( \mathbf { R } ^ { 2 } )$ from 0.3439 to 0.6753 was found when the mixture rutting slope obtained from the repeated shear constant height test was correlated with $| G ^ { \star } |$ /sin δ obtained from the Superpave DSR test and the asphalt binder rutting slope obtained from the RCR test, respectively [36]. Such an increase demonstrated the advantage of the RCR test over the Superpave DSR test. Note that the correlation shown in this figure is much lower than the original SHRP research, which did not include polymer modified asphalt binders. Thus, the broad consensus among researchers and practitioners is that the key and important idea of capturing nonlinear viscoelastic/viscoplastic behaviors of asphalt is principally important when considering polymer modified asphalt binders.

Based on the RCR test, Delgadillo et al. made an attempt to derive the specification criteria for $G _ { \gamma }$ with consideration of the high pavement design temperature, traffic speed, and traffic volume [34]. Given the fact that the stress level used in the RCR test was low, Delgadillo et al. further conducted the RCR test at different stress levels in order to investigate the stress dependency of asphalt binders and the relationship with asphalt mixture rutting performance [78]. They concluded that asphalt binders which could resist higher stress levels without failure of the sample led to asphalt mixtures with higher resistance to permanent deformation.

In general, the RCR test represents one step forward from the Superpave DSR test. However, the stress level used in the RCR test is much smaller than the stress level at which rutting occurs [78]. The stress dependency in asphalt binder strain response merits further consideration.

## 3.2.2. Shenoy parameter

The Shenoy parameter was proposed based on mechanistic analysis of the creep-recovery cycle [75]. According to Shenoy, the maximum strain accumulated in the creep phase and the recoverable strain in the recovery phase can be related to the oscillatory shear test at matching timescale by following basic principles. The relations are shown in Eqs. (18) and (19), which were verified using experimental data in his study [75].

$$
\gamma _ { m a x } = \frac { \tau } { | \boldsymbol { G } ^ { * } | }\tag{18}
$$

$$
\gamma _ { \mathrm { r e c } } =   \frac { \tau G } { G ^ { 2 } }\tag{19}
$$

where: $\gamma _ { m a x }$ is the maximum shear strain; $\gamma _ { r e c }$ is the recoverable shear strain; $G ^ { \prime }$ is the storage modulus; and $G ^ { \alpha }$ is the loss modulus. A deduction of the recoverable shear strain from the maximum shear strain yields the unrecovered shear strain, as presented in $\operatorname { E q . }$ (20).

$$
\gamma _ { \mathrm { u n r } } = \frac { \tau } { \left| \boldsymbol { G } ^ { * } \right| } \left( 1 - \frac { \left| \boldsymbol { G } ^ { * } \right| \boldsymbol { G } } { \boldsymbol { G } ^ { 2 } } \right)\tag{20}
$$

where: $\gamma _ { u n r }$ is the unrecoverable shear strain. Eq. (20) can be further simplified as Eq. (21) in accordance with the interrelationships among storage modulus, loss modulus, and dynamic modulus. The Shenoy parameter is then extracted from Eq. (21) and shown in Eq. (22).

$$
\gamma_{\mathrm{unr}} = \frac{\tau}{\left| \boldsymbol{G}^* \right|} \left( 1 - \frac{1}{\tan \delta \sin \delta} \right)\tag{21}
$$

$$
\mathrm{ShannonPareter} = \frac{\left | G^{*} \right | }{1 - \frac{1}{\tan \delta \sin \delta } }\tag{22}
$$

Compared with $| G ^ { \star } | / \sin \delta ,$ the Shenoy parameter is more sensitive to phase angle variations and thus maximizes the effect of elasticity on the rutting performance of asphalt binders [79]. All available field data included in Shenoy’s study showed that the Shenoy parameter did a better job than $| G ^ { \star }$ /sin δ in terms of the correlation with the mixture rutting behavior for unmodified and modified asphalt binders [79]. However, there is a major deficiency with the Shenoy parameter that the phase angle must be no less than $5 2 ^ { \circ } ;$ otherwise, the Shenoy’s parameter would predict unrealistically negative value of recoverable shear strain. In an attempt to remedy this deficiency, Shenoy proposed to use (sin $\delta ) ^ { 9 }$ as a close approximation to 1–1/tan δ sin $\delta ,$ as shown in Eq. (23) [79]. The phase angle 55◦ was used in Eq. (23) for demarcation instead of 52◦ because the two right-hand side terms acquire identical values at 55◦. One important thing to note is that (sin $\delta \hat { \mathbf { \delta } } ^ { 9 }$ was found through a best fit of sin δ to 1–1/tan δ sin δ and therefore lacks of fundamental basis [79].

$$
\text { Shenoy Parameter }=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{|G^{^*}|}{1-\frac{1}{tan \delta s i n \delta}} & \delta>5 \delta^{^*} \\ &\frac{|G^{^*}|}{\left(s i n \delta\right)^{9}} & \delta<5 \delta^{^*} \end{aligned}\right.\tag{23}
$$

In summary, the Shenoy Parameter is better than $| G ^ { \star } | / \sin \delta$ in terms of rutting characterization but its application is limited to phase angles that are higher than 52◦.

## 3.2.3. Zero shear viscosity

The ZSV is a rheological parameter that describes the shear viscosity of an asphalt binder when the applied shear rate is approaching zero [60,61]. It has been adopted as a rutting indicator in the European specification CEN/TS 15325 [76]. Lots of studies have demonstrated that the ZSV correlates well with the asphalt mixture rutting performance [61,80–82]. An example is provided in Fig. 5, which is presented using the data collected by Gungor and Saglik [82]. In their study, a total of twelve asphalt binders were selected for testing, and the ZSV of each asphalt binder at $5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C }$ was measured following the test method specified in CEN/TS 15325. Triaxial compression tests were performed on corresponding asphalt mixture specimens at $5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C }$ as per the standard EN 12697–25, and the deformation at the end of $^ { 1 0 , 0 0 0 }$ loading cycles was used as a rutting indicator [82]. It can be found from Fig. 5 that the ZSV was superior to $| G ^ { \star } | / \sin \delta$ in correlation with asphalt mixture permanent deformation. Nowadays, the ZSV has extensive applications in evaluating the rutting potential of asphalt binders modified by different technologies.

The ability of ZSV to evaluate the rutting performance depends on the measurement method, especially for modified asphalt binders. Generally, the ZSV of an asphalt binder can be measured in steady and dynamic shear domains in accordance with the Cox-Merz rule, which depicts the equivalency between the steady-state shear viscosity and the complex viscosity when the shear rate and angular frequency are numerically identical [83–86]. It can be summarized from the literature that the ZSV can be measured using three test methods: (1) creep test; (2) shear rate sweep test; and (3) frequency sweep test [87–91]. The creep test and the shear rate sweep test measure the ZSV in the steady shear domain, whereas the frequency sweep test measures the ZSV in the dynamic shear domain.

As for the creep test, a constant load is imposed on an asphalt binder specimen, and the variation of the resulting strain is monitored with time. The specimen reaches the stage of steady-state flow when it shows a pure viscous deformation. At such a stage, the shear strain rate of the asphalt binder tends to be constant. The ZSV can then be estimated using the Burgers model. During a creep test, the applied shear stress should be maintained at a low level so that the test specimen stays in the linear viscoelastic state. A shear stress below 5000 Pa is recommended for unmodified asphalt binders and a shear stress between 10 and 50 Pa for polymer modified asphalt binders [76]. The creep test method has been adopted in the European specification CEN/TS 15325 to determine the ZSV of asphalt binders at $6 0 \; ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } \; [ 7 6 ]$ . However, it has been reported by many that polymer modified asphalt binders may not reach a steadystate flow even after a creep time of 8 h, which therefore makes it challenging to measure the ZSV reliably [88,89].

In terms of the shear rate sweep test, an asphalt binder specimen is sheared at a series of increasing shear rates. At each shear rate, the test is maintained until a steady-state flow is reached. Usually, the lower the shear rate is, the longer the time required to achieve an equilibrium state of steady flow will be [60]. According to Shan et al., the shear rate sweep test can be terminated when a drop in the resulting shear stress is observed with the increase of shear rate [84]. The drop in the shear stress gives an indication that damage has been generated in the test specimen. After the completion of the shear rate sweep test, a flow curve can be constructed by potting the steady-state shear viscosity versus the shear rate. Rheological models, such as the Cross model, the Carreau model, and the Carreau-Yasuda model, can be used to fit the test data and to determine the ZSV from the flow curve [92–94]. Their expressions are presented in Eqs. (24)–(26).

$$
\eta ( \dot { \gamma } ) = \frac { \eta _ { 0 } - \eta _ { \infty } } { 1 + ( k \dot { \gamma } ) ^ { m } }   +   \eta _ { \infty }\tag{24}
$$

$$
\eta ( \dot { \gamma } ) = \frac { \eta _ { 0 } - \eta _ { \infty } } { \left[ 1 + ( \lambda _ { 1 } \dot { \gamma } ) ^ { 2 } \right] ^ { b / 2 } } + \eta _ { \infty }\tag{25}
$$

$$
\eta ( \dot { \gamma } ) = \cfrac { \eta _ { 0 } - \eta _ { \infty } } { [ 1 + ( \lambda _ { 2 } \dot { \gamma } ) ^ { a } ] ^ { ( n - 1 ) / a } } + \eta _ { \infty }\tag{26}
$$

where: $\eta , \; \dot { \gamma _ { \star } }$ and $\eta _ { 0 }$ have been previously explained; $\eta _ { \infty }$ is the limiting viscosity at infinite shear; k and m are the fitting parameters of the Cross model; $\lambda _ { 1 }$ and b are the fitting parameters of the Carreau model; and $\lambda _ { 2 } ,$ $a ,$ and n are the fitting parameters of the Carreau-Yasuda model.

The frequency sweep test is less time-consuming when compared with the creep test and the shear rate sweep test. It measures the complex viscosity of an asphalt binder at different frequencies, which is calculated as the ratio of complex modulus to angular frequency. The frequency sweep test should be conducted within the linear viscoelastic range of the test specimen. Therefore, a strain amplitude sweep test should be performed at the highest test frequency to determine an applicable strain amplitude. After the frequency sweep test is completed, the measured complex viscosity and corresponding angular frequency are presented in a log–log plot, and the ZSV can be predicted using one of the previously mentioned rheological models. However, it has been found that for some polymer modified asphalt binders, the ZSV obtained from the model prediction can be unrealistically high due to the absence of a viscosity plateau in the complex viscosity versus angular frequency plot [89,91]. In this case, the determined ZSV is overestimated and thus unreliable.

Currently, there is no clear consensus reached on which test method is the best one. Usually, researchers arbitrarily select one of the three test methods for measurement of the ZSV. However, it can be summarized from the above that the creep test and the frequency sweep test may not be applicable to some polymer modified asphalt binders due to the inaccessibility of a steady-state flow and the absence of a viscosity plateau, respectively. This fact highlights the importance of using a proper test method for ZSV measurement. In contrast to the creep test and the frequency sweep test, the shear rate sweep test is a more promising test method that can be applicable to asphalt binders with and without modifications. The study conducted by Li et al. demonstrated the ability of the shear rate sweep test to measure the ZSV of unmodified and polymer modified asphalt binders, and a simplified test method was proposed [95]. In conclusion, future research is still required to develop a universally applicable standard test protocol for ZSV measurement.

## 3.2.4. Low shear viscosity

In view of the shortcoming of the frequency sweep test, De Visscher et al. proposed the concept of LSV to avoid overpredicted ZSV values [88]. Fig. 6 shows the relationship between LSV and ZSV in the plot of complex viscosity versus angular frequency. In their study, the complex viscosity measured at the frequency of 0.001 Hz was considered as an approximation of the ZSV. Different from the ZSV, the LSV could be directly measured at a low frequency combined with a low strain amplitude using a DSR. Morea later proposed a LSV limit for original asphalt binders $( \mathbf { i . e . } ,$ 500 Pa⋅s) and aged asphalt binders (i.e., 2000 Pa⋅s) [96].

The concept of LSV was adopted by the European specification CEN/ TS 15324 to determine the equiviscous temperature (EVT) of asphalt binders using a DSR in the low frequency oscillation mode [77]. The developed test method can be regarded as a surrogate for the conventional softening point test [97]. The test method of measuring the EVT consists of two major steps. The first step is to conduct a temperature sweep test on an asphalt binder specimen at 0.01 Hz with a strain amplitude of 0.1, according to the recommended test conditions [77]. The temperature at which the complex viscosity is equal to 2000 Pa is identified from the complex viscosity versus temperature curve using a linear regression. The identified temperature is denoted as EVT1. The second step is to perform a frequency sweep test at EVT1 with the same strain amplitude and the selected frequencies are 1, $0 . 3 , 0 . 1 , 0 . 0 3 , 0 . 0 1 ,$ and 0.003 Hz [77]. Subsequent to the completion of the frequency sweep test, the measured complex viscosity is plotted against frequency. An extrapolation is made to 0.0001 $\mathbf { H z } ,$ which is closer to zero than 0.01 Hz, and the complex viscosity at $0 . 0 0 0 1$ Hz is predicted. The increase in temperature is then estimated by assuming the temperature sweep test in the first step was conducted at 0.0001 Hz instead of 0.01 Hz. The sum of EVT1 and the increased temperature is denoted as EVT2. EVT2 is seen as a performance indicator for evaluating the partial contribution of asphalt binders to the overall rutting resistance of asphalt mixtures [77].

![](images/page_10_chart_16.jpg)

Fig. 6. Definition of LSV based on CEN/TS 15324 (refer to [77]).

The test method in CEN/TS 15324 was later examined by several researchers [97–101]. The test results were not as promising as expected for modified asphalt binders. Zoorob et al. reported that the applicability of this test method was limited to neat asphalt binders; for an SBS-modified asphalt binder, this test method remained entirely inadequate [97]. Santagata et al. found that the complex viscosity of the asphalt rubber obtained from the first step never fell below 2000 Pa⋅s over the selected temperature range, which made it impossible to proceed to the subsequent frequency sweep test [100]. Similar findings were also reported by Liu et al. [101]. They attributed the failure of the test method in CEN/TS 15324 to the lack of a viscosity plateau in the low frequency range. An improved test method, named as ZSV-based test method, was developed to determine the EVT. In their study, the EVT was defined as the temperature at which an asphalt binder specimen exhibited a given ZSV (e.g., 2000 Pa⋅s). The ZSV-based test method utilized the shear rate sweep test rather than the frequency sweep test, and the Arrhenius equation was employed to determine the EVT instead of empirical linear regression. It was demonstrated that the EVT values determined using the ZSV-based test method were reliable and repeatable irrespective of the asphalt binder type.

It can be summarized from the above that the LSV concept is not practically sound for modified asphalt binders. Therefore, it is important to determine the actual ZSV rather than to use an approximation.

## 3.2.5. Refinement of the MSCR test

The MSCR test has been included in the Superpave PG system to grade the high temperature performance of asphalt binders [37,38]. A detailed description of the MSCR test protocol and analysis has been provided in Section 2.3. When compared with the preceding test methods, the MSCR test has the ability to evaluate the stress dependency of asphalt binder response and to further identify excessively stresssensitive asphalt binders [47]. Many studies have demonstrated that the non-recoverable creep compliance has a satisfactory correlation with the rutting performance of asphalt mixtures [48,102–104]. Fig. 7 pre-sents an example to show the advantage of $J _ { n r 3 . 2 }$ over $| G ^ { \star } | / \sin \delta ,$ in which the data points were collected from the study conducted by Zhang et al. [104]. In Fig. 7, the $| G ^ { \star } | / \sin \delta$ and $J _ { m r 3 . 2 }$ values of ten asphalt binders were measured at $6 4 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C }$ by the Superpave DSR test and the MSCR test, respectively, and the rut depth at 10,000 passes of corresponding asphalt mixtures was measured at $5 0   ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C }$ by the Hamburg wheel tracking test. It can be observed from $\mathrm { F i g . } ~ 7$ that the $\mathtt { R } ^ { 2 }$ between $J _ { n r 3 . 2 }$ and rut depth was much higher than that between $\left\| G ^ { \star } \right\| ,$ /sin δ and rut depth, which demonstrated the superiority of the MSCR test.

Although being superior to the Superpave DSR test in terms of rutting characterization, the MSCR test is debatable in three aspects [51,105]: (1) the number of creep-recovery cycles at each stress level; (2) the duration of creep and/or recovery time; and (3) the selection of representative stress levels. These debates originate from the following experimental observations [47]: (1) the strain response of some modified asphalt binders cannot reach a steady state at the end of the 10 cycles; (2) the $9 - s$ recovery time is not sufficient for some modified asphalt binders to fully recover the viscoelastic strain generated in the creep portion; as a result, the non-recoverable strain at the end of the recovery portion is in fact an addition of the true permanent deformation and partial unrecovered viscoelastic strain; and (3) the two stress levels in the MSCR test (i.e., 0.1 and 3.2 kPa) were not necessarily intended to represent the asphalt binder stress state in asphalt pavements; a higher stress level is recommended to be better representative of the stress level experienced in typical asphalt mixtures. The deficiencies of the current MSCR test protocol can adversely affect the rutting evaluation of modified asphalt binders. For example, White found that the $J _ { m 3 , 2 }$ values of highly modified asphalt binders were poorly correlated with the asphalt mixture rutting performance due to the incomplete recovery of viscoelastic strain [106].

Lots of efforts have been made in recent years to improve upon the MSCR test protocol [51,107,108]. Golalipour et al. added a higher stress level $( \mathbf { e . g . } ,$ 10 kPa) in the MSCR test to measure the stress sensitivity and the strain response nonlinearity [51]. A total of 30 cycles were repeated at each stress level to ensure a stable and consistent state of strain response, and the strain data in the last 5 cycles were selected to calculate the $J _ { n r }$ values. It was found that the changes imposed upon the standard MSCR test yielded a better correlation with the mixture results, which validated the feasibility of the modified MSCR test [51]. Similarly, Zhou et al. concluded that the increase of creep-recovery cycles and the addition of a higher stress level could be helpful in ranking the SBS modified and reclaimed asphalt pavement (RAP) binders [107]. Gaspar et al. investigated the effect of different combinations of creep and recovery times on the $J _ { n r }$ and R values for highly modified asphalt binders [108]. According to their findings, an increase of the creep time resulted in an increase in $J _ { n r }$ and a decrease in $R ;$ on the contrary, an increase of the recovery time led to lower $J _ { m }$ and higher R [108]. When both creep time and recovery time increased while maintaining the ratio of 1:9, the effect of increased creep time was found to be more pronounced, which was indicated by higher $J _ { n r }$ and lower R [108].

In addition to the modification of the MSCR test protocol, some researchers developed a mechanistic modeling approach to analyze the test data [109,110]. Masad et al. applied the Schapery’s nonlinear viscoelastic theory to decouple the nonlinear viscoelastic strain and viscoplastic strain in the creep portion [109]. The viscoplastic strain was then employed to calculate the $J _ { m }$ values of selected asphalt binders. When compared with the $J _ { n r }$ values of five asphalt binders calculated using Eqs. (8) and (9), those calculated using the mechanistic modeling approach were found to have a better correlation with the asphalt mixture permanent deformation measured in the rutting experiment [109]. Based on the work of Masad et al., Liu et al. applied the full form of the Schapery’s nonlinear viscoelastic theory to analyze the test data in the last 5 cycles where the strain response was deemed to reach a relatively steady state [110]. It was demonstrated in their study that the mechanistic modeling approach smoothed the recovered strain data of the neat asphalt binders and solved the issue of incomplete viscoelastic recovery for SBS-modified asphalt binders [110].

The MSCR test is generally regarded as the best available test method for rutting characterization of asphalt binders. A more detailed review of the MSCR test from its development, validation to new refinements in test protocol and analysis method can be found in the literature [47].

## 3.2.6. Summary and comparison of different test methods

Table 6 presents a brief summary of all existing test methods for evaluation of the rutting resistance of asphalt binders. There was evidence that all of the rutting indicators in Table 6 showed improvements in comparison to |G\*|/sin δ. A number of studies have evaluated and compared different test methods in order to screen out the best approach for rutting characterization of asphalt binders [81,111–114]. Most of these studies reached a consistent conclusion that the MSCR test was the best among all existing test methods [111,113,114].

Take the study conducted by Radhakrishnan et al. as an example. Table 7 lists the data collected from their study [114]. All of the binder and mixture tests were conducted at $6 0   ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C } .$ In their study, $| G ^ { \star } | / \sin \delta ,$ the Shenoy parameter, the LSV, and the $J _ { n r }$ were compared based on the correlation between each rutting indicator and the rut depth measured by the wheel tracking test. Furthermore, the effect of angular frequency on $| G ^ { \star } | / \sin \delta ,$ the Shenoy parameter, and the LSV and the effect of stress level on the $J _ { m }$ were also investigated. ## 3.3. Test methods for evaluating fatigue cracking resistance

The Superpave fatigue parameter $| G ^ { \star }$ |sin δ was derived based on the DSE concept. Two limitations have been identified that hinder an accurate evaluation of the fatigue resistance of asphalt binders: (1) the Superpave DSR test is performed in the linear viscoelastic state using small strains, whereas fatigue cracking introduces damage into asphalt binders; and (2) not all of the DSE is consumed for the development of fatigue cracking because partial strain energy is dissipated due to the viscoelastic effect. With the awareness of these two limitations, some new DSR-based test methods have been devised for fatigue characterization of asphalt binders, which include the time sweep test [31], the binder yield energy test (BYET) [115], and the linear amplitude sweep (LAS) test [116]. The idea behind these fatigue tests is to measure the fatigue resistance by introducing significant damage to the test material. Specifically, the time sweep test and the LAS test can be regarded as an evolution of the Superpave DSR test with higher stress or strain amplitude and more loading cycles applied [117]. The details on each test method are explained in the following subsections.

## 3.3.1. Time sweep test

As one of the most complicated damage phenomena in asphalt pavements, fatigue cracking is recognized to initiate and propagate in the asphalt binder, which highlights the need to control a binderassociated damage in the asphalt binder specification [36]. Bahia et al. pioneered the use of the time sweep test to characterize the fatigue resistance of asphalt binders [31]. In their study, the stiffness of five asphalt binders of the same grade (i.e., PG 76− 22) was correlated with the associated mixture stiffness at multiple intermediate temperatures. Distinct relationships were found in spite of identical aggregate gradation, mixture preparation, and testing conditions, which indicated that linear properties were not good indictors of the mixture response. Simple finite element analysis was then used to calculate the strain amplitude range within an asphalt binder of a typical asphalt mixture. The results showed that the strains within asphalt binder films could be as high as 10 to 100 times of the bulk strains of the asphalt mixture [31]. Therefore, the strain dependency and the effect of nonlinearity should be carefully considered in the binder testing.

Nowadays, the time sweep test has become one of the most widely used test methods to investigate the fatigue and healing properties of asphalt binders.

As mentioned previously, the time sweep test can be regarded as an evolution of the Superpave DSR test with higher stress/strain levels and more loading cycles. The time sweep test allows the material to go beyond the linear viscoelastic range and into the damage accumulation range. In a typical time sweep test, an asphalt binder specimen is subjected to repeated sinusoidally oscillatory shear at a preselected stress/ strain level until it fails. The fatigue life is employed as an indicator of the fatigue resistance of asphalt binders, which corresponds to the loading cycle at which the fatigue failure initiates [36]. In other words, the fatigue life indicates the number of loading cycles an asphalt binder can sustain before failure. Determination of the fatigue life requires a predefined failure criterion. A number of failure criteria have been proposed by different researchers, which can be generally divided into three types [118]: (1) phenomenological failure criterion; (2) dissipated energy-based failure criterion; and (3) stiffness ratio-based failure criterion. Those failure criteria were originally developed to characterize the fatigue behavior of asphalt mixtures, but their applicability to asphalt binders has been fully validated.

A typical example of the phenomenological failure criterion is the 50% reduction in the initial modulus, which identifies the fatigue life as the loading cycle where the dynamic shear modulus drops by half compared with the initial dynamic shear modulus [119]. Such a failure criterion is empirical in nature and lacks of solid theoretical support. However, this failure criterion is still being used because of its simplicity. The peak in phase angle is another widely used phenomenological failure criterion, which was proposed by Reese [120]. The loading cycle corresponding to the peak in phase angle is determined as the fatigue life. It can be found that the phenomenological failure criteria were developed based on the variation of dynamic shear modulus and phase angle with loading cycles.

There are three dissipated energy-based failure criteria: (1) the dissipated energy ratio (DER) method developed by Hopman et al. [121]; (2) the cumulative dissipated energy ratio (CDER) developed by Pronk and Hopman [122]; and (3) the ratio of dissipated energy change (RDEC) method developed by Ghuzlan and Carpenter [123]. The kernel of these failure criteria lies in the utilization of the DSE concept. The DER, CDER, and RDEC are calculated based on Eqs. (27)–(29), respectively.

Three distinct stages can be observed when plotting RDEC against loading cycle, as shown in Fig. 11(a). The transition point between the plateau stage and the tertiary stage is regarded as the initiation of fatigue failure, and the corresponding loading cycle is regarded as the fatigue life in the RDEC method. The PV was defined as the RDEC value at the 50% modulus reduction point [125]. This practice was based on the finding that $N _ { f 5 0 }$ was highly related to the fatigue life determined by the REDC method for asphalt mixtures [126].

Currently, there is no consensus reached on which failure criterion best determines the fatigue life of asphalt binders. Researchers have been seeking for a failure criterion that is independent of loading mode and has a unified definition for both loading modes. In this sense, the $N _ { p 2 0 }$ used in the CDER method, the 50% modulus reduction used in the RDEC method, and the SR/NSR method stand out. Wang et al. conducted a comprehensive comparison study of different failure criteria used in the time sweep test [129]. They concluded that the phase angle-based failure criterion, the RDEC method, and the SR method provided equivalent fatigue life results. The SR method was strongly recommended for detection of fatigue failure of asphalt binders because it is well defined and easy to calculate [129].

In addition to the failure criterion, a fatigue law is usually used to predict the fatigue damage growth with increasing stress or strain levels. It is represented by a simple power law model, which links the fatigue life to a fatigue-related parameter [118]. Depending on the specific failure criterion, the fatigue-related parameter can be assigned as the initial shear stress, initial shear strain, PV, and the like. With the use of the fatigue law, the fatigue life out of the measured stress or strain range can thus be predicted. Besides the fatigue law, the viscoelastic continuum damage (VECD) theory has been employed by many researchers to characterize the fatigue performance of asphalt binders. A detailed introduction to the VECD theory will be presented in Subsection 3.3.3 as the VECD theory is essential for analyzing the LAS test results.

## 3.3.2. Binder yield energy test

In spite of the effectiveness in characterizing the fatigue resistance of asphalt binders, the time sweep test has long been criticized for the lengthy time it takes, which hinders it from being accepted as a specification test method [117]. As mentioned previously, a specification test should not take longer than 30–60 mins to perform [115]. In an attempt to reduce the test time, the BYET was developed by Johnson et al. to investigate the fatigue behavior of asphalt binders at intermediate temperatures [115]. The BYET is essentially a constant strain rate test, which means the shear strain increases linearly with time throughout the test. With the growth of loading time, the resulting shear stress increases first and then decreases, which leads to a maximum shear stress.

Johnson et al. performed the BYET on five RTFO-aged asphalt binders at a temperature of $^ \textrm { \scriptsize 1 9 ^ { \circ } C }$ and at shear rates of 0.005, 0.0075, and $0 . 0 1 ~ \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 } ~ [ 1 1 5 ]$ . These asphalt binders were used by the Federal Highway Administration (FHWA) in the ALF fatigue study. Based on the test results, the shear stress was plotted versus the shear strain to form a stress–strain curve. It was pointed out that the test specimen never truly appeared to fracture during the BYET; instead, it seemed to reach a point where it began to yield [115]. Two parameters were derived from the stress–strain curve as measures of fatigue resistance: (1) the area underneath the stress–strain curve before the maximum shear stress, termed yield energy in their study; and (2) the shear strain corresponding to the maximum shear stress, denoted as $\gamma _ { \mathrm { { r m a x } } } [ 1 1 5 ]$ . A schematic plot of typical stress–strain relationship obtained from the BYET is shown in Fig. 13. In addition to the two parameters, Johnson et al. established a framework of using the VECD theory to analyze the BYET. This framework was employed in their study to estimate the fatigue behavior of selected asphalt binders under varying levels of traffic and pavement conditions.

## 3.3.3. Linear amplitude sweep test

The LAS test is another attempt to overcome the deficiencies of the time sweep test, which provides the ability to analyze the test results based on continuum damage mechanics. The LAS test was originally developed by Johnson and Bahia [134]. The LAS test is essentially different from the BYET. The BYET is a constant strain rate test, whereas the LAS test is an oscillation test with varying strain amplitudes. In their study, the LAS test consisted of two steps, namely frequency sweep test and amplitude sweep test, which were employed in succession to obtain the undamaged and damaged material properties of tested asphalt binders.

The frequency sweep test was performed at a strain amplitude of 0.1% over a wide range of frequencies from 0.1 to 30 Hz and temperatures from 7 to $2 8 \; ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C }$ [134]. The concept of iso-stiffness testing was used to determine the test temperature for the following amplitude sweep test. Specifically, the temperature at which $| G ^ { \star } |$ |sin δ reached 5000 kPa was selected for each asphalt binder. After determination of the test temperature, the amplitude sweep test was conducted at 10 Hz with an initial strain amplitude of 0.1% and subsequent strain amplitudes increasing linearly from 1 to 20% [134]. At each strain level, 100 loading cycles were repeated, which resulted in a total of 2100 cycles lasting for 210 s. As a consequence, the test duration of the LAS test greatly reduced when compared with that of the time sweep test. Despite the reported success of the LAS test, many researchers attempted to improve on the analysis method described in the AASHTO specification to pursue better characterization of asphalt binder fatigue behavior. Those improvements are reflected in two aspects: (1) use of simplified-VECD (S-VECD) model developed by Underwood et al. [141,142]; and (2) use of different failure criteria. Safaei et al. were among the first to analyze the LAS test using the S-VECD model [143]. The Schapery’s extended elastic–viscoelastic correspondence principle and work potential theory provide the basis for the S-VECD model [144,145]. In accordance with the extended elastic–viscoelastic correspondence principle, pseudo-strain is defined as the ratio of viscoelastic stress to a reference modulus, as shown in Eq. (46) [144].

## 3.3.4. Summary and comparison of different test methods

All of the aforementioned test methods are briefly summarized in Table 8. Unlike the Superpave DSR test that is performed within the linear viscoelastic range to derive |G\*|sin δ, the time sweep test, the BYET, and the LAS test are destructive tests and introduce significant fatigue damage to the material. In essence, these three tests can better characterize the fatigue behavior of asphalt binders than the Superpave DSR test. Among the three tests, the BYET may not be able to fundamentally characterize the damage growth nature of some modified asphalt binders based on the VECD theory. In contrast, the VECD theory enables the time sweep test and the LAS test with the ability to predict the fatigue life of asphalt binders at any specified strain level. However, the time sweep test can be time-consuming, which makes it a less desirable method for specification use. Therefore, the LAS test is widely considered (though not universally accepted) as the best current fatigue test for asphalt binders. With the use of the S-VECD model and the $\stackrel { \cdot } { \cdot } _ { G ^ { R } }$ approach, the LAS test is capable of accurately predicting the fatigue life obtained from the time sweep test.

## 3.4. Test methods for evaluating thermal cracking resistance

Thermally-induced transverse cracking is a major distress mode in cold climates. It occurs when the thermal stress builds up in the restrained asphalt pavement and exceeds the tensile strength of the material as the temperature drops. However, the BBR test does not provide a measure of the material strength, and the DT test has not gained widespread use due to testing difficulties [158]. It has been reported that the BBR specification underestimates the low temperature performance of some modified asphalt binders [35]. In recent years, some new test methods have been developed for testing the low temperature cracking resistance of asphalt binders with newly designed test device or specimen geometry, such as the asphalt binder cracking device (ABCD) [159], the BBR Pro [160], the double edge notched tension (DENT) test [161], and the single edge notched beam (SENB) test [162]. The common feature in these test methods is that they test the asphalt binder at its failure condition [158].

As the BBR test requires a relatively large sample size and a long conditioning time to reach thermal equilibrium, use of the DSR 4-mm parallel plates to conduct rheological tests has been proposed to characterize the low temperature performance of asphalt binders with an intent to reduce the test complexity [163]. This approach was not intended to fundamentally address the limitation of the BBR test but aimed to predict the low temperature grade of asphalt binders with acceptable accuracy. In addition, some researchers proposed to indirectly measure the ductility of asphalt binders because the ductility measured at an appropriate temperature was reported to be a good indicator of pavement durability [164–167]. Typical examples are the Glover-Rowe (G-R) parameter [168] and $\Delta T _ { c }$ [169], which are used as surrogates quantifying the loss of relaxation properties as an asphalt binder ages. The following subsections present detailed discussion on each of the aforementioned approach.

## 3.4.1. Development of new test device

According to AASHTO R 49 and ASTM D6816, the critical cracking temperature of an asphalt binder can be indirectly measured based on the combined use of the BBR test and the DT test [170,171]. The temperature at which the thermal stress calculated from the BBR test is equal to the tensile strength measured from the DT test is defined as the critical cracking temperature. However, calculation of thermal stress can be complex and lengthy, and the tensile strength derived from the DT test may not be representative of field strength because the strain rates used in the DT test are orders of magnitude faster than field thermal strain rates [159]. As an alternative, the ABCD was developed by Kim to directly measure the critical cracking temperature of asphalt binders with the simulation of thermal cracking of asphalt pavements in the laboratory [159]. The ABCD consists of a hollow cylindrical metal ring, an electrical strain gauge and a temperature sensor attached to the inside of the ring, a data acquisition system, and an environmental chamber, as illustrated in Fig. 18 [159]. A flexible silicone mold is utilized to form the asphalt binder into a ring shape when it is molded onto the outside of the ring.

In principle, the ABCD makes use of the dissimilar coefficients of thermal expansion/contraction of asphalt binders $( \mathbf { i . e . } ,$ 170–200 × $1 0 ^ { - 6 } / ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ and the metal ring [159]. As the temperature drops, thermal stress builds up in the specimen due to the differential thermal contraction, which eventually results in thermal cracks. The critical cracking temperature of an asphalt binder is directly determined as the temperature at which a sudden jump in the strain reading occurs due to the relief of thermal stress in the test specimen [159]. In addition, the tensile strength of the binder at failure can be calculated using Eq. (56) [172].

Such an assumption is an inherent limitation of the ABCD test. It should also be noted that the ABCD cracking temperature of an asphalt binder is always lower than the thermal cracking temperature of the associated asphalt pavement because the aggregate contraction can induce additional strain to the asphalt binder [173].

Another new test device developed for low temperature characterization of asphalt binders is the BBR $\mathbf { p r o } ,$ which is capable of performing both creep test and strength test [160]. ## 3.4.2. Development of new specimen geometry

The DENT test was another trial to measure the tensile strength of asphalt binders in addition to the DT test. It was initially employed by Andriescu et al. to determine the essential and plastic works of fracture for tested asphalt binders [177]. A schematic plot of the specimen geometry is presented in Fig. 20(a), which has a 45◦ notch angle. In their study, the DENT test was conducted at a constant speed of 100 mm/min and at a temperature of $2 5 \pm 1   ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ The total energy determined from the force versus displacement curve was divided into two parts: (1) the energy necessary for the fracture process; and (2) the energy necessary for the plastic deformation outside the fracture zone [177]. This approach has been adopted in AASHTO TP 113 to evaluate the resistance of an asphalt binder to ductile failure [178]. The output parameters of the DENT test include the essential work of failure, the plastic work of failure, and an approximate critical crack tip opening displacement at a specified temperature and loading rate [178]. Equations for calculating these parameters can be found in AASHTO TP 113.

Gauthier and Anderson performed the DENT test on eleven asphalt binders at three low temperatures using a DT tester [179]. The DT tester was selected because the tensile specimens could be tested on a standard DT tester without any equipment modification [179]. The test specimens, as shown in Fig. 20(b), were prepared with modified molds and a jig for sharpening the notch. Three theories, which included linear elastic fracture mechanics (LEFM), elastic plastic fracture mechanics (EPFM), and time dependent fracture mechanics (TDFM), were used to analyze the DENT test results. Gauthier and Anderson concluded that the LEFM and the EPFM were inapplicable at temperatures above the glass transition temperature, and the TDFM must be used in the vicinity of the grading temperature to characterize the asphalt binder fracture [179].

Based on the work conducted by Gauthier and Anderson, Li et al. performed the DENT test on nine asphalt binders using a DT tester [180]. The test specimens had 1.5 mm pre-cracks. The strain rate used in the DENT test was reduced to 1.8%/min because of the rapid failure occurring at low temperatures when using the standard strain rate (i.e., 3%/min). It was found that the strength values for the pre-cracked DENT specimens were 3 to 5 times lower than those measured using the DT test [180]. In a follow-up study, Zofka and Marasteanu explained the DT mold modification procedures and the DENT specimen preparation procedures in detail [161]. The DENT test, the BBR test, and the DT test were performed on nine asphalt binders typical for cold climates. The DENT test results showed extremely different fracture behaviors for two asphalt binders of the same grade (PG 64− 34) but from different suppliers [161]. Comparisons between the DENT test results and the DT test results showed that the DENT test provided better repeatability than the DT test and produced lower values of failure stress and strain due to the manually initiated pre-crack in the specimen [161]. The average ratio of the DT failure stress to the DENT failure stress was 2.8, and the ratio became 3.3 in terms of failure strain. With the determined DENT failure stress, the fracture toughness $K _ { I C }$ was calculated based on the LEFM [161]. It was observed that the fracture toughness followed an identical pattern as the failure stress. A new binder classification in terms of the low temperature resistance could be developed based on the fracture toughness from the DENT test. The DENT test results were also used in combination with the BBR test results to calculate the critical cracking temperature of each asphalt binder. For most of the binders, this approach resulted in much lower critical cracking temperature than the BBR and DT approach [161].

The SENB test is essentially a three-point bending test on pre-notched beam specimens. It is a fracture mechanics-based test method commonly used for determination of the linear elastic plane-strain fracture toughness of metallic materials. A standard test method is provided in ASTM E399 [181]. A schematic plot of the SENB configuration is shown in Fig. 21. The SENB test has been adopted by many researchers to investigate the low temperature performance of asphalt binders [182,183]. Hoare and Hesp performed the SENB test on a large number of both unmodified and modified asphalt binders [182]. Specimens of three different sizes with and without notches were tested at a constant crosshead displacement rate of 0.01 mm/s using a three-point bending fixture that was set in an environmental chamber mounted on an MTS mechanical testing scheme. The fracture toughness $K _ { I C }$ and fracture energy $G _ { I C }$ were calculated based on the specimen dimensions, the load at failure, and the measured modulus. Their study showed that the facture energy might be a better performance indicator because it was independent of sample size and configuration [182]. Following CEN/TS 15936 [183], Chailleux et al. employed a tensile-compression tester machine to conduct the SENB test on eight asphalt binders with different aging states at a displacement rate of 0.6 mm/min [184]. The critical energy release rate and limit cracking temperature were used to rank asphalt binders. It was reported that the resulting ranking differed from the BBR output.

The test specimen used in the aforementioned studies is usually a piece of pre-notched asphalt binder extended by two metal bars. Velasquez et al. observed adhesion problems between the asphalt binder and metal bars during specimen preparation and handling, which could result in premature failure at the binder-metal interface [162]. To eliminate the need for metal bars, a new SENB geometry was proposed in their study, which added a 3-mm notch to the midpoint of the wide side of the BBR mold side-beams [162]. The BBR was also modified by adding a loading motor that controlled the displacement rate during testing and using a load cell with a higher load capacity [162]. The new SENB geometry along with the modified BBR is termed as the BBR-SENB system. Velasquez et al. performed the BBR-SENB test on seven asphalt binders at temperatures of $-12,   -18,$ and $-24\ ^{\circ} C$ and at a constant displacement rate of 0.01 mm/s. The fracture load and fracture deflection, which were determined as the peak load occurring during the test and the deflection at the peak load, were correlated with S(60) and m (60) obtained from the BBR test. It was found that S(60) correlated extremely well with both fracture load and fracture deformation, whereas m(60) had a moderate correlation with both fracture parameters [162].

Bahia et al. introduced the failure energy to analyze the BBR-SENB test. Failure energy was calculated as the ratio of the total area under the entire load–deflection curve to the area of the ligament, as presented in Eq. (60) [185].

$$
G _ { f } = \frac { \int P \mathrm { d } u } { A _ { \mathrm { l i g } } }\tag{60}
$$

where: $G _ { f }$ is the failure energy; P is the load; u is the deflection; and $A _ { \mathrm { l i g } }$ is the ligament area. In their study, the BBR-SENB test was conducted on a large set of unmodified and modified asphalt binders. Three parameters were used for analysis: failure energy, fracture toughness, and fracture deflection. Comparisons between the BBR-SENB test results with the BBR test results showed that S(60) and m(60) had very poor correlations with the three fracture parameters. This finding was inconsistent with what was reported by Velasquez et al. [185]. In order to select the performance indicators for the BBR-SENB test, the three fracture parameters were plotted against the glass transition temperature for all asphalt binders. The fracture toughness was identified as a poor indictor of the ductile to brittle transition of asphalt binders and was therefore not recommended for specifications or material selection purposes [185]. The failure energy was then used as a performance indicator for the BBR-SENB test and the semi-circular bending (SCB) test to verify whether the BBR-SENB test results were relevant to the asphalt mixture behavior. A plot of SENB failure energy versus SCB failure energy is presented in Fig. 22, which indicates that the failure energy from BBR-SENB testing appears to be a good potential of the asphalt mixture performance [185]. Bahia et al. also emphasized the complexity of the viscoelastic behavior in terms of time dependency. Later, Tabatabaee and Bahia adopted a pseudo-strain analysis methodology to analytically separate time-dependent and time-independent energy mechanisms [186]. Such a separation was found unnecessary to relate binder failure energy to mixture fracture.

## 3.4.3. Use of DSR 4-mm parallel plates

Field studies on asphalt pavements become more and more important, which monitor the change of pavement performance over time and thereafter provide guidance to determine the right time for preventive maintenance. A test method that uses a small amount of material and produces data quickly is usually expected in such field studies [163]. In this sense, the BBR test is not a good option to monitor the low temperature performance of asphalt pavements because it requires a large amount of asphalt binders $( \mathbf { i } . \mathbf { e } . ,$ approximately 15 g per beam) and takes a long time (i.e., approximately 3 h) to prepare specimens and conduct testing [163,187]. Pavement researchers has been seeking a way to investigate the low temperature properties of asphalt binders with the use of a DSR. However, this approach was considered infeasible in the past many years because of the requirement for a large torque that was beyond the capability of the traditional DSR [158]. In addition to the torque limitation, machine compliance is another practical issue that needs to be addressed when a DSR is used for low temperature testing. According to Rowe et $\mathsf { a l . , }$ when the asphalt binder stiffness starts to exceed 10 to 20 MPa at low temperatures, compliance issues can occur with the DSR [188]. As a result, the deformation within the loading frame is included in the recorded measurements because the asphalt binder stiffness approaches the loading frame stiffness, which leads to unreliable measurements [163].

![](images/page_23_chart_5.jpg)

Fig. 22. Correlation between SENB fracture energy and SCB fracture energy [185].

As the DSR has been evolving in recent years, the current DSR can provide higher torque capacities [158]. Sui et al. from the Western Research Institute proposed using the 4-mm parallel plates to characterize the low temperature performance of asphalt binders with machine compliance corrections applied [163]. This method required an asphalt binder sample with a mass of only 25 mg. In their study, the 4-mm parallel plates were machined from the 8-mm parallel plates and had a full testing temperature range $\mathrm{from}-40\mathrm{to}60^{\circ}\mathrm{C}[163]$ . The frequency sweep test was conducted on selected asphalt binders using both the 4- mm and the 8-mm parallel plates in different temperature ranges. Both manual and automatic machine compliance correction methods were utilized to analyze the test data. The manual machine compliance correction method was developed based on the work of Schroter ¨ et al. [189] and Hutcheson and McKenna [190]. Several findings were reported in their study [163]: (1) the machine compliance was non-negligible at low temperature or high frequency conditions where the material became stiff, which resulted in significant corrections; (2) the two different machine compliance correction methods yielded consistent dynamic shear modulus data; (3) after machine compliance corrections, the data collected by parallel plates of different sizes were consistent with each other with good repeatability; and (4) the dynamic shear modulus data converted from the BBR test overlapped with those measured by the 4-mm parallel plates. Therefore, Sui et al. concluded that with the correction of machine compliance, the use of the 4-mm parallel plates could be a reliable technique to measure the low temperature properties of asphalt binders and had the potential to replace the BBR test [163].

## 3.4.4. Indirect measurement of ductility: Glover-Rowe parameter

Field data have shown that the ductility of asphalt binders measured at an appropriate temperature could correlate well with pavement cracking $[164 - 167]$ . Doyle reported that although the ductility at $2 5 \; ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C }$ was a poor indicator of pavement cracking, the ductility measured at $^ \mathrm { 1 2 . 8 ~ ^ { \circ } C }$ and at an elongation rate of 1 cm/min or less correlated quite well with pavement cracking [164]. Kandhal and his colleagues investigated the pavement performance of four test sections laid during 1960–1961 and found that only the ductility at $1 5 . 6 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ gave a proper ranking in road conditions after 10 years when compared with the penetration at $2 5   ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C }$ and the viscosity at $60^{\circ}C\left[165-167\right]$ . A relationship was established between the ductility at $^ \textrm { \scriptsize 1 5 . 6 } ^ { \scriptsize \textrm { \scriptsize 0 } } \mathrm { C }$ and the pavement condition. Specifically, a good pavement condition could be indicated by a ductility value larger than 10 cm, and cracking began to develop when the ductility value reduced to about 3 to 5 cm $[ 1 6 5  –  1 6 7 ]$ . These studies showed that the ductility measured at $1 5   ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C }$ and 1 cm/min could be a good indicator of durability. A caveat does exist, however, because these studies have generally used only unmodified asphalt binders, and so the correlation between cracking and polymer modified asphalt binders is less clear. As ductility measurement is a time and material consuming process, some researchers proposed to indirectly measure the ductility of asphalt binders using a DSR with an intent to derive a performance indicator which could accurately predict the thermal cracking in asphalt pavements [168,200].

A good example is the work conducted by Ruan et al. and Glover et al., who developed an improved method to screen asphalt binders for long-term pavement performance [168,200]. Based on a viscoelastic Maxwell element, a new DSR function, $G ^ { \prime } / ( \eta ^ { \prime } / G ^ { \prime } ) ,$ was derived as a surrogate for ductility, where $\eta ^ { \prime }$ is the storage viscosity. Detailed derivation of $G ^ { \prime } / ( \eta ^ { \prime } / G ^ { \prime } )$ can be found in the literature [168]. Compared with ductility, this parameter was easier to measure and required less amount of material for testing. According to their study, for seventeen unmodified asphalt binders aged at different conditions, the $G ^ { \prime } / ( \eta ^ { \prime } / G ^ { \prime } )$ values measured at $1 5   ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C }$ and 0.005 rad/s correlated remarkably well with the ductility values measured at $1 5   ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C }$ and 1 cm/min. Particularly, when the ductility was below 10 cm, there was a linear region in the log–log plot of ductility versus $G ^ { \prime } / ( \eta ^ { \prime } / G ^ { \prime } )$ . The correlation equation in the linear region is presented in $\mathbf { E } \mathbf { q } .$ (70) [168]. For modified asphalt binders, Glover et al. reported that there was no universal correlation between ductility and $G ^ { \prime } / ( \eta ^ { \prime } / G ^ { \prime } )$ , even in the low ductility region; however, these two parameters correlated well with each other for different asphalt binders within the same group [200].

$$

$$

As the slow loading rate $( \mathbf { i . e . } ,$ 0.005 rad/s) made testing very difficult, Glover et al. further shifted the test condition from $1 5   ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C }$ and 0.005 rad/s to $4 4 . 7 \; ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ and 10 rad/s in line with the time–temperature superposition principle [200]. In their study, the frequency sweep test was performed on three unmodified asphalt binders at $5 , 1 5 ,$ and $2 5   ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C }$ from

## 3.4.5. Indirect measurement of ductility: $\varDelta T _ { c }$

Delta $T _ { c }$ (ΔT<sub>c</sub>) was firstly proposed by Anderson et al. in a research project supported by the Airfield Asphalt Pavement Technology Program (AAPTP), i.e., Project 06–01, with an attempt to identify a laboratory technique as a potential means to determine an optimum time for preventive maintenance treatments [169]. It represents the difference between two BBR critical temperatures, namely one obtained from stiffness measurements and the other one obtained from m-value measurements. Mathematically, these two critical temperatures can be calculated by Eqs. (77) and (78), respectively [169].

$$
T_{c,S} = T_{1,S} + \left[ \frac{(T_{1,S} - T_{2,S}) \cdot (log300 - logS_1)}{logS_1 - logS_2} \right] - 10\tag{77}
$$

$$
T_{c,m} = T_{1,m} + \left[ \frac{(T_{1,m} - T_{2,m}) \cdot (0.3 - m_1)}{m_1 - m_2} \right] - 10\tag{78}
$$

where: $T _ { c ,   S }$ is the critical temperature determined based on stiffness; $T _ { 1 } ,$ $s$ is the temperature at which stiffness passes; $T _ { 2 ,   S }$ is the temperature at which stiffness fails; S<sub>1</sub> is the creep stiffness at $T _ { 1 } ; S _ { 2 }$ is the creep stiffness at $T _ { 2 } ; T _ { c ,   m }$ is the critical temperature determined based on m-value; $T _ { 1 ,   m }$ is the temperature at which m-value passes; $T _ { 2 , \; m }$ is the temperature at which m-value fails; $m _ { 1 }$ is the creep rate at $T _ { 1 } ;$ and $m _ { 2 }$ is the creep rate at $T _ { 2 } .$

The original definition of $\Delta T _ { c }$ by Anderson et al. was the difference between Eqs. (78) and $( 7 7 ) ,$ as presented in Eq. (79) [169]. However, $\operatorname { E q . }$ (80) is now more commonly used. The sign on $\Delta T _ { c }$ is an indicator of whether the low temperature grade of an asphalt binder is governed by the stiffness or creep rate, and the magnitude of $\Delta T _ { c }$ indicates the degree to which the asphalt binder is controlled by either its stiffness or creep rate [46].

$$
\Delta T _ { c } = T _ { c , m } - T _ { c , S }\tag{79}
$$

$$
\Delta T _ { c } = T _ { c , S } - T _ { c , m }\tag{80}
$$

According to Anderson et al., $, \mathbf { a }$ fairly good relationship $( \mathsf { R } ^ { 2 } = 0 . 7 7 4 )$ existed between $\Delta T _ { c }$ and the ductility measured at 15 ◦C and 1 cm/min for three unmodified asphalt binders that were PAV aged for 0, 20, 40, and 80 h [169]. With the increase of PAV aging time, the ductility decreased and $\Delta T _ { c }$ became more m-controlled, which indicated the loss of relaxation properties. Since $G ^ { \prime } / ( \eta ^ { \prime } / G ^ { \prime } )$ was reportedly to have a good correlation with ductility, an attempt was made by Anderson et al. to correlate $\Delta T _ { c }$ with $G ^ { \prime } / ( \eta ^ { \prime } / G ^ { \prime } )$ . Surprisingly, the relationship between $G ^ { \prime } / ( \eta ^ { \prime } / G ^ { \prime } )$ and $\Delta T _ { c }$ were themselves highly related. As shown in Fig. 25, the plot of log $G ^ { \prime } / ( \eta ^ { \prime } / G ^ { \prime } )$ versus $\Delta T _ { c }$ could be excellently fitted using a three-order polynomial with an $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 }$ of 0.987 [169]. In accordance with the criteria used in $G ^ { \prime } / ( \eta ^ { \prime } / G ^ { \prime } )$ for cracking identification, the cracking warning and cracking limit of $\Delta T _ { c }$ were then determined as 2.5 and $5   ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C } ,$ respectively [169]. When $\mathbf { E } \mathbf { q } .$ (80) is used, the cracking warning and cracking limit of $\Delta T _ { c }$ become − 2.5 and $- 5 \; ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C } ,$ respectively. In contrast with $G ^ { \prime } / ( \eta ^ { \prime } / G ^ { \prime } )$ , this parameter was viewed by Anderson et al. as being more useful because $G ^ { \prime } / ( \eta ^ { \prime } / G ^ { \prime } )$ should be measured at 15 $^ \circ \mathrm { C }$ whereas $\Delta T _ { c }$ is independent of climate [169]. It is important to note that the study conducted by Anderson et al. did not include any polymer modified asphalt binders; the relationships between the physical properties and aging of unmodified asphalt binders may not hold true for modified asphalt binders [169]. To validate the laboratory findings, Anderson et al. performed the BBR test on asphalt binders recovered from several field cores obtained from four airport pavements. It was found that newer pavements having $\Delta T _ { c }$ values that indicated less aging and more flexibility than older pavements, which generally matched previous laboratory studies [169].

The applicability of $\Delta T _ { c }$ was later investigated by several studies, which showed both positive and negative findings [207–209]. Rekine et al. reported that the $\Delta T _ { c }$ values of recovered asphalt binders subject to 40-h PAV aging showed strong correlation to the pavement distress at 5 years or longer on two test projects in Minnesota [207]. In their study, frequency sweep tests using the DSR 4-mm parallel plates were used to predict S(60) and m(60) at multiple temperatures following the method developed by the Western Research Institute, and $\Delta T _ { c }$ was then calculated based on predicted critical low temperatures grades. The study conducted by Hajj et al. showed a fair correlation between $\Delta T _ { c }$ and formation of cracking in the Overlay test [158]. They also noted that $\Delta T _ { c }$ might have large errors due in part to the compounding errors from different BBR measurements, each of which had an allowable error between specimens [158].

Willis and Turner evaluated the applicability of $\Delta T _ { c }$ to asphalt binders extracted from reclaimed asphalt shingles (RAS) based on both the BBR test and the use of DSR 4-mm parallel plates [208]. Two observations were made from their study: (1) regardless of the test method, both the S(60)-based critical temperature and the m(60)-based critical temperature had high variability, which resulted in variable $\Delta T _ { c }$ values; and (2) there was a lack of correlation between S(60)/m(60) measured from the BBR test and S(60)/m(60) predicted using the DSR approach [208]. McDaniel et al. investigated the possibility of implementing $\Delta T _ { c }$ in Indiana to control binder-related durability cracking issues. However, it was not recommended that Indiana Department of Transportation implement $\Delta T _ { c }$ in the specifications [209]. They found that polymer modified asphalt binders might exhibit $\Delta T _ { c }$ values that exceeded the cracking limit although they were typically less susceptible to cracking because of their elastomeric properties [209]. Another issue raised in their study is that determination of $\Delta T _ { c }$ of highly oxidized asphalt binders based on the BBR test could be very difficult [209]. The difficulty occurs because it is often not possible to determine $T _ { c ,   m }$ based on interpolation for highly oxidized asphalt binders; extrapolation may produce excessively high $T _ { c , \textit { m } }$ values, which eventually result in excessively low $\Delta T _ { c }$ values [210].

It can be concluded from preceding studies that further research is necessary to investigate the applicability of $\Delta T _ { c }$ to various types of modified asphalt binders. Currently, $\Delta T _ { c }$ has been included in AASHTO PP 78 to evaluate the embrittlement of blended asphalt binders [211]. According to AASHTO PP $^ { 7 8 , }$ the maximum allowable content of RAS binder in a blend is controlled by $\textbf { a }   \Delta T _ { c }$ value of $- 5 \mathrm { ~  ~ ^ { \circ } C . ~ }$ A more comprehensive review of $\Delta T _ { c }$ can be found in Asphalt Institute technical document IS–240 [46].

## 3.4.6. Summary and comparison of different test methods

Table 10 presents a brief list of all before-mentioned test methods. It can be summarized from Table 10 that there are three types of test methods developed in recent years to characterize the thermal cracking resistance of asphalt binders: (1) development of new test device or specimen geometry; (2) use of DSR 4-mm parallel plates; and (3) indirect measurement of ductility. The idea behind each type of test method differs from one to another. The first type features in testing the asphalt binder at its failure condition. The second type is targeted at prediction of the low temperature grade with acceptable accuracy. The third type aims to develop a surrogate quantifying the loss of relaxation properties for aging asphalt binders. Currently, there lacks a comprehensive comparison study that includes all three types of test methods. It is recommended that all of the test methods shown in Table 10 be performed on asphalt binders modified by different technologies (e.g., elastomer, plastomer, and crumb rubber). The test results can be used to identify the relationship among different test methods and to establish the correlation with the low temperature performance of asphalt mixtures. The optimum test method can then be screened out through comprehensive comparison of the correlation with mixture performance and the test efficiency.

## 4. Discussion on recent developments in Performance-based test methods

The previous section makes a review of the newly developed performance-based test methods. Each test method has an advantage over its counterpart in the Superpave PG system. Being advantageous to the test method in the Superpave PG system does not necessarily mean this method can be adopted as a specification test method. A lot of factors need to be taken into account for selecting a specification test method, such as efficiency, simplicity, and cost of test apparatus. However, potential specification tests can be selected from those performance-based test methods in order to refine the Superpave PG system.

It can be perceived from this literature review that there have been greater and greater pushes to get away from linear viscoelastic properties. For example, the MSCR test adopts a large stress level (i.e., 3.2 kPa) to characterize the rutting potential and evaluate the stress sensitivity of asphalt binders; the LAS test introduces significant amount of fatigue damage to the test material by imposing successively increasing strain levels. However, this is not universal as developments have also included adaptations of linear viscoelastic parameters, such as G-R parameter and $\Delta T _ { c } ,$ which are promising means to investigate the non-load related cracking. Generally, the linear viscoelastic theory is still widely used in the characterization of non-load related distresses (i.e., thermal cracking), while the importance of nonlinear behavior is highlighted in the characterization of load-related distresses (i.e., rutting and fatigue cracking).

One of the reasons that load-related distresses have moved towards nonlinear factors is the growth in the use of polymer modified asphalt binders, which have characteristically different relationships between linearity and nonlinearity in comparison to non-polymer modified systems. D’Angelo et al. found that the permanent deformation in polymer modified asphalt binders was highly stress dependent, and the stress dependency should be further employed as a possible mechanism to explain the rutting behavior of asphalt pavements [48]. The study conducted by Bahia et al. showed that polymer modified asphalt binders were highly sensitive to strain amplitude; the role of asphalt binder could be better defined with consideration of the strain dependency of different asphalt binders in the fatigue testing [31]. It has been demonstrated by many studies that the movements towards nonlinear considerations are effective in evaluating the rutting resistance and fatigue cracking resistance of asphalt binders [36,48]. Such an effectiveness can be reflected by a better correlation of the new performance indicator with the asphalt mixture performance than the counterpart used in the Superpave PG system. There is still room for future work on the stress dependency related to the rutting behavior. As noted previously, the two stress levels used in the MSCR test were not necessarily intended to represent the asphalt binder stress state in asphalt pavements. Use of a higher stress level may better represent the stress experienced in typical asphalt mixtures and better characterize the stress dependency of asphalt binders.

The stress/strain dependency may also play an important role in the low temperature characterization of polymer modified asphalt binders. The new adaptations of linear viscoelastic parameters were primarily developed for unmodified asphalt binders based on Kandhal’s study which arrived at critical ductility values at $1 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C } ,$ Specifically, Glover et al. reported that the ductility prediction using the Glover-Row parameter was found to be accurately only for unmodified asphalt binders [200]; the AAPTP study validated the accuracy of $\Delta T _ { c }$ using only three unmodified asphalt binders [169]. It is also important to note that Kandhal’s original study did not include any modified asphalt binders because modified asphalt binders were not common at that time [165–167]. Therefore, there is a need to verify the efficacy of using Glover-Rowe parameter and $\Delta T _ { c }$ to assess the non-load related distresses of modified asphalt binders, especially polymer modified asphalt binders.

To summarize, there are now two divergent philosophies about how we should go about grading and selecting asphalt binders. Scientifically answering this question cannot be currently accomplished. But if this is the fundamental question, then the pavement professionals should strive to answer it and work in a unified way to improve upon the technologies being currently used. It is encouraged that all people who do mixture tests should supplement their data with asphalt binder tests, and make sure to save and stockpile their asphalt binders for later studies that can reconcile these issues.

## 5. Conclusions

This paper provides a review of the Superpave PG system with respect to workability, rutting resistance, fatigue cracking resistance, and thermal cracking resistance. In addition, the performance-based test methods developed in recent years are also reviewed comprehensively. The following conclusions are obtained based on this study:

(1) The Superpave PG system made the first attempt to directly relate the measured physical properties of asphalt binders to field performance with due consideration of pavement conditions. Novel tests and specifications were devised to address the performance requirements.

(2) Currently, the MSCR test is employed to grade the asphalt binder with traffic condition taken into account. The use of |G\*|/sin δ is restricted to determine the high temperature grade of unaged asphalt binder.

(3) The ZSV method, the HSRV method, the SSF method, the phase method, and the rheogram-based test method were developed to determine the mixing and compaction temperatures of asphalt binders. However, there is no consensus reached on which method is the most effective.

(4) The RCR test, the Shenoy parameter, the ZSV, the LSV, and the MSCR test are test methods or rutting indicators developed in recent years to characterize the rutting resistance of asphalt binders. It is generally acknowledged that the MSCR test is the best among all existing test methods.

(5) The time sweep test, the BYET, and the LAS test were proposed to characterize the fatigue resistance of asphalt binders. Nowadays, the LAS test has been well accepted as an efficient accelerated fatigue test. Using the VECD theory for analysis, the LAS test is capable of predicting the fatigue life of an asphalt binder at any selected strain level.

(6) Three types of test methods were developed to investigate the thermal resistance of asphalt binders: (1) development of new test device or specimen geometry; (2) use of DSR 4-mm parallel plates; and (3) indirect measurement of ductility. The first type features in testing the asphalt binder at its failure condition; the second type aims to predict the low temperature grade with acceptable accuracy; and the third type is used as a surrogate quantifying the loss of relaxation properties as an asphalt binder ages.

In summary, the Superpave PG system represents a significant advancement over the traditional grading systems, and the newly developed performance-based test methods have the potential to be adopted as specification tests in order to further refine the Superpave PG system. Some potential future work related to the performance-based test methods presented in Section 3 is also summarized in this study and includes:

(1) The concentric cylinder system can be utilized in future studies as a standard geometry in designing new test methods for estimating the mixing and compaction temperatures of modified asphalt binders. More efforts should be devoted to investigating the practical shear rate during compaction.

(2) Refinement of the MSCR test protocol $( \mathbf { i } . \mathbf { e } . ,$ stress level, number of creep-recovery cycles, creep and/or recovery time) and analysis method (i.e., mechanistic modeling) is likely to improve the characterization of the rutting performance of highly modified asphalt binders.

(3) It is important to establish a criterion for the fatigue life predicted using the LAS test so that the LAS test can be integrated in the asphalt binder grading system.

(4) There are currently different test methods and analysis methods regarding using the DSR 4-mm parallel plate geometry to predict the low temperature grade of asphalt binders. It is imperative to develop standard test method and analysis method.

(5) Future work is essential to validate the applicability of the G-R parameter and $\Delta T _ { c }$ to various types of modified asphalt binders, especially polymer modified asphalt binders. The associated criteria for cracking warning and cracking limit should be evaluated accordingly.
